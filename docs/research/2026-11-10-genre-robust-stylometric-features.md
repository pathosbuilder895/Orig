# Count opportunities, not words, to survive genre shifts

| | |
|---|---|
| **Tool** | Claude Code with web search (four research subagents and a code audit) |
| **Date** | 2026-11-10 |
| **Question** | How can the consistency score stay reliable when a student changes genre or topic, by sorting habits into genre-forced and writer-chosen, and by measuring each against the right yardstick for text length? |
| **Decision it informs** | What to build after `OPPORTUNITY_MASK`; whether grammar-choice or explanatory-style measurements earn a place in the score. NORTH_STAR rules 2, 3 and 4. |

Several sources were read only at abstract or secondary level; the text marks which. Nothing here is validated on real student writing.

The system can be made more reliable across genre and topic changes, but only by doing three things it does not do today: stop scoring measurements that had no chance to occur, judge each habit against a tolerance band that widens for short texts and rare habits, and compare a student to classmates writing the same kind of assignment instead of to a fixed scale. Published research supports those three moves well. It does not support the more attractive idea that grammar choices between equivalent forms (such as "that" versus "which") or "explanatory style" (jargon versus example, story, analogy) are proven, portable fingerprints of a student: nobody has followed the same writers across genres and measured whether those habits hold, and at 300-600 words most of them occur too rarely to trust in a single paper. The code audit shows the current system has no length correction on any of its 109 measurements, treats "nothing to measure" as a real value on the new paper, and uses one tolerance floor for every measurement regardless of length; its own files show only 7 of 95 measured features reach even modest reliability at 500 words. The practical order of work is therefore: fix the measurement faults, build the per-feature reliability and genre-sensitivity tables that the literature does not supply, then change scoring in shadow mode (computed and logged, but not allowed to affect any result). Two limits apply throughout. Every external number here comes from published prose, emails, reviews or expert academics, and every internal number from public-domain authors and synthetic seminary papers; **nothing in this report is validated on real student writing**. And both goals pull against each other: most changes below reduce false flags on honest students, and each one must be checked to confirm it does not also hide a ghostwriter or an AI imitation.

## Most "style" measurements are forced by genre first and chosen by the writer second

Stylometry (measuring writing habits to compare texts) has a well-documented weak point: many of its favourite measurements are among the strongest markers of register, meaning the kind of writing a situation calls for. Biber's analysis of 67 features found that contractions, leaving out "that" ("I think [that] it works"), first and second person pronouns, present versus past tense, noun and preposition density, word length, vocabulary range and passives all move together when the type of text changes. Leaving out "that" and using contractions are the second and third strongest markers of informal, "involved" writing in his whole analysis ([summary of Biber 1988](https://www.uni-bamberg.de/fileadmin/eng-ling/fs/Chapter_21/23DimensionsofEnglish.html)). That source is a teaching summary, so the exact figures need checking against the book, but the direction is not in doubt. The consequence for this system is direct: a student who moves from a personal reflection to an exegetical paper will shift on all of these at once, for reasons that have nothing to do with who wrote the paper.

The evidence that a person has repeatable preferences among equivalent forms (linguists call these alternations: two ways of saying the same thing, such as "that" versus "which" or "picked up the book" versus "picked the book up") is real but thin, and all of it is inside one kind of writing. Eight academics given the same fill-in-the-gap test twice, two weeks apart, each made a distinct set of choices and were more like themselves than like each other, but that is a conference presentation with eight people and one task ([Nini, Cameron and Murphy 2021](https://zenodo.org/record/5227221)). Studies of press secretaries and of 176 Enron email writers found that individuals reuse distinctive short word sequences, again within a single setting ([Barlow 2013](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/ijcl.18.4.01bar); [Wright 2017](https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/id/eprint/31377)). The research notes found **no study that uses grammar alternations as an authorship feature set and reports accuracy, and no study that follows the same writers across genres to see whether their preferences hold**. That second gap is the most important missing piece for this project. The one careful warning that exists is discouraging: in a Greek newspaper study, one author wrote longer sentences than the other on culture and shorter ones on politics, so the "habit" reversed with topic ([Mikros and Argiri 2007](https://www.ceur-ws.org/Vol-276/paper5.pdf)). The defensible position is that a writer's stable trait, if there is one, is their offset from what classmates do in the same genre, not their raw rate, and whether that offset travels across genres has to be measured, not assumed.

What does survive a change of genre or topic, according to published tests? Three things recur. First, punctuation and very frequent function words (small grammatical words such as "of", "the", "but") hold up better than vocabulary: character sequences around punctuation were the worst features within one topic and among the best across topics ([Sapkota et al. 2015](https://aclanthology.org/N15-1010.pdf)). Second, hiding topic words helps when topic changes and costs a little when it does not: masking raised accuracy on a newspaper corpus from 66.2% to 75.1% across topics and from 39.4% to 58.9% across genres, for a loss of about 2.4 points within a topic ([Wang survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.00436), secondary report). Third, scoring against a reference population (asking "is this paper more like the claimed writer than like other writers of the same kind of text?") is the common factor in the methods that keep working. The strongest current example, LambdaG, masks content words and compares grammar patterns to about 100 reference samples; it reached accuracy of 0.71-0.96 across twelve collections, but its two weakest results were news and academic papers (0.71 and 0.78), the formal registers closest to student essays, and using a reference group from the wrong genre cost up to about 0.27 in accuracy ([Nini et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.08462)). Genre change is consistently harder than topic change: in a 21-author corpus built to cross both, holding out a topic gave 94% accuracy and holding out a genre 82% ([Wang survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.00436)), and in the 2023 shared evaluation essay-to-email pairs scored 0.888 while interview-to-email pairs scored 0.535, near chance ([Wang survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.00436)).

The system's own experiments point the same way, which is encouraging. On its leave-one-genre-out test of C.S. Lewis against a Chesterton comparison pool, the plain deviation score had an AUC of 0.387 (AUC is the chance that a genuine paper scores better than an impostor's; 0.5 is a coin flip, so 0.387 is worse than guessing), while the route that compares against a peer pool scored 0.863 (`validation/genre_crossgenre_2026-08/README.md`). That is the reference-population finding reproduced in-house. The same study ranked features by how little they move across one author's genres while still separating authors, with semicolon and colon rate, word reuse between sentences, adjective rate, dash rate and contraction rate at the top and theological vocabulary, claim markers, imperatives and function-word share at the bottom. Two cautions apply. That ranking rests on one author against one contrast author, and it disagrees with Biber on contractions, which he finds strongly register-driven. And the attempt to build a general per-feature topic-sensitivity table failed: it is recorded as a negative result because differences between editions of published books swamped topic differences (`validation/topic_sensitivity_2026-08/README.md`), which is why the `TOPIC_SENSITIVITY` table ships empty.

A published recipe for doing the sorting properly exists and transfers directly. For each feature, on a collection where the same writers appear in several genres, measure three things: how much it differs between writers, how much between genres, and how much the writer difference changes from genre to genre. Keeping only the 22 of 68 features that differed by author and not by topic or by the combination held author identification at 93% while topic identification fell from 79.5% to chance ([Mikros and Argiri 2007](https://www.ceur-ws.org/Vol-276/paper5.pdf)). That study had two authors, in Greek. No such ranking has been published for English across genres, so it has to be built locally, and the result should be a three-way sort, not two: habits forced by the assignment (compare only against classmates on the same assignment, or leave out), habits shown to be the writer's own (score them), and unproven habits (compute and display, do not score).

## Explanatory style: the choice of marker is promising, the rate of examples is too sparse to score

The owner's intuition that some writers explain through jargon and others through example, story or analogy has partial research support, with one usable idea inside it. The best evidence concerns the small fixed phrases that introduce an example or a rewording, which researchers call code glosses ("for example", "such as", "e.g.", "in other words", "i.e."). Six leading linguists, 15 texts each, differed 2.4-fold in how often they gave explicit examples (29.5 to 71.6 per 10,000 words) and each had a preferred phrase: one used "like", one preferred "for instance" over "e.g." by two to one, one used "sample" where everyone else wrote "example" ([Triki 2024](https://iris.unito.it/retrieve/d9ee6324-be59-44c3-bc24-d6bfcdf664b9/2024%20Exemplification%20and%20reformulation%20in%20expert%20linguists%e2%80%99%20writings-%20Elaborative%20metadiscourse%20between%20disciplinarity%20and%20individuality.pdf)). This separates two measurements. The **rate** of giving examples is driven as much by subject as by person: code glosses run at 167.5 per 100,000 words in literature articles against 423.4 in linguistics ([Guziurova 2022](https://topling.ukf.sk/index.php/topling/article/view/101)), as wide a spread as that between the six linguists. The **choice among interchangeable phrases** is a true choice between equivalents and does not depend on topic by construction, so it is the better candidate for a personal habit. The evidence for stability, though, is for senior academics writing about 9,700 words per text, whose rates still drifted over their careers; nothing shows the same for undergraduates.

The arithmetic for short papers is unforgiving. Applying the published rates to a 600-word essay gives roughly 1-3 explicit examples and 1-2 rewordings (this is a calculation from the notes, not a published count for student essays). Even the two most extreme linguists would differ by about 1.8 versus 4.3 examples in 600 words, which is within ordinary chance variation. A preference among "for example", "for instance", "e.g." and "such as" needs something like ten or more instances before it means anything, which is several essays pooled together. So the rate of examples, analogies and anecdotes should be shown to the teacher as plain counts with context ("2 explicit examples here; 1 to 4 in past papers") and never turned into a score for a single paper.

The other parts of explanatory style are weaker still. Word concreteness (how physical and picturable a word is) can be looked up in a free list of about 40,000 rated words ([Brysbaert et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-013-0403-5)), but it mostly reflects what the paper is about, and the notes found no study testing it as a marker of an individual. Academic vocabulary is dense enough to count (lists cover roughly 7-14% of words in academic prose, [Gardner and Davies](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_52.pdf)) but mixes terms the topic forces with a personal taste for formal wording; the proposed fix, counting only formal words that are not key terms of the assignment and comparing to classmates on the same prompt, is untested. Metaphor is common but mostly conventional and register-bound (18.5% of words in academic prose against 7.7% in conversation, [Steen et al. 2010](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/cogl.2010.024/pdf)); automatic detectors score about 0.72 on student essays on a 0-1 accuracy scale called F1 ([Leong et al. 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.figlang-1.3/)), so on one essay their errors are as large as the differences between writers. Detecting the implied "claim, then example" move without a marker phrase is poor (about 64% for the best models, [arXiv 2311.00451](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.00451)), and discourse-structure features gave no authorship benefit below 600-1,000 words ([Ferracane et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.02271)).

Where this family may earn its place is the second goal, catching AI. Compared with British university students, ChatGPT essays had fewer personal asides, fewer questions and fewer hedges and boosters ([Jiang and Hyland 2025](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/97952)), and instruction-tuned AI models use nominalisations (verbs turned into nouns, such as "implementation") at 1.5-2 times the human rate ([Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)). A plain, concrete writer who submits a noun-heavy, impersonal paper has moved in the AI direction. Two warnings attach. The same impersonal profile describes many second-language writers trained in formal English and writers in impersonal disciplines, so it is only safe as a change within one student, never as an absolute test. And imitation works best exactly where this system lives: when AI models were given five samples of a writer, a verification model accepted 95-97% of the imitations as genuine for news and email, against 17-21% for blogs ([Wang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.14543v1)). Student essays are formal and structured, so stylometry alone should not be expected to stop a prompted imitation. Nothing was found on whether human ghostwriters reproduce a client's explanatory habits.

## The right yardstick: shares for choices, rates for frequent habits, counts shown but not scored

Linguists who study choices between forms hold that a habit should be counted against the places where it could have occurred, not against total words. Dividing by words mixes "how often did the writer get the chance" with "what did the writer choose", and the number of chances is itself set by genre ([Szmrecsanyi](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-linguistics-revue-canadienne-de-linguistique/article/variationist-sociolinguistics-and-corpusbased-variationist-linguistics-overlap-and-crosspollination-potential/8AD81762C33EAA857B7D554CC448ABE7); [review of Wallis 2021](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/product/9664EC54674C48390B6004F3A55A0CA2/core-reader), both read at summary level). So a share of opportunities is, in theory, the more genre-proof yardstick for a true choice. **This has never been tested head to head in an authorship study**, and it has a cost: the denominator shrinks from hundreds of words to a handful of eligible places, so each share is noisier, and with zero opportunities it does not exist at all.

That yields a workable rule of three. A habit that is a choice between equivalents (which exemplifier, "that" or "which", "and" lists or comma lists) should be a share of opportunities, scored only above a minimum number of opportunities and otherwise marked "not measured". A frequent habit with no natural set of alternatives (commas, common function words, sentence length) should be a rate per word or, better, per sentence or clause, with a tolerance that depends on length. A rare event (an anecdote, an analogy, a block quotation) should be a raw count shown to a human. The size of the problem is easy to state: a habit that truly occurs 5 times per 1,000 words is expected 3 times in 600 words and will show up zero times in about 1 paper in 20 by chance alone; to pin a rate down to within about 20% takes roughly 25 expected occurrences, which at 600 words only the commonest function words, punctuation marks and broad word classes reach (the research notes' own arithmetic from standard formulas, not a published table).

Some measurements stay tied to length even after dividing by words. Vocabulary range measured as distinct words over total words falls as a text grows, and nearly all classic "vocabulary richness" measures drift the same way ([Tweedie and Baayen 1998](https://www.doi.org/10.1023/A:1001749303137), abstract only). The best-supported replacement is the moving-average type-token ratio (MATTR), which averages vocabulary range over a sliding window of fixed size, usually 50 or 100 words, so text length drops out; it was the most stable of nine measures across lengths in 4,542 student essays ([Kyle et al. 2024](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/evaluating-evidence-for-the-reliability-and-validity-of-lexical-diversity-indices-in-l2-oral-task-responses/E51955F0291E21A916CD2C4787508B80), reporting Zenker and Kyle 2021). By the same mechanism, share of once-used words and entropy measures (entropy is a number for how varied a set of patterns is) should be assumed length-dependent unless computed on fixed-size windows; the notes found no study testing that for entropy specifically.

On how short is too short, the literature places 300-2,500 words at the hard edge. Classic work wanted 5,000 words, later revised to "under 2,000 for texts with a clear signal", with the warning that some texts never become attributable and that this is unpredictable in advance ([Eder 2017](https://dh2017.adho.org/abstracts/341/341.pdf)). A forensic study that reports the strength of evidence found about 76% accuracy at 500 words and about 94% at 2,500 ([Ishihara 2017](https://journal.equinoxpub.com/IJSLL/article/view/5830), abstract level). The fair reading: a 300-600 word paper can supply weak evidence at best, and the system should narrow what it is willing to conclude as papers get shorter or fewer.

Two further findings bear on the scoring arithmetic. The system uses z-scores (how many typical swings a new value sits from the student's average). With only 3-5 past papers the "typical swing" is itself a poor estimate, and forensic statistics solves this by estimating ordinary within-person variation from the whole population and using the individual's papers only for their average ([Aitken and Lucy 2004](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/evaluation-of-trace-evidence-in-the-form-of-multivariate-data/)). And counts in short texts are lumpy and often zero, which z-scores assume away; a model built for counts beat a standard distance method at 700 words per document ([Carne and Ishihara 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.alta-1.4.pdf)). No published authorship system was found that scales its tolerance by document length or abstains below a minimum number of opportunities; published work sidesteps the issue by cutting all texts to one length. And **no published table gives the reliability of individual stylometric features at about 500 words**. Both have to be built here.

## The current system corrects nothing for length and scores placeholders as measurements

The audit of the code at commit `382be03` (reading only; the feature pipeline was not run) found the following. About a third of the 109 features are honest rates per word, sentence or paragraph (35), which are not biased by length but are noisy on short texts. Only 4 are clean two-form choices (`that_which_ratio`, `epistemic_certainty_ratio`, `abbreviation_tendency`, `polysyndeton_ratio`), and none has a minimum number of opportunities: a single occurrence returns exactly 0 or 1. Twelve are entropies and six are vocabulary-growth measures that are expected to move with length for the same writer, with no correction. There is no MATTR, no fixed-window sampling and no adjusted entropy anywhere in `original/features/`.

Several faults need no research to justify fixing. `discourse_marker_density` counts distinct marker types (at most 53) and divides by words, so it shrinks as a paper grows: 10 types in 500 words scores 2.0, 30 types in 5,000 words scores 0.6. The four marker-category ratios are shares of distinct types, not of uses. `article_omission_rate` examines only the first 4,000 characters but divides by all words, biasing it downward beyond roughly 600-700 words. The "that" pattern in `that_which_ratio` also catches "said that the...", which is a different construction from the one "which" competes with. `passive_voice_ratio` is pattern matches capped at the sentence count, not the share of sentences that are passive.

The most serious fault is the handling of "nothing to measure". When a paper offers no opportunities, features return 0.5 or 0.0 (the convention is inconsistent, and 0.5 is also a legitimate "balanced" value). A feature is dropped for a student only if every past paper is stuck. If past papers have real values and the new paper has the placeholder, the placeholder is scored as a genuine difference (`original/quantum/scoring.py:1161-1162`). A student who used "which" in every past paper and writes a short piece with no relative clauses is marked as having changed. That is a direct source of false flags on honest students, and it worsens with genre change because a new genre changes which opportunities exist.

The tolerance band has the same blind spot. The floor on each feature's expected swing is `max(0.005, 0.15 / sqrt(N))`, where N is the number of past papers: identical for every feature and unaware of how long any paper is (`original/quantum/state.py:280-305`). Nothing handles a length mismatch between past papers and the new one, so 2,000-word past papers against a 400-word submission shifts every length-dependent feature and that shift is scored as deviation. The optional `LENGTH_ADAPTIVE_WEIGHTS` flag adjusts by group of features, not by feature, and looks only at the new paper's length; its "holds up when short" verdict for one group is driven by a single feature whose figure is an artefact. The scoring route has no minimum word count; under 300 words it adds a note that does not change the recommended action.

The system's own reliability file shows the cost. Reliability here means ICC (intraclass correlation): the share of a feature's variation that comes from differences between writers as opposed to noise within one writer, from 0 to 1. At 500 words, **only 7 of 95 measured features reach 0.30, 53 are below 0.10, and all four clean choice features are weak** (0.03 to 0.19) (`validation/short_regime/reliability_500w.json`). The file warns that low values swing between runs, and its corpus is nine pseudo-students, four of them public-domain authors. The choice features do separate authors once there are enough opportunities: `that_which_ratio` improves roughly tenfold between 500-word and 5,000-word windows (`validation/stability/2026-08-01/length_stability.csv`). That matches the literature: the idea is sound, the sample is too small. One caution on reading the table: the top feature at 500 words is pronoun density, which Biber identifies as a register marker, so part of its apparent reliability could be the test authors writing in different genres. That is an inference from the notes, not a measured result. The calibration record adds that even with four 1,300-word past papers of well-edited prose, the margin between same-author and other-author papers stayed negative until samples reached about 6,000 words (`docs/calibration/short_regime_thresholds_2026-07-29.md`).

## An ordered plan: repair measurement, then measure, then change scoring in shadow

The order matters because each stage supplies what the next needs, and because the repository's own rules require score-changing work to ship off by default and run in shadow first. Any change to the measurement scale bounds or the feature ordering in `original/constants.py` needs the owner's explicit permission. The thresholds below are starting proposals from the research notes' arithmetic, not published values.

| Order | Work | Why now | Goal it serves, and its risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Give every feature an explicit "not measured" state with its opportunity count; exclude not-measured features from the comparison on both sides; make the zero-opportunity convention consistent. | Pure fault repair; everything later depends on knowing opportunity counts. | Fewer false flags. Risk: fewer features scored means less power to catch impostors; log how many features survive per paper. |
| 2 | Fix the false rates: `discourse_marker_density` and the four marker ratios to count uses; `article_omission_rate` denominator; align the two halves of `that_which_ratio`; make `passive_voice_ratio` a true share. | Known arithmetic errors that vary with length. | Fewer false flags. These change scores, so run old and new side by side first. |
| 3 | Build the missing reliability table: per-feature ICC across documents at 300, 600, 1,200 and 2,500 words with a fixed random seed, plus the share of papers at each length that clear a minimum opportunity count. | The literature has no such table; step 6 needs it. | Both. Risk: the available corpora are not students. |
| 4 | Measure length mismatch directly: same author, short text against long baselines, per feature. | The existing stability table compares equal-length windows and cannot see this. | Fewer false flags when a short assignment follows long ones. |
| 5 | Build the forced-versus-chosen table on a crossed collection (same writers, several genres), recording writer effect, genre effect and their interaction per feature. | The single most important gap; the earlier attempt failed on edition noise, so the corpus must be chosen to avoid that. | Both. No suitable student corpus exists in the repository; the pilot's own data, with consent, is the only real answer. |
| 6 | Replace length-drifting measures with fixed-window versions (MATTR for vocabulary range; windowed entropies), as new report-only values first. | Supported by the lexical diversity literature. | Fewer false flags; verify with step 4's test. |
| 7 | Change the tolerance band in shadow: take ordinary within-writer variation from the same-tenant peer pool, add a sampling term that grows as expected count falls, widen for few past papers, and skip any feature below about 10 opportunities or 5 expected occurrences. | Needs steps 1, 3 and 5. | Fewer false flags. Risk: wider bands hide impostors; measure catch rate at a fixed false-flag rate before and after. |
| 8 | Add explanatory-style evidence as display only: exemplifier and reformulator choice pooled across all past papers, counts of examples, questions and personal asides, formal wording outside assignment key terms. | Cheap, explainable, and aligned with known AI tendencies. | Mainly AI detection, as within-student change only. Risk: unfair to second-language writers if read as absolute. |
| 9 | Test against deliberate imitation: AI text prompted with the student's own past papers, and the cross-genre gate (G7), before anything leaves shadow. | Formal-genre imitation fooled a verifier 95% of the time in published work. | Catching ghostwriters and AI. |

Three things are deliberately left out. Full metaphor detection, discourse parsing and narrative classifiers are not worth building for single short essays on the evidence above. A scored "examples per paper" feature should not be built at all at these lengths. And none of the alternation features should gain weight on linguistic argument alone; they earn a place only by passing step 5.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. It began as "which features are the writer's own?", but the literature and the system's own cross-genre result agree that no feature is genre-proof in itself: what is stable, where anything is, is a writer's position relative to peers doing the same task, and what makes a measurement trustworthy is the number of chances it had to occur. That reframing favours the peer-comparison route the system already has over further tuning of the raw deviation score, and it turns "explanatory style" from a proposed score into a small set of counts a teacher can read and question.

It also sets an honest ceiling. With three to five past papers of a few hundred words in a formal register, published methods give weak to moderate evidence, formal prose compresses the differences between writers, and a prompted AI imitation of formal text passes verification most of the time. The changes above should make the system wrong less often about honest students who change genre; they do not make stylometry sufficient against a careful ghostwriter or AI, and none of it is known to hold for real students until the pilot's shadow data says so.
