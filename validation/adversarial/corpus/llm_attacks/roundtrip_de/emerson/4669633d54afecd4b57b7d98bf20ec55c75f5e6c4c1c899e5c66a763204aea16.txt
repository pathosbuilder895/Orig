And I have known gentlemen of great weight in the community, whose sympathies were nonetheless cold—presidents of colleges, and governors, and senators—who felt bound to sign every temperance pledge, to make common cause with Bible societies and missions and peacemakers, and to cry _Huzza, boy!_ to every good dog. We must not carry our complaisance too far, but we all have kindly impulses in this direction. When the boys come into my yard and ask leave to gather horse chestnuts, I confess that I enter into nature's game, and pretend to grant the permission reluctantly, for fear that at any moment they might discover the cheat of that gaudy chaff. But this tenderness is quite unnecessary; the enchantments are laid on very thick. Their young life is thatched with them. Bare and grim to tears is the lot of the children in the hovel I saw yesterday; and yet they hung it with no less tinsel of romance than the children of the happiest fortune, and talked of “the dear cottage where so many joyful hours had flown.” Well, this thatching of hovels is the custom of the country. Women, more than all others, are the element and kingdom of illusion. Enchanted, they enchant. They see through Claude Lorrain glasses. And how could anyone dare, if he could, to tear away the _coulisses_, the stage effects and ceremonies by which they live? Too touching, too pitiable is the realm of affection, and its atmosphere is ever exposed to the _Fata Morgana_. We are not much to blame for our bad marriages. We live amid hallucinations; and this particular snare is set to entangle our feet, and all of us fall into it sooner or later. But the mighty Mother, who had been so sly with us, as if she felt that she owed us some compensation, slips into the Pandora's box called marriage some deep and serious benefits and some great joys. We find a delight in the beauty and happiness of children that makes the heart too big for the body. In the worst-matched unions there is always some admixture of true marriage. Teague and his wench arrive at some right relations of mutual respect, kindly observation, and mutual care, learn something, and would behave more wisely if they were only beginning now. It is easy for us to talk when we point to this or that fine madman, as if there were any exceptions. The scholar in his library is none. I, who have all my life heard any number of speeches and debates, read poems and miscellaneous books, and conversed with many geniuses, am still the victim of every new page; and if Marmaduke, or Hugh, or Moosehead, or anyone else invents a new style or a new mythology, I fancy that the world will be quite splendid and right once it is dressed in these colors, which I had not thought of. Then I want to daub with this new paint at once; but it will not stick. It is like the cement the peddler sells at the door; he makes broken crockery hold with it, but you can never buy of him a bit of the cement that will make it hold once he is gone. Men who make themselves felt in the world avail themselves of a certain fate in their constitution, which they know how to use. But they never interest us deeply unless they lift a corner of the curtain, or betray, in the least degree, that they see what lies behind it. It is the charm of practical men that outside their practice there lie a certain poetry and play, as if they led the good horse Power by the bridle and preferred to walk, though they can ride so fiercely. Bonaparte is a man of intellect, and so is Caesar; and the best soldiers, sea captains, and railroad men have a gentleness when off duty; a good-natured admission that there are illusions, and who shall say that he is not their plaything? We brand the cast-iron fellows who cannot so detach themselves as “dragon-ridden,” “thunder-struck,” and fools of fate, with whatever powers they may be endowed. Since our instruction comes through emblems and indirections, it is well to know that there is method in it, a fixed scale and rank above rank in the phantasms. We begin low with coarse masks and rise to the finest and fairest. The red men told Columbus “they had an herb which took away fatigue”; but he found the illusion of “arriving at India from the east” more soothing to his lofty spirit than any tobacco. Is not our faith in the impenetrability of matter more soothing than narcotics? You play with jackstraws, balls, bowls, horse and gun, estates and politics; but there are finer games before you. Is not time a pretty toy? Life will show you masks that are worth all your carnivals. Yonder mountain must migrate into your mind. The fine stardust and the nebulous veil in Orion, “the ominous year of Mizar and Alcor,” must come down and be dealt with in your household thoughts. What if you should come to see that the play and the playground of all this pompous history are radiations from yourself, and that the sun borrows its beams? What terrible questions we are learning to ask! Former men believed in magic, by which temples, cities, and men were swallowed up, and every trace of them vanished. We are coming upon the secret of a magic which sweeps out of men's minds every vestige of the theism and the beliefs which they and their fathers held and were formed upon. There are deceptions of the senses, deceptions of the passions, and the structural, beneficent illusions of sentiment and of the intellect. There is the illusion of love, which attributes to the beloved person all that this person shares with his or her family, sex, age, or condition, nay, with the human mind itself. It is these that the lover loves, and Anna Matilda gets the credit for it. As if one who was always shut up in a tower with a single window, through which the face of heaven and earth could be seen, should fancy that all the marvels he beheld belonged to that window. There is the illusion of time, which is very deep; who has done with it? or come to the conviction that what seems the succession of thought is only the distribution of wholes into causal series? The intellect sees that every atom carries the whole of nature within it; that the mind opens to omnipotence; that, in the endless striving and ascent, the metamorphosis is complete, so that the soul does not know itself in its own act when that act is accomplished. There is an illusion that shall deceive even the elect. There is an illusion that shall deceive even him who works the miracle. Though he makes his body, he denies that he makes it. Though the world is made of thought, thought is cowed in the presence of the world. One after another we accept the mental laws, and still resist those that follow, which must nevertheless be accepted. But all our concessions only compel us to new prodigality. And what avails it that science has come to treat space and time as simply forms of thought, and the material world as hypothetical, and that at the same time our claim to property, and even to selfhood, fades with the rest, if at last even our thoughts are not finalities, but the incessant flowing and ascension reaches them too, and every thought which yesterday was a finality yields today to a larger generalization? With such volatile elements to work in, it is no wonder if our estimates are loose and wavering. We must work and affirm, but we have no idea of the value of what we say or do. The cloud is now as big as your hand, and now it covers a county. That story of Thor, who was set in Asgard to empty the drinking horn, to wrestle with the old woman, and to race with the runner Lok, and who presently found that he had been drinking up the sea, wrestling with time, and racing with thought, describes us, who amid these seeming trifles are wrestling with the highest powers of nature. We fancy that we have fallen into bad company and squalid conditions, paying petty debts, shoe bills, broken glass, buying pots, butcher's meat, sugar, milk, and coal. “Set me some great task, ye gods! and I will show my spirit.” “Not so,” says the good Heaven; “plod and plough, patch your old coats and hats, weave a shoestring; great affairs and the best wine by and by.” Well, it is all phantasm; and if we weave a yard of tape in all humility and as well as we can, long hereafter we shall see that it was no cotton tape at all, but some Milky Way we were braiding, and that the threads were time and nature. We cannot write down the order of the variable winds. How can we penetrate the law of our shifting moods and susceptibility? And yet they differ as all and nothing. Instead of yesterday's firmament, which our eyes demand, today it is an eggshell that coops us in; we cannot even see what or where our stars of destiny are. From day to day the principal facts of human life are hidden from our eyes. Suddenly the mist rolls up and reveals them, and we think how much good time is gone that might have been saved had any hint of these things been given. A sudden rise in the road shows us the system of mountains and all the peaks, which have been just as near us all year, but quite out of mind. But these alternations are not without their order, and we have our share in our changing fortune. If life seems a succession of dreams, yet poetic justice is done in dreams also. The visions of good men are good; it is the undisciplined will that is whipped with bad thoughts and bad fortunes. When we break the laws, we lose our hold on the central reality. Like sick men in hospitals, we only change from bed to bed, from one folly to another; and it cannot signify much what becomes of such castaways—wailing, stupid, comatose creatures—lifted from bed to bed, from the nothing of life to the nothing of death. In this kingdom of illusions we grope eagerly for stays and foundations. There are none, save a strict and faithful dealing at home and a severe barring out of all duplicity or illusion there. Whatever games are played with us, we must play no games with ourselves, but deal in our privacy with the last honesty and truth. I look upon the simple and childish virtues of veracity and honesty as the root of all that is sublime in character. Speak as you think, be what you are, pay your debts of all kinds. I prefer to be owned as sound and solvent, and my word as good as my bond, and to be what cannot be skipped, or dissipated, or undermined, to all the _éclat_ in the universe. This reality is the foundation of friendship, religion, poetry, and art. At the top or at the bottom of all illusions I set the cheat which still leads us to work and live for appearances, in spite of our conviction, in all sane hours, that it is what we really are that avails with friends, with strangers, and with fate or fortune. One would think from the talk of men that riches and poverty were a great matter; and our civilization is chiefly ordered by it. But the Indians say they do not believe that the white man, with his care-worn brow, always toiling, afraid of heat and cold, and keeping indoors, has any advantage over them. The permanent interest of every man is never to be in a false position, but to have the weight of nature behind him in all that he does. Riches and poverty are a thick or thin garment; and our life—the life of all of us—is the same. For we continually rise above circumstances and taste the real quality of existence; as in our employments, which differ only in their manipulations but express the same laws; or in our thoughts, which wear no silks and taste no ice cream. We see God face to face every hour, and know the savor of nature. The early Greek philosophers Heraclitus and Xenophanes measured their strength on this problem of identity. Diogenes of Apollonia said that unless the atoms were made of one stuff, they could never mix and act upon one another. But the Hindus, in their sacred writings, express the liveliest feeling both of the essential identity and of that illusion which they conceive variety to be: “The notions ‘_I am_’ and ‘_This is mine_,’ which govern mankind, are but delusions of the mother of the world. Dispel, O Lord of all creatures! the conceit of knowledge which proceeds from ignorance.” And in their view the beatitude of man lies in being freed from enchantment. The intellect is stimulated by the statement of truth in a figure, and the will by the clothing of the laws of life in illusions. But the unities of truth and of right are not broken by the disguise. There need never be any confusion in these. In a crowded life of many parts and performers, on a stage of nations, or in the remotest hamlet in Maine or California, the same elements offer the same choices to every newcomer, and, according to his choice, he fixes his fortune in absolute nature. It would be hard to put more mental and moral philosophy into one sentence than the Persians have put into this:— “Deceived you must be, though you were the wisest of the wise: Then be the fool of virtue, not of vice.” There is no chance and no anarchy in the universe. All is system and gradation. Every god sits there in his sphere. The young mortal enters the hall of the firmament: there he is alone with them alone, and they shower him with blessings and gifts, and beckon him up to their thrones. At the same moment, and without ceasing, snowstorms of illusions fall. He fancies himself in a vast crowd which sways this way and that, and whose movement and doings he must obey: he fancies himself poor, orphaned, insignificant. The mad crowd drives hither and thither, and furiously commands him now to do this, now that. Who is he, that he should resist their will, and think or act for himself? Every moment new changes, and new showers of deceptions, to baffle and distract him. And when, by and by, for a moment the air clears and the cloud lifts a little, there are the gods still sitting around him on their thrones—they alone with him alone. THE END.
