In Europe, where the feudal forms secure the continuance of wealth in certain families, those families buy and keep such things and open them to the public. But in America, where democratic institutions break every fortune up into small parts, the public should, after a few years, step into the place of these owners and supply the citizen with this culture and stimulus. Man was born to be rich, or he inevitably grows rich through the use of his faculties; through the union of thought with nature. Property is a product of the mind. The game demands of its players coolness, right reasoning, quickness, and patience. Cultivated labor drives out crude labor. An infinite number of shrewd men, in an infinite number of years, have arrived at certain best and shortest ways of doing things, and this accumulated skill in arts, methods of cultivation, harvests, preserving, manufactures, navigation, and barter makes up the value of our present world. Trade is a game of skill which not everyone can play, and which only a few can play well. The true merchant is one who has that right average measure of faculties which we call _common sense_; a man with a strong bent toward facts, who grounds his decision on what he has seen. He is thoroughly persuaded of the truths of arithmetic. There is always a reason, _in the man himself_, for his good or bad fortune, and so too in the making of money. People talk as if there were some magic in it, and believe in magic in every department of life. He knows that everything goes the old way, pound for pound, cent for cent,—for every effect a perfect cause,—and that luck is only another name for steadiness of purpose. He insures himself in every transaction, and loves small and sure gains. Honesty and closeness to the facts are the foundation, but the masters of the art add a certain long-range arithmetic. The task is to bring to many and distant enterprises that exactness and fidelity to the facts which are easy in near and small dealings; and so to arrive at enormous results without any loss of safety. Napoleon liked to tell the story of the banker of Marseilles who said to his visitor, astonished at the contrast between the splendor of the banker's chateau and hospitality and the meanness of the counting-house in which he had seen him:—"Young man, you are too young to understand how masses are formed,—the true and only power,—whether they are made of money, of water, or of men, it is all the same,—a mass is an immense center of motion, but it must be begun, it must be kept up:"—and he might have added that the way in which it must be begun and kept up lies in obedience to the law of particles. Success consists in close adherence to the laws of the world, and since those laws are intellectual and moral, in an intellectual and moral obedience. Political Economy is as good a book in which to read the life of man, and the supremacy of laws over all private and hostile influences, as any Bible that has come down to us. Money is representative, and follows the nature and fortunes of its owner. The coin is a delicate measuring instrument of civil, social, and moral changes. The farmer is sparing of his dollar, and with good reason. It did not fly to him. He knows how many strokes of labor it stands for. His bones ache with the day's work that earned it. He knows how much land it stands for;—how much rain, frost, and sunshine. He knows that in the dollar he gives you so much discretion and patience, so much hoeing and threshing. Try to lift his dollar; you must lift all that weight. In the city, where money follows the quick stroke of a pen or a lucky rise in exchange, it soon comes to be thought light. I wish the farmer held it dearer, and would spend it only for real bread; force for force. The farmer's dollar is heavy, and the clerk's is light and nimble; leaps out of his pocket; jumps onto cards and faro tables: but still more curious is its sensitiveness to metaphysical changes. It is the finest barometer of social storms, and foretells revolutions. Every step of civil progress makes every man's dollar worth more. In California, the country where it grew,—what would it buy there? A few years ago it bought a shanty, dysentery, hunger, bad company, and crime. There are wide countries, like Siberia, where today it would buy little else than some small relief from suffering. In Rome it buys beauty and magnificence. Forty years ago a dollar did not buy much in Boston. Now it buys a great deal more in our old town, thanks to railroads, telegraphs, steamers, and the simultaneous growth of New York and of the whole country. Yet there are many goods belonging to a capital which are not yet to be bought here, no, not with a mountain of dollars. A dollar in Florida is not worth as much as a dollar in Massachusetts. A dollar is not value, but the representative of value, and, at last, of moral values. A dollar is reckoned by the grain it can buy, or, to speak strictly, not by the grain or the house-room, but by Athenian grain and Roman house-room,—by the wit, the honesty, and the strength for whose sake we eat bread and live in houses, that we may share in them and exercise them. Wealth is mental; wealth is moral. The value of a dollar is to buy just things: a dollar goes on rising in value with all the genius and all the virtue of the world. A dollar in a university is worth more than a dollar in a jail; in a temperate, educated, law-abiding community, more than in some sink of crime where dice, knives, and arsenic are always in play. The "Bank-Note Detector" is a useful publication. But the circulating dollar, whether silver or paper, is itself the detector of right and wrong wherever it circulates. Does it not gain value at once with every increase of rectitude? If a trader refuses to sell his vote, or holds fast to some unpopular right, he makes so much more rectitude in Massachusetts; and every acre in the State is worth more in the hour of his deed. If you take out of State Street the ten most honest merchants, and put in ten roguish persons controlling the same amount of capital,—the rates of insurance will announce it; the soundness of the banks will show it: the highways will be less safe: the schools will feel it; the children will bring home their little dose of the poison: the judge will sit less firmly on the bench, and his decisions will be less upright; he has lost so much support and constraint,—which all need; and the pulpit will betray it, in a laxer rule of life. An apple tree, if you take away a cartload of loam from it every day for several days and heap a cartload of sand about its roots,—will notice it. An apple tree is a stupid kind of creature, but if this treatment were kept up for a short time, I think it would begin to suspect something. And if you took out of the powerful class engaged in trade a hundred good men and put in a hundred bad ones, or, what is exactly the same thing, introduced a demoralizing institution, would not the dollar, which is not much more stupid than an apple tree, soon notice it? The value of a dollar is social, since it is created by society. Every man who moves into this town carrying in him some salable talent or skill gives a new value to every man's labor in the town. When a talent is born into the world anywhere, the community of nations is enriched; and how much more by a new degree of honesty. The cost of crime, one of the chief burdens of every nation, is to that extent saved. In Europe it is observed that crime rises and falls with the price of bread. If the Rothschilds in Paris refuse to accept bills, the people in Manchester, in Paisley, in Birmingham are driven onto the highway, and landlords are shot down in Ireland. The police records bear witness to it. The shocks are soon felt in New York, New Orleans, and Chicago. In much the same way, economic power reaches the masses by way of their political masters. Rothschild refuses the Russian loan, and there is peace, and the harvests are saved. He grants it, and there is war, and an agitation running through a great part of mankind, with every hideous consequence, ending in revolution and a new order. Wealth brings its own checks and counterweights with it. The basis of Political Economy is non-interference. The only safe rule is found in the self-adjusting meter of demand and supply. Make no laws. Meddle, and you sever the sinews with your sumptuary laws. Give no bounties: make equal laws: secure life and property, and you need give no alms. Open the doors of opportunity to talent and virtue, and they will do themselves justice, and property will not be in bad hands. In a free and just commonwealth, property flows from the idle and incapable to the industrious, the brave, and the persevering. The laws of nature play through trade as a toy battery displays the effects of electricity. The level of the sea is not more surely kept than is the equilibrium of value in society by demand and supply: and artifice or legislation punishes itself by reactions, gluts, and bankruptcies. The sublime laws play indifferently through atoms and galaxies. Whoever knows what happens in the getting and spending of a loaf of bread and a pint of beer; that no wishing will alter the rigid limits of pints and penny loaves; that for everything consumed there is so much less left in the basket and the jug; but that what has gone out of them is not wasted, but well spent, if it nourishes his body and enables him to finish his task;—he knows all of Political Economy that the budgets of empires can teach him. The charm of the small economy is this way it has of symbolizing the great economy; the way a household and a private man's methods tally with the solar system and with the laws of giving and taking throughout nature; and however wary we may be of the falsehoods and petty tricks we suicidally play on one another, every man feels a certain satisfaction whenever his dealing touches the inescapable facts; when he sees that things themselves set the price, as they always tend to do, and as one sees them do in large manufactures. Your paper is not fine or coarse enough,—is too heavy or too thin. The manufacturer says he will supply you with exactly the thickness or thinness you want; the pattern is a matter of complete indifference to him; here is his price list;—every sort of paper, cheaper or dearer, with the prices set beside it. A pound of paper costs so much, and you may have it made in any pattern you like. There is in all our trade a self-regulation that makes haggling unnecessary. You want to rent a house, but it must be cheap. The owner can lower the rent, but in doing so he makes himself unable to make proper repairs, and the tenant gets not the house he would like but a worse one; not to mention that a somewhat injurious relation springs up between landlord and tenant. You dismiss your laborer, saying, "Patrick, I will send for you as soon as I can't do without you." Patrick goes off content, for he knows that the weeds will grow with the potatoes, that the vines must be set out next week, and that, however unwilling you may be, the muskmelons, crookneck squashes, and cucumbers will send for him. Who would not wish that all labor and all value stood on the same simple and blunt market? If it is the best of its kind, it will. We need carpenter, locksmith, planter, priest, poet, doctor, cook, weaver, groom; each in his turn through the year. If a St. Michael's pear sells for a shilling, it costs a shilling to raise it. If in Boston the best securities offer twelve _per cent_ for money, they have just six _per cent_ of insecurity. You may not see that the fine pear costs you a shilling, but it costs the community that much. The shilling represents the number of enemies the pear has, and the amount of risk in ripening it. The price of coal shows the narrowness of the coal field and a forced confinement of the miners to a certain district. All salaries are reckoned on contingent as well as on actual services. "If the wind were always south-west by west," said the skipper, "women might sail ships at sea." One might say that all things are of one price; that nothing is cheap or dear; and that the apparent inequalities that strike us are only a shop trick to hide the damage in your bargain. A youth coming into the city from his native farm in New Hampshire, with the plain fare still fresh in his memory, lodges at a first-class hotel and believes he must somehow have outwitted Dr. Franklin and Malthus, for luxury is cheap. But he pays for the single convenience of a better dinner with the loss of some of the richest social and educational advantages. What safeguards he has lost! what spurs! He may find, little by little, that he left the Muses at the door of the hotel and found the Furies inside. Money often costs too much, and power and pleasure are not cheap. The old poet said, "The gods sell all things at a fair price." There is an instance of compensations in the commercial history of this country. When the European wars of 1800 to 1812 threw the carrying trade of the world into American keels, now and then an American ship was seized. The loss to the owner was of course serious, but the country was indemnified; for we charged three pence a pound for carrying cotton, six pence for tobacco, and so on; which paid for the risk and the loss, and brought the country an immense prosperity, early marriages, private wealth, the building of cities and of states: and after the war was over, we received by treaty an indemnity for all the seizures into the bargain. Well, the Americans grew rich and great. But the pay-day is coming. Great Britain, France, and Germany, impoverished by our extraordinary profits, drawn by the report of our advantages, send out first their thousands, then their millions, of poor people to share in the harvest. At first we employ them and add to our prosperity: but in the artificial system of society and of protected labor, which we too have adopted and extended, checks and stoppages soon come.
