He also said that the beavers were becoming quite numerous again where we were going, but that their skins now brought so little that it was not worth hunting them. I had hung the ears of our moose, which were ten inches long, over the fire to dry along with the moose meat, since I wanted to keep them; but Sabattis told me that I must skin and tan them, or else all the hair would fall out. He remarked that they made tobacco pouches out of the skin of the ears, putting the two together, inside to inside. I asked him how he made a fire; and he brought out a small cylindrical box of friction matches. He also had flint and steel and some tinder, which was not dry; I think it came from the yellow birch. "But suppose you capsize, and all this and your powder get wet." "Then," said he, "we wait until we get to where there is some fire." I took out of my pocket a little vial containing matches, stoppered water-tight, and told him that even if we capsized we would still have a few dry matches; at which he stared at me without saying a word. So we lay awake a long time talking, and they explained to us the meaning of many Indian names of lakes and rivers in the vicinity—especially Tahmunt. I asked the Indian name of Moosehead Lake. Joe answered _Sebamook_; Tahmunt pronounced it _Sebemook_. When I asked what it meant, they answered: Moosehead Lake. When at last they understood what I meant, they repeated the word over to themselves by turns, as a philologist might—_Sebamook_—_Sebamook_—and now and then compared their results in Indian; for there was a slight difference between their dialects; and finally Tahmunt said, "Ugh! I know"—and he half rose up on the moose hide—"like here is a place, and there is a place," pointing to different spots on the hide, "and you take water from there and fill this here, and it stays here; that is _Sebamook_." I understood him to mean that it was a basin of water that does not run off, since the river comes in on one side and goes out again near the same place, leaving a permanent bay. Another Indian said that it meant Large-Bay Lake, and that _Sebago_ and _Sebec_, the names of other lakes, were related words meaning large open water. Joe said that _Seboois_ meant Little River. I noticed their often-described inability to convey an abstract idea. Having grasped the idea, however dimly, they groped in vain for words with which to express it. Tahmunt thought that the whites called it Moosehead Lake because Mount Kineo, which commands it, is shaped like a moose's head, and that Moose River was so called "because the mountain points right across the lake to its mouth." John Josselyn, writing about 1673, says: "Twelve miles from Casco Bay, and passable for men and horses, is a lake, called by the Indians Sebug. On the brink thereof, at one end, is the famous rock, shaped like a moose deer or elk, translucent, and called the Moose Rock." He seems to have confused Sebamook with Sebago, which lies nearer but has no "translucent" rock on its shore. I give some more of their interpretations, for what they are worth—partly _because_ they sometimes differ from the generally accepted ones. They had never analyzed these words before. After long deliberation and many repetitions of the word—for it cost them a good deal of effort—Tahmunt said that _Chesuncook_ meant a place where many rivers empty in (?), and he enumerated them—Penobscot, Umbazookskus, Cusabesex, Red Brook, etc. "_Caucomgomoc_—what does that mean?" "What are those large white birds?" he asked. "Gulls," said I. "Ugh! Gull Lake." _Pammadumcook_, Joe thought, meant Lake with a gravelly bottom or bed. _Kenduskeag_, Tahmunt concluded at last, after asking whether birches went up it—for he said he did not know it very well—meant something like this: "You go up the Penobscot until you come to _Kenduskeag_, and you go by, you don't turn in there. That is _Kenduskeag_." (?) Another Indian, however, who knew the river better, told us later that it meant Little Eel River. _Mattawamkeag_ was a place where two rivers meet. (?) _Penobscot_ was Rocky River. One writer says that this was "originally the name of only a section of the main channel, from the head of tide-water to a short distance above Oldtown." A very intelligent Indian whom we met later, a son-in-law of Neptune, gave us these further interpretations as well: _Umbazookskus_, Meadow Stream; _Millinoket_, Place of Islands; _Aboljacarmegus_, Smooth-Ledge Falls (and Still Water); _Aboljacarmeguscook_, the stream emptying in (the latter was the word he gave when I asked about _Aboljacknagesic_, which he did not know); _Mattahumkeag_, Sand-Creek Pond; _Piscataquis_, Branch of a River. I asked our hosts what _Musketaquid_, the Indian name of Concord, Massachusetts, meant; but they changed it to _Musketicook_ and repeated that, and Tahmunt said that it meant Dead River, which is probably correct. _Cook_ appears to mean river, and perhaps _quid_ signifies the place or ground. When I asked the meaning of the names of two of our hills, they answered that that was another language. As Tahmunt said that he traded at Quebec, my companion inquired the meaning of the word _Quebec_, about which there has been so much dispute. He did not know, but began to guess. He asked what the great ships that carried soldiers were called. "Warships," we answered. "Well," said he, "when the English ships came up the river, they could not go any farther, it was so narrow there; they had to turn back, go back—go-back—that's Que-bec." I mention this to show the value of his authority in the other cases. Late at night the two other Indians came home from moose-hunting without success, rekindled the fire, lit their pipes, smoked a while, took something strong to drink, and ate some moose meat, and, having found as much room as they could, lay down on the moose hides; and so we passed the night, two white men and four Indians, side by side. When I awoke in the morning it was drizzling. One of the Indians was lying outside for want of room, wrapped in his blanket, on the other side of the fire. Joe had neglected to wake my companion, and so he had not hunted that night. Tahmunt was making a crossbar for his canoe with a peculiarly shaped knife, such as I have since seen other Indians use as well. The blade was thin, about three quarters of an inch wide and eight or nine inches long, but bent out of its plane into a hook, which, he said, made it handier to scrape with. Since the Indians far to the north and northwest use the same kind of knife, I suspect that it was made after an aboriginal pattern, though some white craftsmen may use a similar one. For breakfast the Indians baked themselves a loaf of flour bread in a three-legged skillet, which stood on edge before the fire; and while my companion made tea, I caught a dozen sizable fish in the Penobscot, two kinds of suckers and one trout. After we had breakfasted by ourselves, one of our bedfellows, who had also breakfasted, came along, took a cup of tea at our invitation, and finally reached for the common platter and licked it clean. But he was nothing to a white fellow, a lumberman, who was continually stuffing himself with the Indians' moose meat and was accordingly the butt of his companions' jokes. He seems to have thought it was a feast at which one had to "eat it all." It is commonly said that the white man in the end surpasses the Indian on his own ground, and in this case it proved true. I cannot swear to how he occupied himself during the hours of darkness, but I saw him at it again as soon as it was light, though he came a quarter of a mile to his work. The rain kept us from staying any longer in the woods; so we gave the Indians some of our provisions and utensils and took leave of them. Since this was the steamer's day, I set out at once for the lake. I walked over the portage alone and waited at the head of the lake. An eagle, or some other large bird, flew screaming from its perch on the shore at my approach. For an hour after I reached the shore there was not a human being to be seen, and I had all that wide prospect to myself. I thought I heard the sound of the steamer before it came into sight on the open lake. At the landing, as the steamer came in, I noticed one of our bedfellows who had been moose-hunting the night before, now very smartly dressed in a clean white shirt and fine black trousers, a true Indian dandy, who had evidently come over the portage to show himself to any arrivals at the north end of Moosehead Lake, just as New York dandies take a stroll up Broadway and stand on the steps of a hotel. In the middle of the lake we took on board two manly-looking middle-aged men with their batteau, who had been exploring for six weeks as far as the Canadian line and had let their beards grow. They had the skin of a beaver they had recently caught stretched on an oval hoop, though the fur was not good at that season. I talked with one of them and told him that I had come all this way partly to see where the white pine grew, the eastern timber of which our houses are built, but that on this and an earlier excursion into another part of Maine I had found it a scarce tree; and I asked him where I ought to look for it. With a smile he answered that he could hardly tell me. He said, however, that in a place where people believed there was none left, he had found enough to keep two teams busy the next winter. What was now considered a "first-class" tree would not even have been looked at twenty years ago, when he went into the business; but they now got along very well with what was then considered quite inferior timber. The explorer used to cut higher and higher up into a tree to see if it was rotten at the heart, and if there was a rotten core as thick as his arm, he let it stand; but now they felled such a tree and sawed all around the rot, and it yielded the very best boards, for in such a case they were never cracked. A man engaged in the lumber business at Bangor told me that the largest pine his firm cut last winter "scaled" four thousand five hundred feet in the woods, and was worth ninety dollars as a log at the Bangor boom in Oldtown. For this one tree they cut a road three and a half miles long. He thought that the principal region for the white pine now coming down the Penobscot lay on the upper waters of the East Branch and the Allegash, around Webster Stream and Eagle Lake and Chamberlain Lake. Much timber has been stolen from the public lands. (And what kind of forest warden, pray, is the public itself?) I heard of a man who, having discovered some especially fine trees just inside the boundaries of the public land, and not daring to hire an accomplice, cut them down and, with the help of a block and tackle and without any team, rolled them into a river, and so managed to get away with them without the least assistance. Surely it is not as mean to steal pines in this way as to rob henroosts. We reached Monson that evening and rode to Bangor the next day, again all the way in the rain, varying our route a little. Some of the inns on this road, which were particularly dirty, were evidently in a state of transition from camp to house. * * * * * The next forenoon we rode to Oldtown. A slight old Indian on the Oldtown shore, who recognized my companion, was full of merriment and gestures, like a Frenchman. A Catholic priest crossed over to the island with us in the same batteau. The Indian houses are framed, mostly one story high, and stand in rows one behind another at the south end of the island, with a few scattered ones besides. I counted about forty, not including the church and what my companion called the council house. The latter, which I suppose is their town hall, was neatly framed and shingled like the rest. There were several two-story ones, quite neat, with fenced front yards, and at least one had green blinds. Here and there around them moose hides were stretched to dry. There were no carriage roads and no tracks of horses, only footpaths; very little cultivated land, but an abundance of weeds, native and naturalized; more introduced weeds than useful vegetables, just as the Indian is said to cultivate the vices rather than the virtues of the white man. Nevertheless this village was cleaner than I had expected, far cleaner than such Irish villages as I have seen. The children were neither particularly ragged nor dirty. The little boys came to meet us with bows in hand and arrows on the string, crying, "Put up a cent!" Truly, the Indian has only a feeble grip on his bow now; but the white man's curiosity is insatiable, and from the first he has been eager to witness this forest skill. That elastic piece of wood with its feathered arrow, which contact with civilization will so surely unstring, may serve as the emblem, the coat of arms, of the savage. Alas for the hunter race! The white man has driven off their game and put a cent in its place. I saw an Indian woman washing at the water's edge. She stood on a rock, and after dipping the clothes in the river, she laid them on the rock and beat them with a short club. In the graveyard, which was crowded with graves and overrun with weeds, I noticed an inscription in Indian, painted on a wooden grave board. On the island stood a large wooden cross. Since my companion knew him, we called on Governor Neptune, who lived in a little "ten-foot house," one of the humblest of all. Personalities are permitted in speaking of public men, so I will give the particulars of our visit. He was in bed. When we entered the room, which made up one half of the house, he was sitting on the edge of the bed. A clock hung in one corner.
