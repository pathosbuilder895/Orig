It struck me that the outline of this mountain at the southwest of the lake, and of another beyond it, resembled not only that of the enormous rock waves of Webster Stream but, by and large, that of Kineo on Moosehead Lake, with a similar but less abrupt precipice at the southeast end; in short, that all the prominent hills and ridges hereabouts were greater or lesser Kineos, and that there was possibly some such relationship between Kineo and the rocks of Webster Stream. The Indian did not know exactly where the outlet was, whether in the extreme southwest corner or farther east, and at the last resting place had asked to see my plan, but I had forgotten to show it to him. As usual, he felt his way forward on a middle course between two probable points, from which he could in the end turn aside toward either one without losing much distance. As we neared the south shore, since the clouds looked squally and the waves were running rather high, we steered so as to come partly under the lee of an island, though at a great distance from it. I could not make out the outlet until we were almost in it and I heard the water falling over the dam there. Here there was a considerable fall and a very solid dam, but no sign of a hut or a camp. The hunter we had met at Telos Lake had told us that there were plenty of trout here, but at this hour they would not bite, only cousin trout, from right in the midst of the rushing water. There are not as many fish in these rivers as in the Concord. While we lingered here, Polis took the opportunity to cut part of the hair off his moose hide with his big knife, making it lighter and preparing it for drying. I noticed at several old Indian camps in the woods the heap of hair they had cut from their hides. After carrying over the dam, he shot the rapids and left us to walk a mile or more, where for the most part there was no path, only very dense and laborious going close to the river. At last he would call out to let us know where he was waiting for us with his canoe, when, because of the windings of the river, we did not know where the bank was; but he did not call often enough, forgetting that we were not Indians. He seemed to be very sparing of his breath—and yet he was surprised when we walked past or failed to hit the right spot. This was not because he was disobliging, but was a proof of superior manners. Indians like to get along with as little communication and fuss as possible. In truth he was paying us a great compliment all the while, by assuming that we would rather have a hint than a kick. At length, climbing over the willows and fallen trees when that was easier than going around or under them, we caught up with the canoe and glided for several miles down the river on smooth but swift water. I noticed here again, as on Webster Stream, and on a still larger scale the next day, that the river was a smooth and evenly inclined plane down which we glided. As we glided along in this way, we started up the first black ducks that we had recognized as such. We decided to camp early this evening, so that we would have plenty of time before dark; so we stopped at the first suitable bank, where there was a narrow gravel beach on the west side, about five miles below the outlet of the lake. It was an interesting spot, where the river began to make a great bend to the east, and the last of the peculiar moose-faced Nerlumskeechticook mountains, not far southwest of Grand Lake, rose darkly in the northwest a short distance beyond, showing its gray, steeply sloping southeast side; but we could not see it without stepping out onto the bank. Two steps from the water on either side, and you reach the abrupt, bushy and rooty, if not turf-covered, edge of the bank, four or five feet high, where the endless forest begins, as if the river had only just cut its way through it. It is surprising, when you land anywhere in this unbroken wilderness, how often, at least within a few rods of the river, you see the marks of the axe, left by loggers who have either camped here or floated logs past in former springs. You may see where they, bound on the same errand as yourself, hacked large chips off a tall white-pine stump for their fire. While we were pitching camp and getting supper, the Indian cut the rest of the hair off his moose hide and set about stretching it upright on a makeshift frame between two small trees, half a dozen feet away on the opposite side of the fire, tying it and drawing it taut with arbor-vitae bark, which was always at hand and in this case was stripped from one of the trees it was tied to. When we asked for a new kind of tea, he made us some, quite good, from the checkerberry (_Gaultheria procumbens_), which covered the ground, by dropping into the kettle a small bunch of it tied together with cedar bark; but it was not quite equal to the _Chiogenes_. We therefore called this place the Checkerberry-Tea Camp. I was struck by the abundance of _Linnæa borealis_, checkerberry, and _Chiogenes hispidula_ almost everywhere in the woods of Maine. The wintergreen (_Chimaphila umbellata_) was still in bloom here, and Clintonia berries were abundant and ripe. This pretty plant is one of the most common in that forest. Here we first noticed moosewood in fruit along the banks. The prevailing trees were spruce (usually black spruce), arbor-vitae, canoe birch (black ash and elms were beginning to appear), yellow birch, red maple, and a little hemlock lurking about in the woods. The Indian said that the rotten wood of the silver maple was the best tinder, that of the yellow birch quite good, but hard. After supper he put the moose tongue and lips on to boil, cutting out the _septum_. He showed me how to write on the underside of birch bark with a black-spruce twig, which is hard and tough and can be sharpened to a point. Shortly before nightfall the Indian strolled a little way into the woods, and when he came back he said, “Me found great treasure—fifty, sixty dollars’ worth.” “What is it?” we asked. “Steel traps, under a log, thirty or forty, didn’t count ’em. Me think Indian work—worth three dollars apiece.” It was a strange coincidence that he should have gone to that particular log and looked under it, in that pathless forest. I saw chivin and chub in the river as I was washing my hands, but my companion tried in vain to catch them. I also heard the notes of bullfrogs from a swamp on the opposite side, and at first thought they were moose; a duck paddled swiftly by; and as I sat in that dusky wilderness, under that dark mountain, by the bright river, which was full of reflected light, I still heard the wood thrush singing, as if no higher civilization could be attained. By now night had settled over us. You generally make your camp just at sunset, and gather wood, get your supper, or pitch your tent while the shades of night are gathering around you and deepening the already dense gloom of the forest. You have no time to look around or explore anything before it is dark. You may penetrate half a dozen rods farther into that twilight wilderness in search of some dry bark to kindle your fire with, and wonder what mysteries lie hidden still deeper in it, say at the end of a long day’s march; or you run down to the shore to fetch a dipper of water and get a clearer view a short distance up or down the river, and as you stand there you see a fish leap or a duck alight on the river, or hear a wood thrush or a robin singing in the woods. That is as if you had been to town, or to civilized parts. But there is no question of sauntering about to see the country, and ten or fifteen rods seem a great distance from your companions, and you come back with the air of a much-traveled man, as from a long journey, with adventures to relate, although you may have heard the crackling of the fire the whole time—and at a hundred rods you might be hopelessly lost and have to camp out. It is all mossy and _moosey_. In some of these dense fir and spruce woods there is hardly room for the smoke to rise. The trees are a _standing_ night, and every fir and spruce that you fell is a feather plucked from night’s raven wing. Then at night the general stillness is more impressive than any sound, but now and then you hear the hoot of an owl, farther or nearer in the woods, and, if you are near a lake, the half-human cry of the loons at their unearthly revels. Tonight the Indian lay between the fire and his stretched moose hide, to escape the mosquitoes. He even made a small, smoky fire of damp leaves at his head and at his feet, and then, as usual, wrapped his head in his blanket. We, with our veils and our ointment, were fairly comfortable, but it would be difficult to pursue any sedentary occupation in the woods at this season; you cannot read much through a veil in the evening by the light of a fire, nor handle pencil and paper well with gloves on or with greased fingers. * * * * * FRIDAY, July 31. The Indian said, “You and me kill moose last night, so take best wood. Always take hard wood for cook moose meat.” His “best wood” was sugar maple. He threw the moose’s lip into the fire to singe off the hair, and then rolled it up with the meat to take along. When he noticed that we were sitting down to breakfast without pork, he said with a very serious face, “Me want little fat,” whereupon he was told he could have as much as he wished to fry. We had smooth but swift water for a considerable distance, over which we glided rapidly, starting up ducks and kingfishers. But, as usual, our smooth progress soon came to an end, and we had to carry canoe and all about half a mile down the right bank around some rapids or falls. It sometimes took sharp eyes to tell on which side the carry was before going over the falls, but Polis landed us at the right spot every time. The raspberries were especially abundant and large here, and everyone set to eating them, the Indian remarking on their size. Often the path over bare, rocky carries was so indistinct that I lost it repeatedly, but when I walked behind him I observed that he could keep it almost like a hound, and rarely hesitated, or, if he paused for a moment on a bare rock, his eye at once detected some sign that would have escaped me. Frequently _we_ found no path at all at these places and were, inexplicably to him, held up. He would merely say that it was “ver strange.” We had heard of a Great Fall on this river and thought that every fall we came to must be it, but after we had christened several in succession by that name, we gave up looking for it. There were more Great or Little Falls than I can remember. I cannot say how many times we had to go on foot because of falls or rapids. We kept expecting the river to take a final leap and reach still water, but that morning there was no improvement. The carries, however, were a pleasant change. As surely as we got out of the canoe and stretched our legs, we found ourselves in a blueberry and raspberry garden, for both sides of our rocky path around the falls were lined with one or both. There was no carry on the main stream of the East Branch where we did not find an abundance of these two berries, for these were the rockiest places, and partly cleared, as these plants prefer, and no one had been there before us to pick the best. On our three trips over the carries—for we had to cover the distance three times every time the canoe was taken out—we did full justice to the berries, and they were just what we needed to counteract the effects of our diet of hard bread and pork. Another name for carrying around might have been “going a-berrying.” We also found some amelanchier or serviceberries, though most of them were shriveled, but they held on rather more generally than in Concord. The Indian called them _pemoymenuk_ and said that in some places they bore a great deal of fruit. He also sometimes ate the northern wild red cherries, saying they were good medicine, but they were scarcely edible. We bathed and had dinner at the foot of one of these carries. It was the Indian who usually reminded us that it was dinnertime, sometimes even by turning the bow toward the shore. Once he made an indirect but elaborate apology, saying that we might think it strange, but someone who works hard all day sets great store by getting his dinner at the proper time. At the most considerable fall on this river, as I was walking over the carry close behind the Indian, he noticed a track on the rock, which was only thinly covered with soil, and, stooping, muttered, “Caribou.” When we came back, he noticed near the same spot a much larger track, where the foot of some animal had sunk into a small hollow in the rock that was partly filled with grass and earth, and he exclaimed in surprise, “What that?” “Well, what is it?” I asked. He stooped, put his hand in it, and answered with a mysterious air and half in a whisper, “Devil [that is, Indian devil, or cougar]—ledges round here—very bad animal—tear rocks all to pieces.” “How long ago was it made?” I asked. “Today or yesterday,” he said. But when I later asked him whether he was sure it was the devil’s track, he said he did not know. I had been told that the scream of a cougar had recently been heard in the neighborhood of Ktaadn, and we were not far from that mountain.
