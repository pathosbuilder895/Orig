CHAPTER X The Copyist On the eastern edge of Chancery Lane, or more precisely in Cook’s Court, Cursitor Street, Mr. Snagsby, law-stationer, pursues his lawful calling. In the shade of Cook’s Court, which is for the most part a shady place, Mr. Snagsby deals in all sorts of blank forms for legal proceedings; in skins and rolls of parchment; in paper—foolscap, brief paper, draft paper, brown, white, grayish-white, and blotting paper; in stamps; in office quills, steel pens, ink, india rubber, pounce, pins, pencils, sealing wax, and wafers; in red tape and green silk braid; in pocketbooks, almanacs, diaries, and law lists; in twine boxes, rulers, inkstands—glass and lead—penknives, scissors, awls, and other small office implements; in short, in articles too numerous to list, and has done so ever since he finished his apprenticeship and went into partnership with Peffer. On that occasion Cook’s Court was in a manner revolutionized by the new inscription in fresh paint, PEFFER AND SNAGSBY, which displaced the time-honored and not easily deciphered legend PEFFER alone. For smoke, the ivy of London, had so twined itself about Peffer’s name and clung so closely to his dwelling that the affectionate parasite had quite smothered the tree it grew on. Peffer is never seen in Cook’s Court nowadays. Nor is he expected there, for he has lain stretched out for a quarter of a century in the churchyard of St. Andrew’s, Holborn, while the wagons and hackney coaches roar past him all day and half the night like one great dragon. If he should ever steal out, while the dragon is at rest, to breathe fresh air once more in Cook’s Court until the crowing of the hopeful cock in the cellar of the little dairy in Cursitor Street calls him back, a cock whose ideas of daylight it would be curious indeed to fathom, since he knows next to nothing of it from personal observation—if Peffer, that is, should ever revisit the pale glimmer of Cook’s Court, which no law-stationer in the trade can positively deny, he comes invisibly, and no one is any the worse or the wiser for it. In his lifetime, and likewise during Snagsby’s “apprenticeship” of seven long years, there lived with Peffer, in the same business premises, a niece—a small, shrewd niece, laced somewhat too violently about the waist, and with a sharp nose like a cutting autumn evening that turns frosty toward its close. Among the courtiers of Cook’s Court the rumor went about that this niece’s mother, driven in her daughter’s childhood by an all too jealous anxiety that the girl’s figure should approach perfection, had laced her up every morning, bracing her maternal foot against the bedpost to gain a firmer purchase and more leverage; and, further, that she had administered to her internally whole pints of vinegar and lemon juice, which acids, it was believed, had gone to the patient’s nose and temper. From whichever of Rumor’s many tongues this frothy report may have sprung, it either never reached young Snagsby’s ears or never swayed them; for he, having come to man’s estate, wooed the fair heroine of the tale, won her, and so entered into two partnerships at once. And so now, in Cook’s Court, Cursitor Street, Mr. Snagsby and the niece are one; and the niece still tends and cherishes her figure, which, however tastes may differ, is indisputably precious in that there is precious little of it. Mr. and Mrs. Snagsby are not only one bone and one flesh but, in the neighbors’ opinion, one voice as well. That voice, which seems to proceed from Mrs. Snagsby alone, is heard very often in Cook’s Court. Mr. Snagsby is seldom heard except insofar as he finds expression through those dulcet tones. He is a mild, bald, timid man with a shiny head and a scrubby tuft of black hair sticking out at the back. He tends toward meekness and stoutness. When he stands at his door in Cook’s Court in his gray shop coat and black calico sleeves, looking up at the clouds, or when he stands behind a desk in his dark shop with a heavy flat ruler, snipping and cutting at sheepskin in the company of his two apprentices, he is emphatically a retiring and unassuming man. From beneath his feet at such times, as from some shrill ghost that cannot rest in its grave, complaints and lamentations in the voice already mentioned frequently rise; and when on certain occasions these reach a sharper pitch than usual, Mr. Snagsby is apt to remark to the apprentices, “I think my little woman is giving it to Guster good and proper again!” This proper name, as Mr. Snagsby uses it, has before now spurred the wit of the courtiers of Cook’s Court to the observation that it ought by rights to be Mrs. Snagsby’s name, since, in honor of her stormy character, she might with great aptness and expressiveness be called a Guster, a regular squall. It is, however, the property—and, apart from fifty shillings a year and a very small box scantily filled with clothes, the only property—of a lean young woman from a workhouse (whom some suppose to have been christened Augusta), who, although during her growing years she was boarded out or kept under contract by an amiable benefactor of his species resident in Tooting, and so cannot possibly have developed under any but the most favorable circumstances, “has fits,” which the parish cannot account for. Guster, really three or four and twenty but looking a good ten years older, is to be had cheap on account of this inexplicable defect, the fits, and is so afraid of being returned to her patron saint that, except when she is found with her head in the pail, or in the sink, or in the wash boiler, or in the dinner, or in anything else that happens to be near her at the time of her fit, she is always at work. She is a satisfaction to the parents and guardians of the apprentices, who feel that there is little danger of her awakening tender emotions in the youthful breast; she is a satisfaction to Mrs. Snagsby, who can always find fault with her; she is a satisfaction to Mr. Snagsby, who thinks it a work of mercy to keep her. In Guster’s eyes the law-stationer’s establishment is a temple of plenty and splendor. She believes the little drawing room upstairs, which is always kept, so to speak, with its hair in curlpapers and its apron on, to be the most elegant apartment in Christendom. The view it commands of Cook’s Court on one side (to say nothing of a squinting glimpse into Cursitor Street) and of the back yard of Coavinses, the bailiff, on the other, she regards as a panorama of unrivaled beauty. The portraits it displays in oil—and plenty of oil, too—of Mr. Snagsby looking at Mrs. Snagsby and of Mrs. Snagsby looking at Mr. Snagsby are in her eyes masterpieces by a Raphael or a Titian. Guster has some compensations for her many privations. Mr. Snagsby leaves everything that is not among the practical secrets of the business to Mrs. Snagsby. She keeps the money, takes the tax collectors to task, fixes the times and places of Sunday devotions, sanctions Mr. Snagsby’s amusements, and acknowledges no responsibility whatever for what she sees fit to put on the table for dinner, so much so that she serves as the lofty standard of comparison for the wives of the neighborhood a good way down Chancery Lane on both sides, and even out in Holborn, who in all domestic skirmishes habitually invite their husbands to consider the difference between their (the wives’) position and Mrs. Snagsby’s, and between their (the husbands’) conduct and Mr. Snagsby’s. Rumor, which always flits batlike about Cook’s Court and darts in and out at everybody’s windows, does maintain, however, that Mrs. Snagsby is jealous and inquisitive, that Mr. Snagsby is at times fairly tormented out of house and home, and that if he had even the courage of a mouse he would not put up with it. It is even observed that the wives who hold him up to their self-willed husbands as a shining example in fact look down on him, and that nobody does so more haughtily than a certain lady whose lord is more than merely suspected of bringing his umbrella down upon her as an instrument of correction. But this vague muttering may arise from Mr. Snagsby’s being, in his way, a rather contemplative and poetic man, who likes to walk in Staple Inn in the summertime and observe how countrified the sparrows and the leaves are there, and who also likes to stroll about the Rolls Yard of a Sunday afternoon and remark (if he is in good spirits) that there were old times once, and that under that chapel there, he’d lay a wager, you’d be sure to find a stone coffin or two still, if you was to go digging. He also regales his imagination with thoughts of the many Lord Chancellors and Vice Chancellors and Masters of the Rolls who are deceased; and he gets such a breath of country air out of telling the two apprentices how he HAS heard say that a brook “as clear as crystal” once ran right through the middle of Holborn, when Turnstile really was a turnstile, leading straight out into the meadows—gets such a breath of country air out of this that he never feels any desire to go into the country. The day is drawing to a close, and the gas is lit, but it is not yet fully effective, for it is not quite dark. Mr. Snagsby, standing at his shop door and looking up at the clouds, sees a crow, out late, skim westward over the strip of sky that belongs to Cook’s Court. The crow flies straight across Chancery Lane and the garden of Lincoln’s Inn to Lincoln’s Inn Fields. Here, in a large house, once a lordly mansion, lives Mr. Tulkinghorn. It is now divided up and let in separate sets of chambers, and in those shrunken fragments of its former greatness lawyers lie like maggots in nuts. But its spacious staircases, passages, and antechambers remain; and even its painted ceilings, where Allegory, in Roman helmet and celestial linen, sprawls among balustrades and columns, flowers, clouds, and thick-legged boys, and gives one a headache—which, it seems, is more or less always the purpose of Allegory. Here, among his many boxes labeled with exalted names, lives Mr. Tulkinghorn, when he is not a speechless guest at country houses where the great ones of the earth bore themselves to death. Here he sits today, quietly at his table. An oyster of the old school, whom nobody can open. As he looks, so looks his dwelling in the dusk of this afternoon. Rusty, out of date, shrinking from notice, and able to afford it. Heavy, broad-backed, old-fashioned chairs of mahogany and horsehair, not easily lifted; obsolete tables with spindle legs and dusty felt covers; dedicatory engravings of the bearers of great titles of the last generation or the one before it surround him. A thick, grimy Turkey carpet muffles the floor where he sits, attended by two candles in old-fashioned silver candlesticks that shed a most inadequate light on his large room. The titles on the backs of his books have retreated into the bindings; everything that can have a lock has one; no key is to be seen. Very few loose papers lie about. He has a manuscript beside him but does not consult it. With the round lid of an inkstand and two broken bits of sealing wax he works silently and slowly at some chain of indecision running through his head. Now the inkstand lid is in the middle, now the red bit of sealing wax, now the black. That is not it. Mr. Tulkinghorn must gather them all up again and begin afresh. Here, beneath the painted ceiling, with the foreshortened Allegory staring down at the intruder as though it meant to pounce on him, and he studiously not deigning to give it a glance, Mr. Tulkinghorn has both his home and his office. He keeps no staff, only one middle-aged man, usually a little threadbare at the elbows, who sits in a high pew in the hall and is seldom overburdened with business. Mr. Tulkinghorn is no ordinary case. He needs no clerks. He is a great storehouse of confidences, not to be tapped in that way. His clients want HIM; he is all in all. Drafts he wants drawn up are drawn up by special pleaders in the Temple on mysterious instructions; fair copies he wants made are made at the stationers’, expense being no object. The middle-aged man in the pew knows scarcely more of the affairs of the high nobility than any street sweeper in Holborn. The red bit, the black bit, the inkstand lid, the other inkstand lid, the little sand box. So! You into the middle, you to the right, you to the left. This chain of indecision must surely be brought to an end now or never. Now! Mr. Tulkinghorn gets up, adjusts his spectacles, puts on his hat, puts the manuscript in his pocket, goes out, and says to the middle-aged man with the threadbare elbows, “I shall be back directly.” Very rarely does he tell him anything more definite. Mr. Tulkinghorn goes, as the crow came—not quite so straight, but nearly—to Cook’s Court, Cursitor Street. To Snagsby’s, Law Stationery, Deeds Drawn Up and Copied, Legal Writing of All Kinds Undertaken, etc., etc., etc. It is somewhere around five or six o’clock in the afternoon, and a sweet smell of warm tea hovers in Cook’s Court. It hovers about Snagsby’s door. Early hours are kept there: dinner at half past one and supper at half past nine. Mr. Snagsby had just been about to go down into the subterranean regions to take his tea when he looked out of his door a little earlier and saw the crow that was out late. “Is the master in?” Guster is minding the shop, for the apprentices are taking tea with Mr. and Mrs. Snagsby in the kitchen; consequently the two daughters of the robe-maker, combing their curls before the two mirrors in the two second-floor windows of the house opposite, are not, as they fondly believe, driving the two apprentices out of their wits, but are merely awakening the fruitless admiration of Guster, whose hair will not grow, never would, and, it is confidently supposed, never will. “Is the master in?” asks Mr. Tulkinghorn. The master is in, and Guster will fetch him. Guster vanishes, glad to get out of the shop, which she regards with a mixture of dread and awe as an armory of terrible instruments of the great torture of the law—a place not to be entered once the gas has been turned off. Mr. Snagsby appears, greasy, warm, smelling of herbs, and chewing. Swallows a bit of bread and butter. Says, “Goodness me, sir! Mr. Tulkinghorn!” “I should like half a word with you, Snagsby.” “Certainly, sir! Dear me, sir, why didn’t you send your young man round for me? Please step into the back room, sir.” Snagsby has brightened up in an instant. The cramped room, which smells strongly of parchment grease, is storeroom, counting house, and writing room in one. Mr. Tulkinghorn sits down, facing him, on a stool at the desk. “Jarndyce and Jarndyce, Snagsby.” “Yes, sir.” Mr. Snagsby turns up the gas and gives a little cough behind his hand, in modest anticipation of profit. Mr. Snagsby, being a timid man, is in the habit of coughing with a variety of expressions and so saving words. “You copied some affidavits in that case for me recently.” “Yes, sir, we did.”
