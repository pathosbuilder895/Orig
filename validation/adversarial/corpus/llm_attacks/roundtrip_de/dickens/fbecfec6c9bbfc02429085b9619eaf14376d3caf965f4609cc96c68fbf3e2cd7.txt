The little plaintiff or defendant who had been promised a new rocking-horse for the day Jarndyce versus Jarndyce should be decided has grown up, got himself a real horse, and trotted off into the other world. Pretty wards of the court have faded into mothers and grandmothers; a long procession of Lord Chancellors has come and gone; the legion of pleadings in the suit has turned into mere lists of the dead; there are perhaps not three Jarndyces left on earth since old Tom Jarndyce, in his despair, put a bullet through his head in a coffee-house in Chancery Lane; but Jarndyce versus Jarndyce still drags its whole dreary length along before the court, forever hopeless. Jarndyce versus Jarndyce has become a joke. That is the only good that has ever come of it. To many the suit has been death, but in the profession it is a joke. Every Master in Chancery has had a reference out of it. Every Lord Chancellor, back when he still stood at the bar as counsel, was "in it" for somebody or other. Excellent bons mots have been made about it by blue-nosed old benchers of the Inns in bulbous shoes, over select rounds of port after dinner in hall. Articled clerks used to sharpen their legal wit on it. The last Lord Chancellor handled it deftly when he corrected Mr. Blowers, the eminent Crown counsel in his silk gown, who had said that such a thing might happen when it rained potatoes from the sky, with the remark, "Or when we are through with Jarndyce versus Jarndyce, Mr. Blowers"—a jest that particularly delighted the maces, bags, and purses. How many people outside the suit Jarndyce versus Jarndyce has stretched out its baleful hand to spoil and corrupt would be a very wide question. From the Master, upon whose impaling file-spikes stacks of dusty orders in Jarndyce versus Jarndyce have grimly writhed into many shapes, down to the copying clerk in the Six Clerks' Office who has copied his tens of thousands of Chancery folio pages under that eternal heading, no man's nature has been made the better by it. In intrigue, evasion, delay, plunder, drudgery, and false pretenses of every kind, there are influences that can never lead to good. Even the lawyers' errand boys, who have kept the wretched suitors at bay by protesting time out of mind that Mr. Chizzle, Mizzle, or whatever his name was, was particularly engaged just then and had appointments until lunch, may have picked up an extra moral twist and a bent for shuffling from Jarndyce versus Jarndyce. The receiver in the case has acquired a handsome sum of money by it, but he has also acquired a distrust of his own mother and a contempt for his own kind. Chizzle, Mizzle, and whatever else they are called have fallen into the habit of vaguely resolving that they will look into that little outstanding matter and see what can be done for Drizzle—who was badly treated—as soon as Jarndyce versus Jarndyce has been got out of Chancery. Shirking and sharp practice in all their many varieties have been sown with a lavish hand by the ill-fated suit; and even those who have contemplated its history from the outermost circle of such evil have been insensibly tempted into the lax habit of leaving bad things to themselves and their own bad course, and into the lax belief that if the world goes wrong, it was, in some offhand way, probably never meant to go right. And so, in the midst of the mud and at the heart of the fog, the Lord High Chancellor sits in his High Court of Chancery. "Mr. Tangle," says the Lord High Chancellor, who has lately grown somewhat restless under that learned gentleman's eloquence. "Mlud," says Mr. Tangle. Mr. Tangle knows more about Jarndyce versus Jarndyce than anybody else. He is famous for it—he is supposed never to have read anything else since leaving school. "Will you soon be finished with your argument?" "Mlud, no—variety of points—think it m'duty t'submit—ludship," is the reply that slips out of Mr. Tangle. "Several members of the bar are still to be heard, I believe?" says the Chancellor with a slight smile. Eighteen of Mr. Tangle's learned friends, each armed with a little summary of eighteen hundred sheets, bob up like eighteen hammers in a pianoforte, make eighteen bows, and sink back into their eighteen places of obscurity. "We will resume the hearing on Wednesday fortnight," says the Chancellor. For the question at issue is only a question of costs, a mere bud on the forest tree of the main suit, and really will come to a decision one of these days. The Chancellor rises; the bar rises; the prisoner is hurriedly brought forward; the man from Shropshire cries out, "My lord!" Maces, bags, and purses indignantly call for silence and glare at the man from Shropshire. "With regard to," the Chancellor continues, still occupied with Jarndyce versus Jarndyce, "the young girl—" "Beg y'r ludship's pardon—boy," says Mr. Tangle prematurely. "With regard to," the Chancellor continues with particular distinctness, "the young girl and the boy, the two young people"—Mr. Tangle is crushed—"whom I directed to appear today and who are now in my private room, I will see them and satisfy myself as to whether it is expedient to make the order that they shall reside with their uncle." Mr. Tangle is on his feet again. "Beg y'r ludship's pardon—dead." "With their"—the Chancellor looks through his double eyeglass at the papers on his desk—"grandfather." "Beg y'r ludship's pardon—victim of rash act—brains." Suddenly, from the farthest settlements of the fog, a very little counsel with a tremendous bass voice rises, fully inflated, and says, "Will your lordship allow me? I appear for him. He is a cousin, several times removed. I am not at the moment in a position to inform the court in precisely what degree he is a cousin, but a cousin he IS." While this address (delivered like a message from the tomb) echoes in the rafters, the very little counsel sinks back, and the fog knows him no more. Everyone looks for him. Nobody can see him. "I will speak with both young people," says the Chancellor again, "and satisfy myself on the question of whether they shall reside with their cousin. I will raise the matter tomorrow morning when I take my seat." The Chancellor is just about to bow to the bar when the prisoner is brought forward. Nothing can possibly come of the prisoner's tangled situation except his being sent back to prison, which is soon done. The man from Shropshire ventures another protesting "My lord!", but the Chancellor, having noticed him, has deftly vanished. Everyone else quickly vanishes too. A battery of blue bags is loaded with heavy charges of papers and carried off by clerks; the little mad old woman marches away with her documents; the empty courtroom is locked up. If only all the injustice it has done and all the misery it has caused could be locked up along with it, and the whole lot burned on one great pyre—well, so much the better for other parties than the parties in Jarndyce versus Jarndyce! CHAPTER II In the Fashionable World It is only a passing glance at the fashionable world that we need on this same muddy afternoon. It is not so unlike the Court of Chancery that we cannot pass from the one scene to the other in a straight line, as the crow flies. Both the fashionable world and the Court of Chancery are things of precedent and custom: sleepy Rip Van Winkles who have played strange games through a great deal of thundery weather; Sleeping Beauties whom the knight will one day wake, when all the stopped spits in the kitchen begin to turn prodigiously! It is not a large world. Even in relation to this world of ours, which has its limits too (as Your Highness will find when you have gone all the way around it and reached the edge of the void beyond), it is a very tiny speck. There is much good in it; there are many good and sincere people in it; it has its appointed place. But the trouble with it is that it is a world wrapped up in too much jeweler's cotton and fine wool, and it cannot hear the roar of the larger worlds, and cannot see them as they circle round the sun. It is a deadened world, and for want of air its growth is sometimes unhealthy. My Lady Dedlock has returned to her town house for a few days before leaving for Paris, where her ladyship intends to stay for some weeks; where she will go after that is uncertain. So the society columns report, for the comfort of the Parisians, and they know everything that is fashionable. To know things otherwise would be unfashionable. My Lady Dedlock has been down at what she calls, in confidential conversation, her "country seat" in Lincolnshire. In Lincolnshire the waters have overflowed their banks. An arch of the bridge in the park has been undermined and sodden and washed away. The adjoining low-lying land is, for half a mile across, a stagnant river, with melancholy trees for islands in it and a surface riddled all over, all day long, by the falling rain. My Lady Dedlock's country seat has been extremely dreary. The weather has been so wet for so many days and nights that the trees seem soaked through, and the soft limbs and branches that the woodcutter's axe lops off can neither crack nor crackle as they fall. The deer, looking drenched, leave quagmires where they pass. The crack of a rifle loses its sharpness in the damp air, and its smoke drifts in a sluggish little cloud toward the green rise crowned with a copse that serves as a backdrop for the falling rain. The view from My Lady Dedlock's own windows is alternately a lead-colored view and a view in Indian ink. The vases on the stone terrace in the foreground catch the rain all day; and the heavy drops fall—drip, drip, drip—all night upon the broad flagstone pavement that has been called the Ghost's Walk since olden times. On Sundays the little church in the park is musty; the oak pulpit breaks out in a cold sweat; and everywhere there is a smell and taste as of the old Dedlocks in their graves. My Lady Dedlock (who is childless), looking out in the early twilight from her boudoir at a gamekeeper's cottage, and seeing the glow of a fire on the lead-framed panes, and smoke rising from the chimney, and a child, chased by a woman, running out into the rain to meet the shining figure of a muffled-up man coming through the gate, has been put thoroughly out of temper. My Lady Dedlock says she has been "bored to death." That is why My Lady Dedlock has gone away from the country seat in Lincolnshire and left it to the rain and the crows and the rabbits and the deer and the partridges and pheasants. The portraits of the departed Dedlocks seemed to vanish into the damp walls out of sheer dejection as the housekeeper went through the old rooms closing the shutters. And when they will next come forth again, the society columns—which, like the Devil, are omniscient about the past and the present but not about the future—cannot yet say. Sir Leicester Dedlock is only a baronet, but there is no mightier baronet than he. His family is as old as the hills and infinitely more respectable. He is of the general opinion that the world could probably get on without hills but would be done for without Dedlocks. He would admit, on the whole, that Nature is a good idea (a little common, perhaps, when not enclosed by a park fence), but an idea whose execution depends on the great county families. He is a gentleman of strict conscience, scorns all pettiness and meanness, and is ready on the shortest notice to die any death you may please to name rather than give occasion for the slightest doubt about his integrity. He is an honorable, obstinate, truthful, high-minded, intensely prejudiced, perfectly unreasonable man. Sir Leicester is a good twenty years older than My Lady. He will never see sixty-five again, nor perhaps sixty-six, nor even sixty-seven. Now and then he has a touch of gout, and he walks a little stiffly. He is of dignified appearance, with his light gray hair and whiskers, his fine shirt frill, his pure white waistcoat, and his blue coat with bright buttons, which is always buttoned up. He is formal, measured, exceedingly courteous to My Lady on every occasion, and holds her personal charms in the highest esteem. His gallantry toward My Lady, which has never changed since he courted her, is the one little touch of romantic fancy in him. He did, in fact, marry her for love. The whisper still goes round that she had no family at all; be that as it may, Sir Leicester had so much family that perhaps he had enough of it and could do without any more. But she had beauty, pride, ambition, insolent resolve, and sense enough to furnish a whole legion of fine ladies. Wealth and station, added to these, soon carried her to the top, and for years now My Lady Dedlock has stood at the center of the society columns and at the very top of the fashionable tree. How Alexander wept when he had no more worlds to conquer, everyone knows—or has some reason to know by now, since the matter has been mentioned rather often. My Lady Dedlock, having conquered HER world, fell not into the melting but rather into the freezing mood. An exhausted composure, a worn-out calm, an equanimity of weariness that neither interest nor satisfaction can disturb, are the trophies of her victory. She is perfectly well-bred. If she could be carried up to heaven tomorrow, she might be expected to ascend without any rapture at all. She still has beauty, and if it is no longer in its bloom, it is not yet in its autumn. She has a fine face—originally of a kind one would call very pretty rather than beautiful, but refined into classical dignity by the acquired expression of her fashionable rank. Her figure is elegant and gives the impression of being tall. Not that she is, but because, as the Honourable Bob Stables has frequently declared upon oath, "the best is made of all her points." The same authority observes that she is perfectly turned out, and remarks, particularly in praise of her hair, that she is the best-groomed woman in the whole stud.
