CHAPTER XIII It was a curious evening. The only costume in the barber's shop that fitted me was that of a teddy bear. I don't mind playing bears with a few nice young girls on a winter's evening in England—but it is hardly the ideal costume for the Equator. Still, I caused a good deal of merriment and won first prize in the "brought on board" category—an absurd description for a costume hired for the evening. But since nobody seemed to have the faintest idea whether the costumes were home-made or brought along, it didn't matter. Mrs. Blair refused to dress up. Apparently she is at one with Pagett on this question. Colonel Race followed her example. Anne Beddingfeld had put together a gipsy costume for herself and looked exceptionally well. Pagett said he had a headache and did not appear. To replace him I invited a quaint little fellow called Reeves. He is a prominent member of the South African Labour Party. A horrible little man, but I want to keep on good terms with him, as he supplies me with information I need. I want to understand this Rand business from both sides. The dancing was a hot affair. I danced twice with Anne Beddingfeld, and she had to pretend she liked it. I danced once with Mrs. Blair, who didn't bother to pretend, and I commandeered various other young ladies whose looks appealed to me. Then we went down to supper. I had ordered champagne; the steward recommended Clicquot 1911 as the best they had on board, and I took his advice. I seemed to have hit upon the one thing that could loosen Colonel Race's tongue. Far from being taciturn, the man became positively talkative. For a while this amused me, then it occurred to me that Colonel Race, and not I, was becoming the centre of the party. He chaffed me at length about keeping a diary. "One day it will bring all your indiscretions to light, Pedler." "My dear Race," I said, "I venture to suggest that I am not quite the fool you take me for. I may commit indiscretions, but I don't put them down in black and white. After my death my executors will know my opinion of a great many people, but I doubt whether they will find anything to add to or subtract from their opinion of _me_. A diary is useful for recording other people's idiosyncrasies—but not one's own." "There is, however, such a thing as unconscious self-revelation." "In the eyes of the psychoanalyst everything is depraved," I replied sententiously. "You must have had a very interesting life, Colonel Race?" said Miss Beddingfeld, looking at him with big, shining eyes. That's how they do it, these girls! Othello enchanted Desdemona by telling her stories, but, oh, didn't Desdemona enchant Othello by the way she listened? At any rate the girl got Race properly going. He began telling lion stories. A man who has shot lions in large numbers has an unfair advantage over other men. It seemed to me that it was time I told a lion story too. One of a livelier kind. "By the way," I remarked, "that reminds me of a rather exciting story I once heard. A friend of mine was out hunting somewhere in East Africa. One night, for some reason, he came out of his tent and was startled by a low growl. He spun round and saw a lion crouching to spring. He had left his rifle in the tent. Quick as lightning he ducked, and the lion leapt right over his head. Annoyed at having missed him, the animal growled and crouched to spring again. Again he ducked, and again the lion leapt right over him. This happened a third time, but by now he was near the tent entrance, and he darted in and seized his rifle. When he came out again, rifle in hand, the lion had vanished. This puzzled him greatly. He crept round to the back of the tent, where there was a small clearing. And there, sure enough, was the lion, busily practising low jumps." This was received with roars of applause. I drank some champagne. "On another occasion," I remarked, "this friend of mine had a second curious experience. He was on a trek across country, and since he wanted to reach his destination before the heat of the day, he ordered his boys to harness the team while it was still dark. They had some trouble with it, as the mules were very restive, but at last they managed it, and they set off. The mules raced along like the wind, and when it grew light they saw why. In the darkness the boys had harnessed a lion as the near-side wheeler." This, too, went down well, a ripple of merriment ran round the table, but I am not sure that the greatest tribute did not come from my friend the Labour member, who remained pale and serious. "My God!" he said anxiously. "Who went and unhitched 'em again?" "I must go to Rhodesia," said Mrs. Blair. "After what you've told us, Colonel Race, I simply must. It's a dreadful journey, though, five days in the train." "You must come with me in my private car," I said gallantly. "Oh, Sir Eustace, how sweet of you, do you really mean it?" "Do I mean it!" I exclaimed reproachfully, and drank another glass of champagne. "Only about another week and we'll be in South Africa," sighed Mrs. Blair. "Ah, South Africa," I said feelingly, and began to quote from a speech I had recently given at the Colonial Institute. "What has South Africa to show the world? What indeed? Its fruit and its farms, its wool and its wattle trees, its herds and its hides, its gold and its diamonds——" I hurried on, for I knew that the moment I paused, Reeves would cut in and tell me the hides were worthless because the animals got caught on barbed wire or something of the kind, that he would run down everything else and end up with the plight of the miners on the Rand. And I was not in the mood to be abused as a capitalist. The interruption, however, came from another quarter, at the magic word diamonds. "Diamonds!" said Mrs. Blair ecstatically. "Diamonds!" breathed Miss Beddingfeld. Both turned to Colonel Race. "I suppose you've been to Kimberley?" I had been to Kimberley too, but I didn't manage to say so in time. Race was showered with questions. What were the mines like? Was it true that the natives were kept shut up in compounds? And so on. Race answered their questions and showed that he knew his subject well. He described how the natives were housed, the searches that had been introduced, and the various precautions De Beers took. "Then it's practically impossible to steal diamonds?" asked Mrs. Blair, with as lively an air of disappointment as if she had travelled there expressly for that purpose. "Nothing is impossible, Mrs. Blair. Thefts do happen—like the case I told you about, where the Kaffir hid the stone in his wound." "Yes, but on a large scale?" "Once, in recent years. Just before the war, to be precise. You must remember the case, Pedler. You were in South Africa at the time?" I nodded. "Tell us," cried Miss Beddingfeld. "Oh, please tell us!" Race smiled. "Very well, you shall have the story. I suppose most of you have heard of Sir Laurence Eardsley, the great South African mining magnate? His mines were gold mines, but he comes into the story through his son. You may remember that just before the war there were rumours going about of a possible new Kimberley, said to lie hidden somewhere in the rocky soil of the jungles of British Guiana. Two young explorers, so it was said, had returned from that part of South America bringing with them a remarkable collection of rough diamonds, some of them of considerable size. Small diamonds had been found before in the region of the Essequibo and Mazaruni rivers, but these two young men, John Eardsley and his friend Lucas, claimed to have discovered beds of large carbon deposits at the common source of two streams. The diamonds were of every colour, pink, blue, yellow, green, black, and the purest white. Eardsley and Lucas came to Kimberley, where they were to submit their stones for appraisal. At the same time it came out that a sensational robbery had taken place at De Beers. When diamonds are sent to England, they are made up into a packet. This remains in the big safe, the two keys of which are held by two different men, while a third man knows the combination. They are handed over to the bank, and the bank sends them to England. Each packet is worth, roughly speaking, about £100,000. "On this occasion the bank noticed something slightly unusual about the sealing of the packet.
