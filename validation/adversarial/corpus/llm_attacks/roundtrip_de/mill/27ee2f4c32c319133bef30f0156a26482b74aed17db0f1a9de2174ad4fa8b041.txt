Their power was the chief grievance of the country. The habit of voting at the bidding of an employer or a landlord was so firmly rooted that hardly anything could shake it except a strong popular enthusiasm, such as seldom arises except in a good cause. A vote given in opposition to those influences was therefore, in general, an honest, a public-spirited vote; but in any case, and from whatever motive it was given, it was almost certainly a good vote, for it was a vote against the fundamental evil, the overpowering influence of the oligarchy. Could the voter at that time have been enabled to exercise his privilege freely without danger to himself, even if neither honestly nor intelligently, it would have been a great gain for reform, for it would have broken the yoke of the power then ruling the country—the power that had created and was maintaining everything bad in the institutions and in the administration of the state—the power of the landlords and the borough-mongers. “The secret ballot was not introduced; but in this respect the course of circumstances has done the work of the secret ballot, and is doing it more and more. Both the political and the social condition of the country, so far as they bear on this question, have greatly changed and are changing every day. The higher classes are today no longer the masters of the country. One would have to be blind to all the signs of the times to believe that the middle classes are still as subservient to the higher, or the working classes still as dependent on the higher and middle, as they were a quarter of a century ago. The events of this quarter century have not only taught every class to know its own united strength, but have also put the individuals of a lower class in a position to defy those of a higher far more boldly. In the majority of cases the vote of the electors, whether it is given against the wishes of their superiors or in accordance with them, is today not the effect of coercion, for the application of which the same means no longer exist, but the expression of their own personal or political inclinations. The very vices of the present electoral system are a proof of this. The increase of bribery, of which there is such loud complaint, and the spread of the contagion to places that were formerly free of it, bear witness that local influences no longer have the upper hand; that the electors now vote to please themselves, and not other people. No doubt a great deal of servile dependence still remains in the counties and in the smaller boroughs; but the spirit of the age is averse to it, and the force of events is constantly working to diminish it. A good tenant can now feel that he is as valuable to his landlord as his landlord is to him; a prosperous tradesman can afford to feel independent of any single customer. At every election the votes are more and more the voters’ own. It is their minds, far more than their personal circumstances, that now need emancipation. They are no longer passive instruments of other men’s will—mere organs for placing power in the hands of a controlling oligarchy. The electors themselves are becoming the oligarchy. “Exactly in proportion as the voter’s vote is determined by his own will and not by that of another who is his master, his position resembles that of a member of Parliament, and publicity is indispensable. So long as any part of the community is unrepresented, the Chartists’ argument against the secret ballot in combination with a restricted franchise is unanswerable. The present electors, and the bulk of those whom any probable Reform Bill would add to their number, belong to the middle class, and have as much a class interest distinct from that of the working classes as the landlords or the great manufacturers. Were the franchise extended to all skilled workers, even these would still have, or might still have, a class interest distinct from that of the unskilled. Suppose it extended to all men—suppose that what was formerly called by the misapplied name of universal suffrage, and is now called by the silly title of manhood suffrage, became law; the voters would still have a class interest as distinguished from women. Suppose there were before the legislature a question specially affecting women—for instance, whether women should be permitted to take academic degrees at universities; whether the mild penalties imposed on brutes who daily beat their wives almost to the gates of death should be replaced by something more effective; or suppose someone were to propose in the British Parliament what one state after another in America is establishing, not by a mere law, but by a provision of its revised constitution: that married women should have a right to their own property—are not a man’s wife and daughters then entitled to know whether he votes for or against a candidate who will support these proposals? “It will of course be objected that these arguments derive all their weight from the assumption of an unjust state of the franchise: that if the opinion of the non-electors is likely to make the elector vote more honestly or more beneficially than he would vote if left to himself, they are fitter to be electors than he is, and ought to have the franchise; that whoever is fit to influence electors is also fit to be an elector; that those to whom the electors are to be responsible should themselves be electors and, as such, should have the protection of the secret ballot, to shield them from the undue influence of powerful individuals or classes to whom they ought not to be responsible. “This argument is seductive, and I once thought it conclusive. Now it seems to me fallacious. Not all who are fit to influence electors are for that reason also fit to be electors themselves. The latter is a much greater power than the former, and there may be people ripe for the lesser political function to whom the higher could not yet be entrusted without danger. The opinions and wishes of the poorest and crudest class of workers may be very useful as one influence among others on the minds of the electors, as well as on that of the legislature, and yet it might be highly harmful to give them the preponderant influence by admitting them, in their present state of morality and intelligence, to the full exercise of the franchise. It is precisely this indirect influence of those who do not have the franchise over those who do which, by its progressive growth, eases the transition to every new extension of the franchise, and is the means by which, when the time is ripe, the extension is peacefully brought about. But there is yet another and still deeper consideration, which should never be left out of account in political reflections. The very notion is unfounded that publicity, and the feeling of being accountable to the public, are of no use when the public is not qualified to form a sound judgment. It is a very superficial view of the utility of public opinion to suppose that it does good only when it succeeds in enforcing a servile conformity to itself. To be under the eyes of others—to have to defend oneself before others—is more important to no one than to those who act in opposition to the opinion of others, for it obliges them to have sure ground of their own. Nothing has so steadying an effect as working against pressure. Except under the temporary sway of passionate excitement, no one will do anything for which he expects severe censure unless from a preconceived and fixed purpose of his own, which is always a proof of a thoughtful and deliberate character and, except in thoroughly bad men, generally proceeds from sincere and strong personal convictions. The mere fact of having to give an account of their conduct is a powerful inducement for people to keep to conduct of which at least some tolerably decent account can be given. If anyone thinks that the mere obligation of preserving decency is not a very considerable check on the abuse of power, his attention has never been drawn to the conduct of those who do not feel obliged to exercise that restraint. Publicity is of inestimable value even when it does no more than prevent what cannot possibly be defended in any plausible way—than compel deliberation and oblige everyone to determine, before he acts, what he will say when he is called to account for his actions. “But if not now (it may be said), then at least in the future, when all are fit to have the vote, and when all men and women are admitted to vote by virtue of their fitness, _then_ there can no longer be any danger of class legislation; then the electors, being the nation, can have no interest apart from the general interest: even if individuals still vote from private or class motives, the majority will have no such motive; and since there will then be no non-electors to whom they ought to be responsible, the effect of the secret ballot, which excludes nothing but the sinister influences, will be wholly beneficial. “Here too I do not agree. I cannot believe that the secret ballot would be desirable even if the people were ripe for universal suffrage and had obtained it. In the first place, because it could not be supposed to be necessary under such circumstances. Only imagine the state of things that the supposition presupposes: a universally educated people, and every adult human being in possession of a vote. If even now, when only a small portion are electors and the majority of the population is almost uneducated, public opinion is, as everyone now sees, in the last resort the ruling power, it is a chimera to suppose that over a community in which all read and all have a vote, any power could be exercised by landlords and rich people against its own inclination which it would find it in the least difficult to throw off. But even if the protection of secrecy would then be unnecessary, the check of publicity would be as necessary as ever. The universal observation of mankind must have been very much mistaken if the mere fact of being a member of the community, and of not standing in a pronounced conflict of interest with the public, were enough to ensure the performance of a public duty without the spur or the curb that comes from the opinion of our fellow men. A man’s particular share in the public interest is, as a rule, not enough, even when no private interest draws him in the opposite direction, to make him do his duty to the public without other external motives. Nor can it be admitted that all, even if they had the vote, would cast their votes as honestly in secret as in public. “The proposition that the electors, when they make up the whole community, can have no interest in voting against the interest of the community will, on closer examination, prove to be a proposition with more sound than sense in it. Although the community as a whole (as the terms imply) can have no other interest than its collective interest, any or every individual in it may have another. A man’s interest consists of whatever he takes an interest _in_. Everyone has as many different interests as he has feelings; likings or dislikings, whether of a selfish or a better kind. None of these, taken by itself, can be said to constitute ‘his interest’: he is a good man or a bad one according as he prefers the one class of his interests or the other. A man who is a tyrant at home will readily sympathize with tyranny (when it is not exercised over himself); he will almost certainly not sympathize with resistance to tyranny. An envious man will vote against Aristides because he is called the Just. A selfish man will prefer even a trifling personal advantage to his share of the benefit his country would derive from a good law, because the interests peculiar to himself are those on which the habits of his mind incline him to dwell and which they best enable him to estimate. A great number of the electors will have two sets of preferences—those from private and those from public motives. The latter are the only ones the elector would willingly avow. The best side of their character is the one people are anxious to show, even to those who are no better than themselves. People will cast dishonest or mean votes from love of gain, from malice, from wounded vanity, from personal rivalry, even from the interests or prejudices of their class or sect, more readily in secret than in public. And there are cases—they may become more frequent—in which almost the only check on a majority of knaves consists in their involuntary respect for the opinion of an honest minority. In a case like that of the repudiating states of North America, is there not some restraint on the unprincipled voter in the shame of looking an honest man in the face? Since all this good would be sacrificed by the secret ballot, even under the circumstances most favourable to it, far stronger reasons for its necessity than can now be adduced (and these reasons are constantly growing weaker) would be needed to make its introduction desirable.” [4] On the other disputed points connected with the mode of voting, there is no need to spend so many words. The system of personal representation, as Mr. Hare has worked it out, makes the use of voting papers necessary. But it seems to me indispensable that the voter’s signature be affixed to the paper at a public polling place or, if no such place is conveniently within reach, at some office open to all the world, and in the presence of a responsible public officer. The proposal that has been put forward, that the voting papers be filled up at the voter’s own home and sent by post or collected by a public officer, I should regard as fatal. The act would be performed in the absence of the salutary influences and in the presence of all the pernicious ones.
