For, after all, the riddle of where the anarchists had got all these followers was still unsolved. The presence of a single man had been enough for them; they had fled at the first sight of the Secretary’s distorted smile. Syme glanced back over his shoulder every now and then at the army that was on their heels. As the wood first thinned and then shrank with distance, he could see the sunlit slopes beyond and above it; and across these the square black crowd was still moving like one monstrous beetle. In the very strong sunlight, and with his own very keen eyes, which were almost telescopic, Syme could make out this mass of men quite distinctly. He could see them as separate human figures; but he was more and more surprised by the way they moved, as if they were a single man. They seemed to be dressed in dark clothes and to wear plain hats, like any ordinary crowd off the street; but they did not spread out, did not scatter, and did not advance to the attack in different lines, as would have been natural for an ordinary mob. They moved with a sort of terrible and malignant woodenness, like a staring army of automata. Syme pointed this out to Ratcliffe. “Yes,” replied the policeman, “that’s discipline. That’s Sunday. He is perhaps five hundred miles away, but the fear of him is on all of them like the finger of God. Yes, they walk regularly; and you can bet your life that they talk regularly too, yes, and think regularly. But the only thing that matters to us is that they disappear regularly.” Syme nodded. It was true that the black patch of pursuers was growing smaller and smaller as the peasant belaboured his horse. The expanse of sunlit country, though level on the whole, fell away on the other side of the wood in billows of heavy slope toward the sea, not unlike the lower slopes of the Sussex Downs. The only difference was that in Sussex the road would have been broken and angular like a little brook, whereas here the white French road plunged down steeply in front of them like a waterfall. Down this straight descent the cart rattled at a considerable tilt, and after a few minutes, as the road grew steeper still, they saw below them the little harbour of Lancy and a great blue arc of the sea. The wandering cloud of their enemies had vanished entirely from the horizon. Horse and cart swung sharply round a clump of elms, and the horse’s nose nearly struck the face of an old gentleman who was sitting on the benches outside the little café “Le Soleil d’Or.” The peasant grunted an apology and climbed down from his seat. The others also got down one after another and addressed fragmentary civilities to the old gentleman, for it was quite evident from his genial manner that he was the owner of the little tavern. He was a white-haired old fellow with apple cheeks, sleepy eyes, and a grey moustache; stout, sedentary, and very innocent, of a type often met with in France but still more common in Catholic Germany. Everything about him, his pipe, his beer mug, his flowers, and his beehive, suggested an ancestral peace; only when his visitors looked up on entering the inn parlour did they see the sword on the wall. The Colonel, who greeted the landlord like an old friend, went quickly into the parlour, sat down, and ordered a ritual refreshment. The military decisiveness of his conduct aroused the interest of Syme, who sat beside him, and when the old landlord had gone out, he took the opportunity to satisfy his curiosity. “May I ask you, Colonel,” he said in a low voice, “why we have come here?” Colonel Ducroix smiled behind his bristly white moustache. “For two reasons, sir,” he said; “and I will give first not the most important but the most useful. We have come here because this is the only place within twenty miles where we can get horses.” “Horses!” repeated Syme, looking up quickly. “Yes,” replied the other; “if you and your people really want to shake off your enemies, it is horses or nothing for you, unless of course you have bicycles and motor-cars in your pocket.” “And where do you think we should make for?” asked Syme doubtfully. “Without question,” replied the Colonel, “you had best get as quickly as possible to the police station beyond the town. My friend, whom I seconded under somewhat deceptive circumstances, seems to me to exaggerate very greatly the possibility of a general rising; but even he would hardly maintain, I imagine, that you would not be safe with the gendarmes.” Syme nodded gravely; then he said abruptly— “And your other reason for coming here?” “My other reason for coming here,” said Ducroix soberly, “is that it can do no harm to see one or two good people when one may be near to death.” Syme looked up at the wall and saw a crudely painted and touching religious picture. Then he said— “You are right,” and then almost immediately afterwards: “Has anyone seen to the horses?” “Yes,” answered Ducroix, “you may be quite sure that I gave orders the moment I came in. Those enemies of yours gave no impression of hurry, but in fact they were moving astonishingly fast, like a well-drilled army. I had no idea that the anarchists had so much discipline. You have not a moment to lose.” Almost as he spoke, the old landlord with the blue eyes and the white hair came ambling unhurriedly into the room and announced that six horses stood saddled outside. On Ducroix’s advice the five others provided themselves with food and wine in some portable form, and, keeping their duelling swords as the only weapons available, they clattered away down the steep white road. The two servants who had carried the Marquis’s luggage when he was still a Marquis were left behind by general consent to drink at the café, and by no means against their own inclination. By this time the afternoon sun was sloping westward, and in its rays Syme could see the sturdy figure of the old landlord growing smaller and smaller, yet still standing there and gazing after them in complete silence, the sunshine in his silver hair. Syme had a fixed, superstitious fancy, left in his mind by the Colonel’s chance remark, that this might indeed be the last honest stranger he would ever see on earth. He was still looking at that dwindling figure, which now stood only as a grey blot touched with a white flame against the great green wall of the steep hill behind him. And as he stared over the crest of the hill behind the landlord, there appeared an army of black-clad, marching men. They seemed to hang over the good man and his house like a black cloud of locusts. THE EARTH IN ANARCHY The riders urged their horses to a gallop, heedless of the rather rough descent of the road, soon regained their lead over the marching men, and at last the mass of the first buildings of Lancy hid their pursuers from view. Even so, the ride had been a long one, and by the time they reached the town proper the west was already warming with the colour and nature of sunset. The Colonel proposed that, before they made finally for the police station, they should try in passing to win over one more person who might be useful. “Four out of the five rich men in this town,” he said, “are common swindlers. I suppose the proportion is pretty much the same all over the world. The fifth is a friend of mine and a very fine fellow; and, what is even more important from our point of view, he owns a motor-car.” “I am afraid,” said the Professor in his cheerful way, looking back along the white road on which the black, crawling patch might appear at any moment, “I am afraid we have hardly time for afternoon calls.” “Doctor Renard’s house is only three minutes away,” said the Colonel. “Our danger,” said Dr. Bull, “is not two minutes away.” “Yes,” said Syme, “if we ride on fast, we are bound to leave them behind, for they are on foot.” “He has a motor-car,” said the Colonel. “But we may not get it,” said Bull. “Oh yes, he is entirely on your side.” “But he might be out.” “Be quiet,” said Syme suddenly. “What is that noise?” For a second they all sat as motionless as equestrian statues, and for a second—for two or three or four seconds—heaven and earth seemed equally still. Then all their ears, in an agony of attention, heard along the road that indescribable thrill and throb that means only one thing—horses! The Colonel’s face changed in an instant, as if lightning had struck it and yet left it unscathed. “They have tricked us,” he said with curt military irony. “Prepare to receive cavalry!” “Where can they have got the horses?” asked Syme, as he mechanically urged his steed into a canter. The Colonel was silent for a little while, then he said in a strained voice— “I was speaking with strict accuracy when I said that the ‘Soleil d’Or’ was the only place where one can get horses within twenty miles.” “No!” said Syme violently, “I don’t believe he would do it. Not with all that white hair.” “He may have been forced,” said the Colonel gently. “They must be at least a hundred strong, and that is why we are all going now to see my friend Renard, who has a motor-car.” With these words he suddenly swung his horse round a street corner and went down the street at such a thundering pace that the others, though already at full gallop, had difficulty keeping up with the flying tail of his horse. Dr. Renard lived in a tall and comfortable house at the top of a steep street, so that when the riders dismounted at his door they could once more see the great green ridge of the hill, with the white road across it, rising above all the roofs of the town. They breathed again when they saw that the road was as yet clear, and rang the bell. Dr. Renard was a beaming, brown-bearded man, a good example of that silent but very busy professional class which France has preserved even more perfectly than England. When the matter was explained to him, he promptly dismissed the former Marquis’s panic as nonsense; he said, with solid French scepticism, that there was not the slightest conceivable likelihood of a general anarchist rising. “Anarchy,” he said, shrugging his shoulders, “that is childishness!” “_Et ça_,” cried the Colonel suddenly, pointing over the other’s shoulder, “and that is childishness, is it?” They all looked round and saw a curve of black cavalry come sweeping over the crest of the hill with all the force of Attila. Yet fast as they rode, the whole line kept well together, and they could see the black visors of the first rank as even as a row of uniforms. But although the main black square was the same, if moving faster, there was now one sensational difference, which they could see plainly on the slope of the hill as on a tilted map. The mass of the riders formed a single block; but one rider flew far ahead of the column, driving his horse faster and faster with frantic movements of hand and heel, so that one might have thought he was not the pursuer but the pursued. Yet even at that great distance they could see something in his figure so fanatical, so unmistakable, that they knew it was the Secretary himself. “I am sorry to cut short a cultivated discussion,” said the Colonel, “but can you lend me your motor-car, now, in two minutes?” “I have a suspicion that you are all mad,” said Dr. Renard with a sociable smile; “but God forbid that madness should in any way interfere with friendship. Let us go over to the garage.” Dr. Renard was a mild man of enormous wealth; his rooms were like the Musée de Cluny, and he owned three motor-cars. These, however, he seemed to use only very sparingly, having the simple tastes of the French middle class, and when his impatient friends came to examine them, it was some time before they had assured themselves that even one of them could be got going. This one they got out, with some difficulty, into the street in front of the Doctor’s house. When they came out of the dim garage they were dismayed to find that dusk had already fallen with the suddenness of night in the tropics. Either they had been in there longer than they thought, or some unusual canopy of cloud had gathered over the town. They looked down the steep streets and thought they saw a slight mist coming up from the sea. “Now or never,” said Dr. Bull. “I hear horses.” “No,” corrected the Professor, “a horse.” And as they listened, it became clear that the sound coming rapidly nearer on the clattering stones was not the sound of the whole cavalcade but that of the one rider who had left it far behind—the mad Secretary. Syme’s family, like most of those who end up in the simple life, had once owned a motor-car, and he knew all about these things. He had at once leapt into the driver’s seat and, flushed in the face, was tugging and wrenching at the rusty machinery. He put his whole strength against a crank and then said quite calmly— “I am afraid it won’t go.” As he spoke, a man came sweeping round the corner, rigid on his rushing horse, with the impetus and the rigidity of an arrow. He wore a smile that thrust out his chin as if it were dislocated. He galloped up alongside the stationary car, into which its occupants had crowded, and laid his hand on its front. It was the Secretary, and his mouth went quite straight in the solemnity of triumph. Syme braced himself hard against the steering wheel, and there was no sound but the rumble of the other pursuers riding into the town. Then, quite suddenly, there came a screech of scraping iron, and the car leapt forward. It plucked the Secretary clean out of his saddle, as one plucks a knife out of its sheath, dragged him, kicking horribly, twenty yards along, and left him lying flung flat on the road far in front of his frightened horse.
