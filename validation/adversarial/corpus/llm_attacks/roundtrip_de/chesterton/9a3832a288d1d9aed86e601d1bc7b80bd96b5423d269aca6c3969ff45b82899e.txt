I see to do is, in fact, to make use of precisely the things that tell in the Marquis’s favour. I shall take advantage of the fact that he is a highly respected nobleman. I shall take advantage of the fact that he has many friends and moves in the best society.” “What the devil are you talking about?” asked the Professor. “The Symes are first mentioned in the fourteenth century,” said Syme; “but there is a tradition that one of them rode behind Bruce at Bannockburn. Since 1350 the family tree has been entirely unbroken.” “He has lost his mind,” said the little doctor, staring at him. “Our arms,” Syme went on calmly, “are ‘argent, a chevron gules charged with three crosses crosslet of the field.’ The motto varies.” The Professor seized Syme roughly by the waistcoat. “We are nearly ashore,” he said. “Are you seasick, or are you making jokes in the wrong place?” “My remarks are almost painfully practical,” answered Syme, without any hurry. “The house of St. Eustache is also very old. The Marquis cannot deny that he is a gentleman. He cannot deny that I am a gentleman. And in order to put the question of my social standing beyond all doubt, I intend to knock his hat off at the first opportunity. But here we are in the harbour.” They went ashore under the strong sun as if dazed. Syme, who had now taken the lead as Bull had taken it in London, led them along a sort of seafront promenade until he came to some cafés that lay embedded in lush greenery and looked out over the sea. As he walked ahead of them his step was a little swaggering, and he swung his stick like a sword. He was evidently making for the far end of the row of cafés, but he suddenly stopped. With a sharp gesture he commanded them to be silent, but pointed with one gloved finger at a café table under a bank of flowering foliage, at which sat the Marquis de St. Eustache, his teeth flashing in his thick black beard, his bold brown face shaded by a light yellow straw hat and outlined against the violet sea. CHAPTER X. THE DUEL Syme sat down with his companions at a café table, his blue eyes sparkling like the bright sea below them, and ordered a bottle of Saumur with cheerful impatience. For some reason he was in a state of curious exuberance. His spirits were already unnaturally high; they rose as the Saumur sank, and after half an hour his talk was a torrent of nonsense. He professed to be drawing up a plan of the conversation that was to unfold between himself and the deadly Marquis. He scribbled it down wildly with a pencil. It was arranged like a printed catechism, with questions and answers, and was delivered with extraordinary glibness. “I shall approach. Before I take off his hat, I shall take off my own. I shall say, ‘The Marquis de Saint Eustache, if I am not mistaken.’ He will say, ‘The celebrated Mr. Syme, I presume.’ He will say in the most exquisite French, ‘How are you?’ I shall reply in the most exquisite Cockney, ‘Oh, just the Syme—’” “Oh, shut up,” said the man in spectacles. “Pull yourself together and throw that scrap of paper away. What do you really mean to do?” “But it was a beautiful catechism,” said Syme plaintively. “Do let me read it to you. It has only forty-three questions and answers, and some of the Marquis’s answers are wonderfully witty. I like to do justice to my enemy.” “But what is the good of it all?” asked Dr. Bull in exasperation. “It leads up to my challenge, don’t you see,” said Syme, beaming. “When the Marquis has given the thirty-ninth answer, which runs—” “Has it by any chance occurred to you,” asked the Professor with ponderous simplicity, “that the Marquis may possibly not say all the forty-three things you have put into his mouth? In that case, as I understand it, your own epigrams might seem somewhat more forced.” Syme struck the table, his face radiant. “Why, how true that is,” he said, “and I never thought of it. Sir, you have an intellect beyond the common. You will make a name for yourself.” “Oh, you’re as drunk as a lord!” said the Doctor. “It only remains,” Syme went on, entirely unperturbed, “to choose some other method of breaking the ice (if I may so express it) between myself and the man I mean to kill. And since the course of a dialogue cannot be predicted by one of its parties alone (as you have pointed out with such profound acuteness), there remains, I suppose, only one thing to do: that the one party should, as far as is humanly possible, carry on the whole dialogue by himself. And that is exactly what I shall do, by Jove!” And he stood up suddenly, and his yellow hair blew in the light sea breeze. In a _café chantant_ hidden somewhere among the trees a band was playing, and a woman had just stopped singing. To Syme’s heated head the blare of the brass band sounded like the clatter and jingle of that barrel organ in Leicester Square to whose tune he had once stood up to die. He looked across at the little table where the Marquis sat. The man now had two companions, solemn Frenchmen in frock coats and top hats, one of them wearing the red rosette of the Legion of Honour, evidently people of solid social standing. Beside these black, cylindrical garments the Marquis, in his loose straw hat and light spring clothes, looked Bohemian, even barbaric; but he looked like the Marquis. Indeed, one might have said that he looked like the king, with his animal elegance, his scornful eyes, and his proud head lifted against the purple sea. But at any rate he was no Christian king; he was rather some swarthy despot, half Greek, half Asiatic, who in the days when slavery seemed natural looked down on the Mediterranean, on his galley and his groaning slaves. Just so, thought Syme, might the brown-gold face of such a tyrant have stood out against the dark green olives and the burning blue. “Are you going to address the meeting?” asked the Professor irritably, seeing that Syme still stood there motionless. Syme drained his last glass of sparkling wine. “I certainly am,” he said, pointing across at the Marquis and his companions, “that meeting over there. I do not like that meeting. I am going to pull that meeting by its great ugly mahogany-coloured nose.” He walked over quickly, if not altogether steadily. When the Marquis saw him he arched his black Assyrian eyebrows in surprise, but smiled politely. “You are Mr. Syme, I believe,” he said. Syme bowed. “And you are the Marquis de Saint Eustache,” he said gracefully. “Allow me to pull your nose.” He leaned forward to do so, but the Marquis started back, knocking over his chair, and the two men in top hats held Syme back by the shoulders. “This man has insulted me!” said Syme, with explanatory gestures. “Insulted you?” cried the gentleman with the red rosette. “When?” “Oh, just now,” said Syme recklessly. “He insulted my mother.” “Insulted your mother!” exclaimed the gentleman incredulously. “Well, anyhow,” said Syme, conceding a point, “my aunt.” “But how can the Marquis have insulted your aunt just now?” said the second gentleman, with some legitimate wonder. “He has been sitting here the whole time.” “Ah, it was what he said!” said Syme darkly. “I said nothing at all,” said the Marquis, “except something about the band. I only said that I like Wagner when it is played well.” “That was an allusion to my family,” said Syme firmly. “My aunt played Wagner badly. It was a sore subject. We are constantly being insulted about it.” “That seems to me most extraordinary,” said the gentleman who was _décoré_, looking doubtfully at the Marquis. “Oh, I assure you,” said Syme earnestly, “your whole conversation was positively riddled with sinister allusions to my aunt’s weaknesses.” “This is nonsense!” said the second gentleman. “I, for one, have said nothing for half an hour except that I like the singing of that girl with the black hair.” “Well, there you are again!” said Syme indignantly. “My aunt’s was red.” “It seems to me,” said the other, “that you are simply looking for a pretext to insult the Marquis.” “By Jove!” said Syme, turning round and looking at him, “what a clever fellow you are!” The Marquis sprang up, his eyes blazing like a tiger’s. “Looking for a quarrel with me!” he cried. “Looking for a fight with me! By God! No man ever had to look long for that. These gentlemen will perhaps act as my seconds. We still have four hours of daylight. Let us fight this evening.” Syme bowed with quite wonderful grace. “Marquis,” he said, “your conduct is worthy of your fame and your blood. Allow me a moment to consult the gentlemen into whose hands I shall place myself.” In three long strides he was back with his companions, and they, who had watched his champagne-inspired attack and heard his idiotic explanations, were rather startled by his look. For now, as he came back to them, he was entirely sober, a little pale, and he spoke in a low voice and with passionate practicality. “I’ve done it,” he said hoarsely. “I’ve forced a duel on the brute. But listen, and listen carefully. There is no time for talk. You are my seconds, and everything must come from you. You must now insist, and insist absolutely, that the duel take place tomorrow after seven o’clock, so that I may have the chance of preventing him from catching the 7.45 train to Paris. If he misses that, he misses his crime. He cannot refuse to oblige you on so trifling a question of time and place. But this is what he will do. He will choose a field somewhere near a little station on the line, where he can still catch the train. He is a very good swordsman, and he will trust to killing me in time to catch it. But I can fence well too, and I think I can at any rate keep him in play until the train is gone. Then perhaps he may kill me to console himself for his disappointment. You understand? Very well, then let me introduce you to some charming friends of mine,” and, leading them quickly across the promenade, he introduced them to the Marquis’s seconds under two very aristocratic names of which they had never heard until then. Syme was subject to fits of a peculiar common sense that was not otherwise part of his character. They were (as he said of his inspiration about the spectacles) poetic intuitions, and they sometimes rose to the sublimity of prophecy. In this case he had correctly calculated his opponent’s course of action. When the Marquis learned from his seconds that Syme could fight only in the morning, it must have become fully clear to him that an obstacle had suddenly risen up between him and his bomb-throwing business in the capital. Naturally he could not explain this objection to his friends, so he took the course that Syme had predicted. He induced his seconds to settle on a small meadow not far from the railway, and he trusted to the deadly effect of the first bout. When he came down very calmly to the field of honour, no one could have guessed that he was anxious about a journey; his hands were in his pockets, his straw hat was pushed to the back of his head, and his handsome face shone like bronze in the sun. Yet a stranger might have thought it odd that there appeared in his train not only his seconds carrying the sword case, but also two of his servants carrying a portmanteau and a breakfast hamper. Early as the hour was, the sun steeped everything in warmth, and Syme was vaguely surprised to see so many spring flowers shining gold and silver in the tall grass, in which the whole company stood almost knee-deep. With the exception of the Marquis, all the men wore sombre and solemn morning coats, with hats like black chimney pots; the little doctor in particular, with his black spectacles on top of it all, looked like an undertaker in a farce. Syme could not help feeling a comic contrast between this funereal Sunday best and the rich, glittering meadow, where wild flowers grew everywhere. But in truth this comic contrast between the yellow flowers and the black hats was only a symbol of the tragic contrast between the yellow flowers and the black business. On his right lay a little copse; far to his left lay the long curve of the railway line, which he was, so to speak, guarding from the Marquis, for whom it was at once goal and means of escape. Before him, beyond the black group of his opponents, he saw, like a tinted cloud, a little flowering almond bush against the pale line of the sea. The member of the Legion of Honour, whose name, it appeared, was Colonel Ducroix, approached the Professor and Dr. Bull with great courtesy and proposed that the encounter should end with the first wound of any consequence. Dr. Bull, however, whom Syme had carefully coached on this tactical point, insisted with great dignity and in very bad French that it should go on until one of the combatants was disabled. Syme had come to the conclusion that he could avoid disabling the Marquis for at least twenty minutes, and at the same time prevent the Marquis from disabling him. In twenty minutes the train to Paris would have gone by. “To a man of the well-known skill and valour of Monsieur de St. Eustache,” said the Professor solemnly, “it must be a matter of indifference which method is chosen, and our principal has weighty reasons for requiring the longer encounter, reasons whose delicacy forbids me to be more explicit, but for the just and honourable nature of which I—” “_Peste!_” burst out the Marquis behind him, whose face had suddenly darkened, “let us stop talking and begin,” and he struck the head off a tall flower with his stick. Syme understood his rude impatience and instinctively glanced over his shoulder to see whether the train was yet in sight. But there was no smoke on the horizon. Colonel Ducroix knelt down, unlocked the case, and took out a pair of matching swords, which caught the sunlight and turned into two streaks of white fire. He offered one to the Marquis, who snatched it without ceremony, and the other to Syme, who took it, bent it, and weighed it with as much delay as was compatible with dignity. Then the Colonel took out another pair of blades, kept one himself, gave the other to Dr. Bull, and proceeded to place the men.
