The only thing I can think of doing, as a matter of fact, is to make use of the very things that are in the Marquis's favor. I'm going to use the fact that he is a highly respected nobleman. I'm going to use the fact that he has a great many friends and moves in the very highest society." "What on earth are you talking about?" asked the Professor. "The Symes first appear in the records in the fourteenth century," said Syme. "But there is also a tradition that one of the family rode behind Bruce at Bannockburn. From 1350 on, the line is perfectly clear." "He's gone mad," said the little Doctor, his eyes wide. "Our arms," Syme went on with perfect composure, "are 'argent, a chevron gules, charged with three cross crosslets of the field.' The motto varies." The Professor seized Syme roughly by the waistcoat. "We'll be ashore any minute," he said. "Are you seasick, or are you cracking jokes with no sense of time or place?" "What I'm saying is almost painfully practical," Syme answered in an unhurried tone. "The house of St. Eustache is also a very old one. The Marquis cannot deny that he is a gentleman. Nor can he deny that I am one. And in order to put the question of my social standing beyond any doubt whatever, I intend, at the first opportunity, to knock his hat off. Ah, here we are in the harbor." They went ashore under the strong sunlight in a kind of daze. Just as Bull had taken the lead in London, Syme now took the lead, guiding the party along what seemed to be a seaside promenade until they came to a row of cafés, wrapped in thick greenery and looking down upon the sea. As he went ahead of them, his walk had something of a swagger in it, and he swung his walking stick like a sword. He was apparently making for the farthest end of the row of cafés, but he stopped abruptly. With a sharp gesture he signaled them all to be silent, and, raising one gloved finger, he pointed to a café table beneath a bank of flowering shrubs. There sat the Marquis de St. Eustache, his teeth gleaming in his thick black beard, his bold brown face shaded by a light, pale yellow straw hat, its outline standing out sharply against the violet sea.

Chapter X: The Duel

Syme sat down at a café table with his companions and, his blue eyes sparkling like the shining sea below, ordered a bottle of Saumur, happily and impatiently. He was, for some reason, in a strangely giddy state. His spirits were already unnaturally high. As the Saumur went down they rose higher still, and within half an hour his talk had become a torrent of nonsense. He claimed to be drawing up the plan of the conversation that was to take place between himself and that dreadful Marquis. He scrawled it down wildly in pencil. It was laid out in questions and answers like a printed catechism, and he rattled it off at an astonishing speed. "I approach. Before taking off his hat, I take off my own. I say, 'The Marquis de St. Eustache, I believe.' He says, 'The celebrated Mr. Syme, I presume.' He says in the most elegant French, 'How are you?' I reply in the most splendid Cockney, 'Oh, just the same old Syme—'" "Oh, stop it," said the man in spectacles. "Pull yourself together and throw away that scrap of paper. What do you really mean to do?" "But it was a lovely catechism," said Syme plaintively. "Do let me read it out. There are only forty-three questions and answers, and some of the Marquis's replies are wonderfully witty. I like to be fair to my enemy." "But what good would all that do?" asked Dr. Bull indignantly. "It leads up to my challenge, don't you see?" said Syme, beaming. "When the Marquis gives his thirty-ninth answer, which goes—" "Has it by any chance occurred to you," asked the Professor with grave simplicity, "that the Marquis may not say all forty-three of the lines you have written down for him? In that case, as I understand it, your own epigrams may look a little forced." Syme struck the table, his face alight. "Why, that's perfectly true," he said. "I never thought of that. Sir, you have an extraordinary intellect. You'll make a name for yourself, I'm sure of it." "Oh, you're blind drunk!" said the Doctor. "There remains only one course," Syme went on, not in the least put out, "and that is to adopt some other method of breaking the ice (if I may put it that way) between myself and the man I want to kill. And since the course of a dialogue cannot be predicted by only one of the parties to it (as you have pointed out with truly profound insight), the only thing to be done, I suppose, is for one party, as far as he possibly can, to carry on the whole dialogue by himself. And that is exactly what I'll do, I swear it!" And he suddenly stood up, his yellow hair stirring in the faint sea breeze. In a _café chantant_ hidden somewhere among the trees a band was playing, and a woman had just finished singing. To Syme's heated head, the blare of the brass band sounded like the jangling of that barrel organ in Leicester Square, to whose tune he had once stood up ready to face death. He looked over toward the little table where the Marquis was sitting. The man now had two companions with him, solemn Frenchmen in frock coats and silk hats, one of whom wore the red ribbon of the Legion of Honor; they were clearly people of solid social standing. Beside these black, cylindrical costumes, the Marquis, in his loose straw hat and light spring clothes, looked Bohemian, even barbaric. But he looked like a marquis. Indeed, with his animal grace, his scornful eyes, and his proud head lifted high against the purple sea, one might have said that he looked like a king. But whatever he was, he was not a Christian king. He was more like some swarthy despot, half Greek and half Asiatic, who in the days when slavery was taken for granted had looked down upon the Mediterranean, upon his galleys and his groaning slaves. Just so, Syme thought, the brownish-gold face of such a tyrant must have stood out against the dark green of the olives and the burning blue. "Are you planning to make a speech to the meeting?" asked the Professor peevishly, seeing that Syme was still standing there motionless. Syme drained his last glass of sparkling wine. "I am," he said, pointing to the Marquis and his companions. "To that meeting there. I don't like that meeting. I'm going to pull its great ugly mahogany-colored nose." He walked quickly across, though not altogether steadily. The Marquis, seeing him, raised his black Assyrian eyebrows in surprise, but smiled politely. "Mr. Syme, I believe," he said. Syme bowed. "And you are the Marquis de St. Eustache," he said gracefully. "Allow me to pull your nose." He leaned forward to do so, but the Marquis sprang back, overturning his chair, and the two men in silk hats seized Syme by the shoulders and held him back. "This man has insulted me!" said Syme, with an explanatory gesture. "Insulted you?" cried the gentleman with the red ribbon. "When?" "Oh, just now," said Syme recklessly. "He insulted my mother." "Insulted your mother!" exclaimed the gentleman incredulously. "Well, anyhow," said Syme, conceding a point, "my aunt." "But how could the Marquis have insulted your aunt just now?" said the other gentleman, in understandable astonishment. "He has been sitting here the whole time." "Ah, it was what he said!" said Syme darkly. "I said nothing at all," said the Marquis. "I only said something about the band. All I said was that I like Wagner when it is played well." "That is a dig at my family," said Syme firmly. "My aunt played Wagner badly. It was a sore subject. We are always being insulted about it." "This is a most extraordinary business," said the _décoré_ gentleman, looking doubtfully at the Marquis. "Oh, it's true, I assure you," said Syme earnestly. "Your whole conversation was packed with malicious allusions to my aunt's weaknesses." "Nonsense!" said the second gentleman. "I, for one, have said nothing for the last half hour except that I liked the singing of that dark-haired girl." "There, you've done it again!" said Syme indignantly. "My aunt's hair was red." "It seems to me," said the other, "that you are simply looking for an excuse to insult the Marquis." "Good heavens!" said Syme, turning around to look at him. "What a sharp fellow you are!" The Marquis sprang to his feet, his eyes blazing like a tiger's. "So you want to pick a quarrel with me!" he cried. "You want to challenge me to a duel! By God, no man who ever wanted a fight with me had to look for me long. These gentlemen will perhaps be good enough to act as my seconds. There are still four hours of daylight left. Let us fight this evening." Syme bowed with truly splendid grace. "Marquis," he said, "your conduct is worthy of your fame and your blood. Allow me a moment to consult with the gentlemen in whose hands I shall place myself." In three long strides he was back with his companions. They had watched his champagne-fueled attack and listened to his idiotic explanations, and now they were thoroughly astonished at the look of him. For the man who came back to them was perfectly sober, a little pale, and spoke in a low voice that was passionately practical. "I've done it," he said hoarsely. "I've forced a fight on the brute. But listen, and listen carefully. There's no time for talk. You are my seconds, and everything has to come from you. If he misses that, he misses his crime. He cannot refuse you on such a small point of time and place. But this is what he will do. He will choose a field somewhere near a little station along the line, so that he can catch the train there. He is a tremendous swordsman, and he'll count on being able to kill me in time to make the train. But I fence well too, and I think I can keep him in play at least until the train has gone. After that he may kill me out of spite, for all I know. Do you understand? Good. Then let me introduce you to my charming friends." With that, he quickly led the party across the promenade and introduced them to the Marquis's seconds under two very aristocratic names that they had never heard before. Syme was subject to odd fits of common sense that did not fit his ordinary character. They were (as he himself had called his impulse about the spectacles) poetic intuitions, and sometimes they rose to the height of prophecy. In this case he had read his opponent's moves exactly. When the Marquis was told by his seconds that Syme could fight only in the morning, he must have fully realized that an obstacle had suddenly sprung up between himself and his bomb-throwing business in the capital. Naturally he could not explain this difficulty to his friends, so he chose exactly the course Syme had predicted. He persuaded his seconds to settle on a small meadow not far from the railway, and he staked everything on the first engagement deciding the matter. When he came down to the field of honor with perfect composure, no one would have dreamed that he had a journey on his mind. His hands were thrust into his pockets, his straw hat was tilted back on his head, and his handsome face shone brazenly in the sunlight. But a stranger might have found it odd to see in his train not only his seconds carrying the sword case but also two servants carrying a traveling bag and a lunch basket. It was still early, but the sun was bathing everything in warmth, and Syme was vaguely surprised to see so many spring flowers burning gold and silver in the tall grass, in which the party stood almost up to their knees. Except for the Marquis, the men were all dressed in gloomy, solemn morning coats and wore black hats like chimney pots; the little Doctor in particular, with the addition of his dark spectacles, looked like an undertaker out of a farce. Syme could not help feeling the comic contrast between this funereal Sunday best and the rich, glittering meadow, with wildflowers blooming everywhere. But in truth, this comic contrast between the yellow flowers and the black hats was only a symbol of the tragic contrast between the yellow flowers and the black deed. On his right was a little wood; far off to his left stretched the long curve of the railway line, which he was, so to speak, guarding from the Marquis, and which was for the Marquis both his destination and his escape. In front, beyond the black group of the enemy, a little almond tree in blossom looked like a colored cloud against the faint line of the sea. The member of the Legion of Honor, whose name, it appeared, was Colonel Ducroix, approached the Professor and Dr. Bull with great courtesy and suggested that the bout might end at the first considerable wound. Dr. Bull, however, who had been carefully coached by Syme on this point of policy, insisted, with great dignity and in very bad French, that it should go on until one of the combatants was disabled. Syme had made up his mind that for at least twenty minutes he could avoid disabling the Marquis and also keep the Marquis from disabling him. In twenty minutes the Paris train would have gone by. "To a man like Monsieur de St. Eustache, whose skill and courage are so widely known," said the Professor solemnly, "it can surely make no difference which method is adopted. And our principal has strong reasons for wanting a longer bout. Those reasons are of so delicate a nature that I cannot state them plainly, but as to their being just and honorable, I can—" "_Peste!_" spat the Marquis from behind him, his face suddenly grown hard. "Stop the chatter and let us begin." And with his stick he slashed the head off a tall flower. Syme knew the reason for his rude impatience, and instinctively looked back over his shoulder to see whether the train was in sight. But there was no smoke on the horizon. Colonel Ducroix knelt down, unlocked the case, and took out a matched pair of swords, which in the sunlight turned into two streaks of white flame. He offered one to the Marquis, who snatched it carelessly. He offered the other to Syme, who took it, flexed it, and poised it, taking as long about it as he could without loss of dignity. Then the Colonel took out another pair of swords, kept one for himself, handed the other to Dr. Bull, and set about placing the two men in position.
