They drove at a good speed, and before long they had the exhilarating sense of drawing clean away from whoever it was that was hunting them. For, after all, the riddle of where the anarchists had gathered so many followers was still unsolved. For them, a single man had been enough; at the first glimpse of the Secretary's twisted smile, they had fled. Every now and then Syme looked back over his shoulder at the army that was following them. As the forest fell away behind, first thinning out and then dwindling, the sunlit slopes came into view beyond it and above it, and across those slopes the square black crowd was still advancing like one enormous beetle. In the very strong sunlight, and with his own very keen, almost telescopic eyes, Syme could see this mass of humanity clearly. He could even make out each of them as a separate human figure. But he was more and more astonished by the way they moved as if they were one man. They seemed to be dressed in dark clothes and plain hats, like any ordinary crowd to be found anywhere in the streets of a city. But they did not scatter, or spread out, or come straggling at their quarry in a number of separate lines, as it would be natural for an ordinary mob to do. They moved with a kind of dreadful, wicked stiffness, like an army of staring automatons. Syme pointed this out to Ratcliffe. "Yes," answered the policeman. "That is what discipline is. That is Sunday. He is perhaps five hundred miles away, and yet the fear of him lies on all of them like the finger of God. Yes, they are walking regularly; and I would bet you anything they are talking regularly, yes, and thinking regularly too. But the only thing that matters to us is that they are disappearing regularly." Syme nodded. It was true that, as the peasant whipped up the horse, the black patch of pursuing men grew smaller and smaller. The sunlit country, though flat as a whole, fell away on the far side of the wood in heavy rolling slopes toward the sea, not unlike the lower slopes of the Sussex Downs. The only difference was that in Sussex the road would have been broken and angular like a brook, whereas here the white French road fell straight down before them like a waterfall. Down this straight descent the cart rattled at a considerable tilt, and in a few minutes, as the road grew steeper still, they saw below them the little harbour of Lancy and a great blue arc of the sea. The travelling cloud of the enemy had vanished entirely from the horizon. As the horse and cart swung sharply round a clump of elms, the horse's nose nearly ran into the face of an old gentleman who was sitting on a bench outside a little café called "Le Soleil d'Or." The peasant grunted an apology and got down from his seat. The others got down one by one as well and offered the old gentleman scraps of polite conversation, for it was clear from his easy, expansive manner that he was the owner of the little tavern. He was a white-haired, apple-faced old man with sleepy eyes and a grey moustache; stout, sedentary, and very innocent, of a type that is often seen in France but is even more common in Catholic Germany. Everything about him, his pipe, his pot of beer, his flowers, and his beehive, suggested an ancestral peace. Only, when his guests happened to look up as they went into the inn parlour, they saw a sword hanging on the wall. The Colonel, who greeted the innkeeper as an old friend, walked straight into the parlour, sat down, and ordered some ceremonial drink. The military decisiveness of this proceeding interested Syme, and, sitting next to him, he took the opportunity of the old innkeeper's going out to satisfy his curiosity. "May I ask, Colonel," he said in a low voice, "why we have come here?" Colonel Ducroix smiled behind his bristling white moustache. "For two reasons," he said. "I will give you first, not the most important, but the most practical. We came here because this is the only place within twenty miles where we can get horses." "Horses!" repeated Syme, looking up quickly. "Yes," replied the other. "If you really mean to get away from your enemies, it is horses or nothing, unless of course you happen to have bicycles or motor cars in your pockets." "Then where do you advise us to make for?" asked Syme doubtfully. "Naturally," replied the Colonel, "the best thing is to make all speed to the police station beyond the town. My friend, whom I served as second under somewhat deceptive circumstances, seems to me to exaggerate very greatly the possibility of a general rising; but even he would hardly maintain that you were not safe with the gendarmes." Syme nodded gravely. Then he said abruptly—"And your other reason for coming here?" "My other reason for coming here," said Ducroix in an even voice, "is that it is no bad thing to see one or two good men when death may be near." Syme looked up at the wall and saw a crudely painted and pathetic religious picture. Then he said—"You are right." And almost immediately afterward, "Has anyone seen about the horses?" "Yes," answered Ducroix. "I gave the orders the moment I came in, so you may be easy on that score. Your enemies did not look as if they were in a hurry, but in fact they were moving astonishingly fast, like a well-trained army. I had no idea the anarchists had so much discipline. You have not a moment to lose." Almost before he had finished speaking, the blue-eyed, white-haired old innkeeper ambled into the room and announced that there were six horses saddled and waiting outside. On Ducroix's advice the other five provided themselves with food and wine in a form they could carry, and, keeping their duelling swords as the only weapons they had, they went clattering down the steep white road. The two servants who had carried the Marquis's luggage when he was still a Marquis were left behind by general agreement to drink at the café, which was not at all against their own inclinations. By this time the afternoon sun was sloping into the west, and in its light Syme could see the sturdy figure of the old innkeeper growing smaller and smaller. Yet the old man still stood there, the sunshine on his silver hair, silently watching them go. In Syme's mind there remained a fixed, superstitious fancy, left there by a chance remark of the Colonel's: that this might perhaps really be the last honest stranger he would ever see upon this earth. He kept on watching the dwindling figure, which looked like nothing more than a grey smudge touched with white flame against the great green wall of the steep hill behind it. And as he gazed over the crest of the hill behind the innkeeper, there appeared upon it an army of men in black, marching. They seemed to hang over that good man and his house like a black cloud of locusts. The horses had been saddled not a moment too soon.

Galloping their horses with no regard for the rather steep descent of the road, the riders soon regained their lead over the men on foot, and at last the cluster of buildings on the outskirts of Lancy cut their pursuers off from view. Still, it was a long ride, and by the time they reached the town proper the west was beginning to warm with the colour and quality of sunset. Before finally making for the police station, the Colonel suggested that they should, in passing, enlist one more person who might be of use. "Four out of the five rich men in this town," he said, "are common swindlers. I suppose the proportion is about the same all over the world. The fifth is a friend of mine, and a very fine fellow; and, what is even more important from our point of view, he owns a motor car." "I am afraid," said the Professor in his usual cheerful way, looking back along the white road on which the black crawling patch might appear at any moment, "I am afraid we have hardly time for afternoon calls." "Doctor Renard's house is only three minutes away," said the Colonel. "Our danger," said Dr. Bull, "is not two minutes away." "Yes," said Syme, "if we ride fast we ought to be able to leave them behind. They are on foot." "He has a motor car," said the Colonel. "But he may not lend it to us," said Bull. "No, he is entirely on your side." "But he may be out." "Quiet," said Syme suddenly. "What is that noise?" For a second they all sat as motionless as equestrian statues, and for a second—for two, three, even four seconds—heaven and earth seemed equally still. Then, to the ears of all of them, straining almost painfully, there came from along the road that indescribable shudder and throb that can mean only one thing—horses! The Colonel's face changed in an instant, as if he had been struck by lightning and yet left unharmed. "They have done us," he said, with brief military irony. "Prepare to receive cavalry!" "Where can they have got the horses?" asked Syme, as he mechanically urged his own horse into a canter. The Colonel was silent for a little while, and then said in a strained voice—"When I told you that the 'Soleil d'Or' was the only place within twenty miles where you could get horses, I was speaking with strict accuracy." "Surely not!" said Syme violently. "I can't believe he would do that. With that white hair of his." "He may have been forced," said the Colonel gently. "There must be at least a hundred of them. That is exactly why we are all going to see my friend Renard, who has the motor car." With that he suddenly wheeled his horse round a street corner and went down the street at such a thundering pace that the others, though already at full gallop, had trouble keeping up with the flying tail of his horse. Doctor Renard lived in a tall, comfortable house at the very top of a steep street, so that when the riders dismounted at his door they could see once more the solid green ridge of the hill, with the white road across it, rising above all the roofs of the town. They breathed again to see that the road was still empty, and rang the bell. Doctor Renard was a beaming, brown-bearded man, a good example of that quiet but intensely hard-working professional class which France has preserved even more completely than England. When the matter was explained to him, he laughed off the ex-Marquis's panic altogether, and said, with solid French scepticism, that a general rising of anarchists was about as unlikely as anything could be. "Anarchism!" he said with a shrug. "Mere childishness!" "_Et ça_," cried the Colonel suddenly, pointing past the other's shoulder, "is that childishness too, then?" They all turned and saw a troop of black cavalry sweeping in a curve over the crest of the hill with all the momentum of Attila. Yet, fast as they rode, the whole body still held tightly together, and they could see the black masks of the front rank lined up as straight as a row of uniforms. But although the main black square was the same as before, only moving faster, there was now one striking difference, which could be seen as plainly on the hillside as on a tilted map. Most of the riders were in one mass, but one rider was flying far ahead of the column, working his hands and heels like a madman to drive his horse faster and faster, so that he might well have been taken for the hunted rather than the hunter. Yet even at that great distance there was something so fanatical, so unmistakable about the figure that they knew it was the Secretary himself. "I am sorry to cut short a cultured discussion," said the Colonel, "but could you lend me your motor car now, in the next two minutes?" "I have a suspicion that you are all mad," said Doctor Renard, smiling amiably, "but far be it from me to let a little madness stand in the way of friendship. Let us go round to the garage." Doctor Renard was a mild man of enormous wealth; his rooms were like the Cluny Museum, and he had three motor cars. But he had the simple tastes of the French middle class and seemed to use them only very rarely, so that when his impatient friends came to look them over, it took some time to make sure that even one of them would run. With some difficulty they got it out into the street in front of the Doctor's house. Coming out of the dim garage, they were startled to find that dusk had already fallen, with the suddenness of a tropical night. Either they had been in there longer than they thought, or some strange canopy of cloud had gathered over the town. Looking down the steep street, they seemed to see a faint mist rising from the sea. "It is now or never," said Dr. Bull. "I hear horses." "No," corrected the Professor, "a horse." And as they listened, it was clear that the sound coming rapidly nearer over the rattling cobbles was not that of the whole troop but of a single rider who had left it far behind—the mad Secretary. Syme's family, like many families that end up in the simple life, had once owned a motor car, and he knew all about them. He jumped at once into the driver's seat and, red in the face, was wrenching and tugging at the long-unused machinery. He put all his strength on one lever, and then said quite quietly—"I am afraid it's no go." As he spoke, a man sitting rigid on a galloping horse came tearing round the corner with the speed and stiffness of an arrow. He wore a smile that jutted out as though his jaw were dislocated. He brought his horse up beside the stationary car, into which the whole company had crammed themselves, and laid his hand on the front of it. It was the Secretary, and his mouth had gone straight in the solemnity of triumph. Syme was leaning hard on the steering wheel. There was no sound except the rumbling of the other pursuers riding into the town. Then suddenly there came a scream of scraping iron, and the car leapt forward.
