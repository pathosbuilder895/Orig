The last-named Indian said that the _Lunxus_, or Indian Devil (which I take to be the cougar, and not the _Gulo luscus_), was the only animal in Maine that a man need fear. It would follow a person, he said, and did not mind fire. He also said that beavers were again becoming fairly plentiful in the parts we had visited, but that their skins now sold so cheaply that it did not pay to hunt them. I had hung our moose's ears, which were ten inches long, to dry over the fire along with the moose meat, wishing to keep them, but Sabattis told me that unless I skinned them and tanned the skins, the hair would all come off. He said that they made tobacco pouches out of the skin of the ears, putting the two together inside to inside. When I asked him how he made a fire, he produced a small cylindrical box of friction matches. He also had flint and steel and some tinder, but the tinder was not dry. I think it was from the yellow birch. "But suppose you upset, and all this and your powder get wet?" "Then," said he, "I wait till I get to where there is fire." I took out of my pocket a little vial in which I kept matches, stoppered so that no water could get in, and told him that even if we upset, I would still have some dry matches left. At this he stared at it without saying a word. So we lay awake talking for a long while, and they told us the meanings of many of the Indian names of the lakes and rivers in the neighborhood—Tahmunt especially. I asked the Indian name of Moosehead Lake. Joe answered _Sebamook_; Tahmunt pronounced it _Sebemook_. When I asked what it meant, they answered, Moosehead Lake. At length, when they saw what I was getting at, they took turns repeating the word to themselves, as philologists do—_Sebamook_—_Sebamook_—now and then comparing notes in their own tongue, for there was a slight difference between their two dialects. And at last Tahmunt said, "Ah! I know"—and he half rose up on the moose hide—"like here is one place, and there is one place," pointing to different parts of the hide, "you take water from there and fill here, and it stays here. That is _Sebamook_." I understood him to mean a reservoir of water that does not run away: the river comes in on one side and goes out again at nearly the same place, leaving a permanent bay. Another Indian said that it meant a large-bay lake, and that _Sebago_ and _Sebec_, the names of other lakes, were related words meaning large open water. Joe said that _Seboois_ meant little river. I noticed their often-remarked failing, the inability to convey abstract ideas. They grasped the idea, however dimly, yet groped in vain for words to express it. Tahmunt thought that the whites called it Moosehead Lake because Mount Kineo, which overlooks the lake, is shaped like a moose's head, and that Moose River was so called "because the mountain points right across the lake to its mouth." John Josselyn, writing about 1673, says: "Twelve miles from Casco Bay, and passable for men and horses, is a lake, called by the Indians Sebug. On the shore of it, at one end, is a famous rock, shaped like a moose deer, or elk, transparent, and called the Moose Rock." He seems to have confused Sebamook with Sebago, which is nearer but has no "transparent" rock on its shore. I will give a few more of their interpretations, for whatever they are worth—partly _because_ they sometimes differ from the commonly accepted ones. They had never analyzed these words before. After long deliberation and repetition of the word—for it was very hard work—Tahmunt said that _Chesuncook_ meant a place into which many rivers flow (?), and he enumerated them—the Penobscot, the Umbazookskus, the Cusabesex, Red Brook, and so on. "_Caucomgomoc_—what does that mean?" "What do you call that large white bird?" he asked. "Gull," said I. "Ah! Gull Lake." _Pammadumcook_, Joe thought, meant the lake with a gravelly bottom or bed. As for _Kenduskeag_, Tahmunt, after asking whether a birch-bark canoe went up it—for he said he did not know it very well—concluded at last that it meant something like this: "You go up the Penobscot and come to _Kenduskeag_, and you go by, you don't turn in there. That is _Kenduskeag_." (?) But another Indian, who knew the river better, told us afterward that it meant little eel river. _Mattawamkeag_ was the place where two rivers meet. (?) _Penobscot_ was rocky river. One writer says that this was "originally the name of only a section of the main channel, from the head of the tide to a little above Old Town." A very intelligent Indian whom we met later, a son-in-law of Neptune's, gave us the following meanings in addition: _Umbazookskus_, meadow river; _Millinocket_, place of islands; _Aboljacarmegus_, smooth-ledge falls (and dead-water); _Aboljacarmeguscook_, the river that flows in (this last was the word he gave when I asked about _Aboljacknagesic_, which he did not know); _Mattahumkeag_, sand-creek pond; _Piscataquis_, branch of a river. I asked our hosts what _Musketaquid_, the Indian name of Concord, Massachusetts, meant; but they changed it to _Musketicook_ and repeated that, and Tahmunt said that it meant dead river. That is probably right. _Cook_ appears to mean river, and _quid_ probably signifies the place or ground. When I asked the meaning of the names of two of our hills, they answered that those were another language. As Tahmunt said that he traded at Quebec, my companion asked him the meaning of the word _Quebec_, about which there has been so much dispute. He did not know, but began to guess. He asked what we called that great ship that carries soldiers. "A man-of-war," we answered. "Well," said he, "when the English ship came up the river, it was so narrow there that she could not go any farther. She had to turn back, go back—goback—that's Que-bec." I mention this to show what his authority is worth in other cases. Late at night the other two Indians came home from moose-hunting empty-handed, stirred up the fire again, lit their pipes, smoked a while, drank something strong, ate some of the moose meat, and, finding what room they could, lay down on the moose hides. And so we passed the night, two white men and four Indians side by side. When I awoke in the morning it was drizzling. One of the Indians, for lack of room, was lying outside on the far side of the fire, wrapped in his blanket. Joe had neglected to wake my companion, and so he had not hunted that night. Tahmunt was making a crossbar for his canoe with a curiously shaped knife, such as I have since seen other Indians use as well. The blade was thin, about three quarters of an inch wide and eight or nine inches long, and curved out of its own plane into a hook, which he said made it more convenient for shaving. Since the Indians far to the north and northwest use the same kind of knife, I suspect that, though some white craftsmen may use a similar one, it was made after an aboriginal pattern. For breakfast the Indians baked a loaf of flour bread by standing a frying pan with legs on its edge before the fire; and while my companion was making tea, I caught a dozen fair-sized fish in the Penobscot. They were two kinds of suckers and one trout. After we had breakfasted by ourselves, one of our bedfellows, who had also breakfasted, came over, drank a cup of tea at our invitation, and finally took up the common platter and licked it clean. But he was nothing to a certain white man, a logger, who kept stuffing himself with the Indians' moose meat without letup and was the laughingstock of his companions for it. He seemed to think it was an "eat-all" feast. It is commonly said that the white man will at last beat the Indian at his own game, and here that proved true. I cannot swear to what he did while it was dark, but as soon as it was light I saw him at it again, though he had come a quarter of a mile for the purpose. The rain kept us from staying any longer in the woods. So we gave the Indians some of our provisions and utensils and bade them good-by. This being the steamer's day, I set out at once for the lake. I crossed the carry alone and waited at the head of the lake. As I approached, an eagle or some other large bird flew screaming from its perch by the shore. For an hour after I reached the shore I saw no one, and had that broad prospect all to myself. I thought I heard the steamer before it appeared on the open lake. When it came in, I noticed on the landing one of our bedfellows, the one who had gone moose-hunting the night before, now very spruce in a clean white shirt and fine black trousers, a true Indian dandy. He had evidently come across the carry to show himself to the people arriving at the north shore of Moosehead Lake, just as a New York dandy takes a turn down Broadway and stands on the steps of a hotel. Midway on the lake we took on board two manly-looking middle-aged men, together with their bateau. They had been exploring for six weeks as far as the Canada line and had let their beards grow unchecked. They had the skins of some beavers they had recently caught stretched on oval hoops, though the fur was not good at that season. I talked with one of them and told him that I had come all this way partly to see where the white pine, the Eastern timber our houses are built of, grew, but that on this trip and on an earlier excursion to another part of Maine I had found it a scarce tree. And I asked him where I should look for it. With a smile he answered that he could hardly tell me that. But he said that, in a place where it was thought not a single tree was left, he had found enough to keep two crews at work the following winter. What is now regarded as "first quality" would not have been looked at twenty years ago, when he went into the business. But now they did very well with what was then considered decidedly inferior timber. The explorers used to cut into a tree higher and higher up to see whether it was rotten at the heart, and if it had a rotten core as thick as a man's arm, they let it alone. But now they cut down such trees and saw all around the rotten part, and get the very best boards from them, since in such cases they never split. A man engaged in the lumber business at Bangor told me that the largest pine his company cut the previous winter "scaled" four thousand five hundred feet in the woods and was worth ninety dollars in the log at the Bangor boom in Old Town. They had cut a road three and a half miles long for this one tree alone. In his opinion, the principal source of the white pine now coming down the Penobscot is the upper waters of the East Branch and the Allagash, around Webster Stream and Eagle and Chamberlain lakes. Much timber has been stolen from the public lands. (Pray, what kind of forest warden is the public itself?) I heard of one man who, having found some particularly fine trees just within the boundary of the public lands, and not daring to hire an accomplice, cut them down, rolled them into the river with a block and tackle, without even the help of oxen, and so got clean away with them without anyone's assistance. Surely, stealing pine trees in this way is not so mean as robbing a hen roost. That night we reached Monson, and the next day, in the rain again the whole way, we rode to Bangor, varying our route a little. Some of the inns along this road were uncommonly dirty, clearly in a transition state from the camp to the house. * * * * * The next forenoon we went to Old Town. A slender old Indian on the Old Town shore, recognizing my companion, was as full of gaiety and gesture as a Frenchman. A Catholic priest crossed to the island with us in the same bateau. The Indian houses are framed of wood, mostly one story high, and stand in rows, one behind another, at the south end of the island, with a few scattered elsewhere. I counted about forty, not including the church and what my companion called the council-house. The council-house, which is probably their town hall, was neatly framed and shingled like the other houses. Several were two stories high, quite neat, with fenced front yards, and one at least had green blinds. Here and there moose hides were stretched and drying about the houses. There were no cart roads, nor any tracks of horses, only footpaths. There was very little cultivated ground, but an abundance of weeds, native as well as naturalized. The foreign weeds outnumbered the useful vegetables, just as the Indian is said to acquire the white man's vices rather than his virtues. Still, the village was cleaner than I had expected, far cleaner than some Irish villages I have seen. Nor were the children particularly ragged or dirty. Little boys met us, bow in hand and arrow on the string, crying, "Set up a cent!" Truly, the Indian now keeps only a feeble hold on his bow. But the white man's curiosity is insatiable, and from the very first he has been eager to witness this forest accomplishment. That elastic piece of wood with its feathered arrow, which must surely be unstrung by contact with civilization, will serve as the symbol of the savage, his coat of arms. Alas for the hunter race! The white man has driven off their game and put a copper cent in its place. I saw an Indian woman washing at the water's edge. She stood on a rock, dipped the clothes in the river, laid them on the rock, and beat them with a short club. The burying ground was crowded with graves and overrun with weeds, and in it I noticed an inscription in the Indian language painted on a wooden headboard. There was a large wooden cross on the island. Since my companion was acquainted with him, we called on Governor Neptune. He lived in a small "ten-footer," one of the humblest of all the houses. As it is permissible to touch on personal matters in speaking of public men, I will give the particulars of our visit. He was in bed.
