Just outside the little island, along the foot of the dark Nerlumskeechticook mountain. For I had seen from the map that this was our course. The distance across the lake was three or four miles. The outline of this mountain at the southwest of the lake, and of another one beyond it, resembled not only that of the huge rock waves of Webster Stream, but also, in its main lines, Mount Kineo on Moosehead Lake, having at its southeast end a similar, though less precipitous, cliff, I thought. In short, all the prominent hills and ridges hereabouts were Kineos of one size or another, and perhaps there was some such relation between Kineo and the rocks of Webster Stream. The Indian did not know exactly where the outlet was, whether in the extreme southwest corner or farther east, and at our last resting place he had asked to see my map, but I had forgotten to show it to him. As usual, he felt his way along, taking a course midway between the two likely points. In that way, whichever way he turned at last, he would not lose much distance. As we drew near the southern shore, the clouds seemed to be charged with squalls, and the waves were rather high, so we steered so as to be partly in the lee of an island, though it was a good way off. The outlet could hardly be made out until we were almost in it, and there we heard the sound of water falling over a dam. Here there was a fall of considerable height and a very solid dam, but no sign of a hut or a camping ground. The hunter we had met at Telos Lake had said there were plenty of trout here, but at this hour they would not take the bait, and all we caught were cousins of the trout, which rose from the very midst of the rushing water. These rivers do not hold as many fish as the Concord. While we lingered here, Polis took the opportunity to cut some of the hair off the moose hide with his big knife, thus lightening it and preparing it for drying. At several old Indian camps in the woods I had seen heaps of the hair they had cut from hides. After carrying over the dam, he shot down the rapids like an arrow, which left us to walk a mile or more, mostly without any path, and very thick and hard going near the river. Every now and then he would call out to let us know where he was waiting for us with the canoe, for the river wound about so much that we could not tell where its bank was. But he forgot that we were not Indians, and did not call often enough. He seemed very sparing of his breath—and yet he was surprised if we passed him by or failed to hit upon the right place. This was not owing to any unkindness on his part, but was rather a mark of good breeding. The Indian likes to get things done with as little talk and fuss as possible. He in fact treated us with great respect all along, and supposed that we would rather be given a hint than a kick. At last, making our way by climbing over the willows and fallen trees whenever that was easier than going around or under them, we caught up with the canoe, and glided several miles down the river on a smooth but swift current. Here I noticed again, as I had on Webster Stream, and as I was to notice the next day on a still larger scale, that the river was a smooth and regularly inclined plane, down which we coasted along by our own momentum. As we glided along in this way, we started up the first black duck that we had identified as such. So as to have plenty of time before dark, we decided to camp early tonight, and so we stopped at the first suitable shore, which had a narrow gravel beach on the west side, about five miles below the outlet of the lake. It was an interesting spot, where the river begins to make a great bend to the east, and the last of the strange moose-faced Nerlumskeechticook mountains, not far from the southwest of Grand Lake, rose black just behind us to the northwest, showing its gray and precipitous southeast face. We could not see it, however, until we went out onto the shore. On both sides, two steps from the water's edge you come to the brink of a bank four or five feet high, steep and full of bushes and roots, if not quite turf, and there the endless forest begins. It is as if the river had only just now cut a road for itself through the forest. It is remarkable how very often, wherever you land in this unbroken primeval forest, at least within a few rods of the river, you see the marks of an axe. They were made by the loggers who had camped here, or who had passed by driving logs in some former spring. Perhaps you may even see where they, having come on the same errand as yourself, have shaved off large chips for their fire from the stump of a tall white pine. While we were setting up camp and getting supper ready, the Indian cut the rest of the hair off the moose hide, and, making a rough frame between two small trees about six feet away on the other side of the fire, set about stretching the hide upright upon it. He lashed it and drew it tight with arbor-vitae bark, which is always at hand, and in this case the bark had been stripped from one of the very trees to which he tied the hide. When we asked for a new kind of tea, he made us a pretty good one out of the checkerberry (_Gaultheria procumbens_) which covered the ground, dropping a little bundle of it, tied with cedar bark, into the kettle. But it was not quite equal to the _Chiogenes_. So we named this place Checkerberry-Tea Camp. I was struck by the abundance, almost everywhere in the Maine woods, of _Linnæa borealis_, checkerberry, and _Chiogenes hispidula_. The pipsissewa (_Chimaphila umbellata_) was still in bloom here, and the berries of the clintonia were ripe in abundance. This beautiful plant is one of the commonest in those woods. Here we noticed for the first time moosewood in fruit along the shore. The prevailing trees were spruce (mostly black spruce), arbor-vitae, canoe birch (black ash and elm were beginning to appear), yellow birch, red maple, and a few hemlocks lurking furtively in the forest. The Indian said that the best thing for tinder was rotten white maple, and that rotten yellow birch was pretty good too, but hard. After supper he set the moose's tongue and lips on the fire to boil, and cut out the _nasal septum_. He showed me how to write on the inner side of birch bark with a twig of black spruce, which is hard and tough and can be sharpened to a point. Just before dark the Indian went a little way into the woods, and when he came back he said, "Me found great treasure—fifty, sixty dollars' worth." "What is it?" we asked. "Steel traps, under a log, thirty or forty, me no count. Maybe Indian work—worth three dollars apiece." It was a curious coincidence that in that pathless forest he should happen to walk up to that particular log and look under it. As I was washing my hands, I saw chivin and chubs in the river, which my companion tried in vain to catch. I also heard a bullfrog in the swamp on the opposite shore, and at first took it for a moose. A duck swam swiftly past. And sitting there in that dim wilderness, under that dark mountain, beside the bright river full of reflected light, I could still hear the song of the wood thrush, as if no higher civilization could be attained. By this time night was closing in on us. You commonly make your camp just at sunset, and while the curtain of night gathers round you and deepens the already thick gloom of the forest, you collect your firewood, get your supper, and pitch your tent. There is no time to explore or look about you before dark. You may go six rods or so into that twilit wilderness, looking for dry bark to kindle the fire with, and, say at the end of a long day's walk, wonder what further mysteries lie hidden beyond. Or you may run down to the shore to dip up a dipperful of water, and get a somewhat clearer view up or down the river, and while you stand there you may see a fish leap, or a duck alight on the river, or hear a wood thrush or a robin singing in the woods. It is as if you had been to some town or civilized place. But there can be no rambling about to see the country; even ten or fifteen rods seems a terribly long way from your companions, and you come back as if from a long journey, with the air of a much-traveled man who has adventures to tell, although you may have heard the crackling of the fire all the while—and if you were to go a hundred rods, you might be lost past recovery and have to camp out. Everything is mossy and _moosey_. In some of those dense fir and spruce woods there is hardly room even for the smoke to go up. The trees are a _standing_ night, and every fir or spruce you fell is a plume plucked from night's raven wing. And at night the stillness all around is more impressive than any sound, but now and then you hear the hooting of an owl, far off or near at hand in the forest, and, if you are near a lake, the half-human cry of the loons revelling at some unearthly feast. Tonight the Indian lay down between the fire and the stretched moose hide, to escape the mosquitoes. Indeed, he made little smoky fires of damp leaves at his head and at his feet, and then, as usual, wrapped his head up in his blanket. We were tolerably comfortable thanks to our veils and our ointment, but it would be hard to carry on any sedentary work in the woods at this season. At night you can hardly read a book through a veil by the light of the fire, and with gloved or greased fingers you cannot manage a pencil and paper very well. * * * * * Friday, July 31. The Indian said, "You and me kill moose last night, so use best wood. Always use hard wood to cook moose meat." By the "best wood" he meant rock maple. He threw the moose's lips into the fire to singe the hair off, and then rolled them up with the meat to carry. When he saw that we were about to sit down to breakfast without any pork, he said, with a very grave face, "Me want fat," and was told that he might take as much as he wished to fry. For a good distance there was smooth but swift water, down which we glided rapidly, starting up ducks and kingfishers. But, as usual, our smooth progress soon came to an end, and we had to carry canoe and all about half a mile along the right bank to get round some rapids or falls. It sometimes took a sharp eye to make out which side the carry was on before we had gone over the falls, but Polis never once made a mistake, and always landed us at the right place. The raspberries here were especially abundant and large, and we all fell to eating them. The Indian remarked on their size. On the bare-rock carries the trail was often so indistinct that I lost it again and again, but when I walked behind him I found that he could follow it almost like a hound, and rarely hesitated. Or, if he paused for a moment on the bare rock, his eye at once detected some sign of it which I would have overlooked. In such places _we_ frequently could not find the path at all, and were, to him, unaccountably slow. He would only say, "Ver' strange." We had heard that there was a Grand Fall on this river, and so at every fall we came to we thought that this must be it, but after we had given the name to several in succession, we gave up looking for it. There were more grand falls and little falls than I could keep count of. I cannot say how many times we had to walk on account of falls and rapids. We kept expecting the river to take its last leap and come out into smooth water, but this forenoon it did not get any better. The carries, however, made a pleasant change. Whenever we got out of the canoe and stretched our legs, we invariably found ourselves in a garden of blueberries and raspberries, one or the other, or both, lining the rocky path around the falls on either side. There was not a carry on the main East Branch where we did not find these two berries in great plenty. For these were the rockiest places, and partly open, as these plants like, and no one had picked the best of the fruit before us. In going over each carry three times—for every time we took the canoe out we had to pass over the same path three times—we feasted on berries to our hearts' content. They were just the thing to correct the effects of a diet of nothing but hard bread and pork. Making a portage might just as well have been called "going a-berrying." We also found a few fruits of the amelanchier, or serviceberry. Most of them had not ripened well, but they were left on the branches more generally than at Concord. The Indian called them _pemoymenuk_, and said that in some places they bore abundantly. He sometimes ate the northern wild red cherry too, saying that it was good medicine, but it was hardly fit to eat. We bathed and dined at the foot of one of these carries. It was usually the Indian who reminded us that it was dinner time, sometimes by turning the bow of the canoe toward the shore. Once he made an indirect but lengthy apology: it might seem strange, he said, but a man who works hard all day is very particular about taking his dinner at the proper hour. At the largest fall on this river, as I was walking the carry close behind the Indian, he noticed a track on a rock that had only a thin covering of soil, and stooping down, he muttered, "Caribou." On our way back he found, near the same spot, a much larger track, where the foot of some animal had sunk into a little hollow in the rock that was partly filled with grass and earth. He exclaimed in surprise, "What that?" "Well, what is it?" I asked. He stooped and laid his hand in the hollow, and answered mysteriously, half in a whisper, "Devil [that is, Indian devil, or cougar]—about these ledges—very bad animal—pull rocks all to pieces." "How long ago was it made?" I asked. "Today or yesterday," he said. But afterward, when I asked him whether he was sure it was the track of a devil, he said he did not know. I had been told that the scream of a cougar had lately been heard about Ktaadn, and we were not far from that mountain.
