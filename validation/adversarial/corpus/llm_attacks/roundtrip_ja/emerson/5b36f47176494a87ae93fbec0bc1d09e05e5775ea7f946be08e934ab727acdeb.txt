I have also known gentlemen who had a great stake in the community but whose sympathies were cold—college presidents, governors, senators—who held it their duty to sign every temperance pledge, to act with the Bible societies, the missions, and the peace workers, and to call out _Go get him!_ to every good dog. We must not carry courtesy too far, but we all have kindly impulses in this direction. When boys come into my yard asking leave to gather horse chestnuts, I confess that I join in nature's game and pretend to give permission reluctantly, fearing all the while that at any moment they will see through the trickery of that showy chaff. But this solicitude is entirely needless. The enchantment is laid on very thick. Their young lives are thatched with it. The children of a shanty I saw yesterday lived in a condition so poor and hard that it would bring tears to your eyes. And yet, just like children in the most fortunate condition, they had decked the shanty out in tinsel romance and spoke of "the dear old cabin where so many happy hours had flowed away." Well, thatching the shanty in this way is the custom of the country. Women, more than anyone, are the element and the kingdom of illusion. Being fascinated, they fascinate. They see through a Claude Lorrain mirror. And who would dare, even if he could, to strip away the _backdrops_, the stage effects, and the ceremonies by which they live? The realm of the affections is too pitiable, too painful, and its atmosphere is always prone to _mirage_. We are not so much to blame for our bad marriages. We live in the midst of hallucinations, and this particular snare is set to trip our feet, and everyone is tripped sooner or later. But the great Mother, who has been so sly with us, as if she felt that she owed us some amends, quietly slips a few deep and serious benefits and some great joys into the Pandora's box of marriage. We find in the beauty and happiness of our children a joy that makes the heart too big for the body. In even the most ill-matched union there is always some true marriage mixed in. Even Teague and his jade gain something of the right relations of mutual respect, considerate observation, and mutual nurture; they learn something, and if they were starting now, they would behave more wisely. It is easy for us to point at this or that worthy madman, as if anyone were exempt. The scholar in his study is no exception. I, who in the course of my life have heard countless speeches and debates, read poems and miscellaneous books, and talked with many men of genius, am still the victim of any new page; and if Marmaduke, or Hugh, or Moosehead, or anyone else invents a new style or mythology, I fancy that the world would be altogether splendid and right if only it would put on these colors I had never thought of. So I promptly daub on this new paint; but it does not hold. It is like the glue the peddler sells at the door. He sticks broken crockery together with it before your eyes, but you can never buy from him the least bit of a glue that will keep things stuck after he has gone. Men who make their presence felt in the world are using a certain fate in their constitution, and they know how to employ it. But they never interest us deeply unless they lift a corner of the curtain, or let slip, however slightly, that they see what lies behind it. The charm of practical men is that outside their practicality there is a certain poetry and play, as if, holding the reins of the good horse of power, they preferred to walk, though they could ride it so hard. Bonaparte, like Caesar, is a man of intellect. And the best soldiers, sea captains, and railroad men have a gentleness about them when off duty; they good-naturedly grant that there is such a thing as illusion. And who can say that he is not its plaything? Those cast-iron fellows who cannot detach themselves in this way, whatever powers they may be gifted with, we brand as "dragon-ridden," "thunderstruck," the fools of fate. Since our education proceeds by symbols and roundabout methods, it is well to know that there is method in it, that there are fixed stages, and that the phantoms stand rank above rank. We begin low, with coarse masks, and climb to the subtlest and most beautiful. The red men told Columbus "there is an herb that takes away weariness"; but for him the illusion of "reaching India from the east" did more than any tobacco to calm his lofty spirit. Is not our faith in the impenetrability of matter more soothing than any narcotic? You play at jackstraws, at ball, at bowls, with horse and gun, with estates and politics; but there are finer games before you. Is not time a beautiful toy? Life will show you masks worth all your carnivals. That mountain yonder must migrate into your mind. The fine stardust and nebular haze of Orion, "the portentous year of Mizar and Alcor," must come down and be handled in your everyday thought. What if you should come to perceive that all the plays and stages of this pompous history issued from yourself, and that the sun borrows its light? What terrible questions we are learning to ask! The ancients believed in magic, by which temples, cities, and men were swallowed up and every trace of them vanished. We are drawing near the secret of a magic that sweeps out of men's minds every trace of the theism and faith that they and their fathers held and were shaped upon. There are deceptions of the senses, deceptions of the passions, and the structural, beneficent illusions of feeling and of intellect. There is the illusion of love, which credits the beloved with everything he or she shares with family, sex, age, and condition, nay, with the human mind itself. It is these that the lover loves, and Anna Matilda gets the credit. It is as if a man who had always been shut up in a tower with a single window, through which he could see the face of heaven and earth, should fancy that all the wonders he beheld belonged to that window. There is the illusion of time, which is very deep. Who has disposed of it? Or who has come to the conviction that what seems a succession of thoughts is only the distribution of wholes into causal series? The intellect sees that every atom carries the whole of nature; that the mind is open to omnipotence; that in the endless striving and ascent the metamorphosis is complete, so that the soul, when its act is perfected, does not recognize itself in that act. There is an illusion that deceives even the elect. There is an illusion that deceives even the worker of miracles. He makes his own body, yet denies that he makes it. Though the world exists from thought, thought shrinks before the world. We accept the laws of the mind one after another, yet still resist the laws that follow, though these too must be accepted. But all our concessions only drive us on to new extravagance. And what does it avail that science has come to treat space and time as mere forms of thought and the material world as hypothetical, and that along with this our claim to property, and even to selfhood, is fading with the rest, if after all even our thoughts are not final, if the ceaseless flowing and ascent reaches them as well, and each thought that was final yesterday is today giving way to a larger generalization? Since we must work in an element so volatile, it is no wonder that our estimates are vague and drifting. We must work and affirm, yet we have no idea of the worth of what we say or do. The cloud is now the size of your hand, and now it covers a county. The story of Thor, who was bidden in Asgard to drain the drinking horn, to wrestle with an old woman, and to race the runner Lok, and who presently learned that he had been trying to drink the sea, wrestling with Time, and racing with Thought, describes us, who amid these seeming trifles are contending with the supreme forces of nature. We fancy that we have fallen into bad company and shabby circumstances: petty debts, shoe bills, broken glass to pay for, pots to buy, butcher's meat, sugar, milk, coal. "Ye gods, set me some great task! Then I will show my mettle." "Not so," says the good heaven. "Plod and plow. Mend your old coat and hat, braid your shoestrings. Great enterprises and the finest wine will come in good time." Well, it is all phantasm; and if we weave a yard of tape in all humility and as well as we can, long afterward we shall find that it was no cotton tape at all, but some galaxy we had braided, and that its threads were time and nature. We cannot write down the order of the shifting winds. How then could we see into the law of our changeable moods and susceptibilities? And yet they differ as everything differs from nothing. Instead of yesterday's firmament, which our eyes demand, today it is an eggshell that shuts us in. We cannot even see what or where the star of our destiny is. Day after day, the chief facts of human life are hidden from our eyes. Suddenly the mist rolls up and reveals them, and we think how much good time has gone by that might have been saved had we been given any hint of these things. When the road suddenly rises, the whole range of mountains and all its peaks come into view; they had been just as near all year long, but quite out of mind. Yet these alternations are not without order, and we are parties to our various fortunes. If life seems a series of dreams, poetic justice is done in dreams too. The visions of good men are good; it is the undisciplined will that is whipped with bad thoughts and bad fortunes. When we break the law, we lose our hold on the central reality. Like the sick in a hospital, we merely change from bed to bed, from one folly to another. And it can matter little what becomes of such castaways—wailing, stupid, comatose creatures—who are lifted from bed to bed and carried from the nothing of life to the nothing of death. In this kingdom of illusions we grope eagerly for supports and foundations. There are none, except a strict and faithful dealing within ourselves and a severe shutting out of all duplicity and illusion there. Whatever games are played upon us, we must play no games upon ourselves, but must deal, in private, with honesty and truth down to the last scrap. I regard the simple, childlike virtues of truthfulness and honesty as the root of all that is sublime in character. Speak as you think, be what you are, pay your debts of every kind. I would rather be acknowledged as sound and solvent, have my word acknowledged to be as good as my bond, and be a thing that cannot be blown away, scattered, or undermined, than have all the _éclat_ in the universe. This reality is the foundation of friendship, religion, poetry, and art. At the top of all illusions, or at the bottom of them, I place that cheat which still leads us to work and live for appearances, even though in every sane hour we are convinced that what counts with friends, with strangers, and with fate or fortune is what we really are. To hear men talk, you would think wealth and poverty were a great matter, and our civilization chiefly esteems it. But the Indians say they do not think that the white man, with his care-lined brow, forever toiling, afraid of heat and cold, shut up indoors, is any better off than they are. The lasting interest of every man is never to put himself in a false position, but to have the weight of nature at his back in everything he does. Wealth and poverty are a thick costume or a thin one, and our life—the life of all of us—is the same. For we are constantly transcending circumstance and tasting the true quality of existence. So it is in our work, which differs only in its handling and expresses the same laws; so too in our thoughts, which wear no silks and taste no ice cream. Every hour we meet God face to face and know the savor of nature. The early Greek philosophers Heraclitus and Xenophanes tried their strength on this problem of identity. Diogenes of Apollonia said that if the atoms were not made of one stuff, they could never mix or act upon one another. But the Hindus, in their sacred books, express the liveliest feeling both of the essential identity and of the illusion which they take variety to be. "The notions '_I am_' and '_This is mine_,' which sway mankind, are but the delusions of the mother of the world. Dispel, O Lord of all things, the conceit of knowledge that springs from ignorance!" And they hold that the beatitude of man lies in being released from enchantment. The intellect is stirred by the statement of truth in a figure, and the will by the wrapping of the laws of life in illusion. But the unity of truth and right is not broken by the disguise. There need never be confusion in these. In the crowded life of many parts and players, on the stage of nations, or in the most obscure hamlet in Maine or California, the same elements offer the same choices to each newcomer, and according to his choice he fixes his destiny in absolute nature. It would be hard to pack more mental and moral philosophy into one sentence than the Persians have—"Thou must be deceived, though thou be the wisest of the wise. Then be the fool of virtue, not of vice." There is neither chance nor anarchy in the universe. All is system and gradation. Every god sits there in his own sphere. The young mortal enters the hall of the firmament. There he stands alone, face to face with the gods alone. They shower blessings and gifts upon him and beckon him up to their thrones. At that instant, and without ceasing, the snowstorms of illusion come down. He fancies himself in the midst of a huge crowd that sways this way and that, whose movements and conduct he must follow. He fancies himself poor, orphaned, of no account. The mad crowd rushes hither and thither, now fiercely commanding him to do this, now that. Who is he, that he should resist their will and think or act for himself? Moment by moment, new changes and fresh showers of deception bewilder and distract him. And at length, for an instant, the air clears and the clouds lift a little, and there sit the gods, still on their thrones around him—the gods alone, with him alone. The End.
