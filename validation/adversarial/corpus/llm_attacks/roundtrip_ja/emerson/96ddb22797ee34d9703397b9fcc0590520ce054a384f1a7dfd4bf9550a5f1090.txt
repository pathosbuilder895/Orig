In Europe, where feudal forms secure the permanence of wealth in particular families, those families buy and preserve these things and open them to the public. But in America, where democratic institutions divide all property into small parts, the public ought within a few years to take the place of such owners and provide this culture and inspiration for the citizen. Man was born to be rich, or grows rich inevitably through the use of his faculties, that is, through the union of thought with nature. Property is a product of the intellect. The game demands of its players coolness, right reasoning, promptness, and patience. Trained labor drives out crude labor. Countless shrewd people, over endless years, have arrived at certain best and shortest ways of doing things, and it is this accumulated skill in the arts, in cultivation, harvesting, curing and preserving, manufacture, navigation, and trade that makes up the value of our world today. Commerce is a game of skill, which not everyone can play, and which few can play well. The true merchant is a man with that right average of faculties which we call _common sense_: a man with a strong affinity for facts, who makes his decisions on the strength of what he has seen. He is convinced, in his very heart, of the truths of arithmetic. There is always a reason, _in the man himself_, for his good fortune and his bad, and so in making money as well. People talk as though there were some magic in it, and they believe in magic in every part of life. He knows that everything goes on in the old way, a pound for a pound, a cent for a cent—that for every effect there is a perfect cause—and that good luck is only another name for tenacity of purpose. He insures himself in every transaction, and prefers small and sure gains. Honesty and a close hold on facts are the foundation, but the masters of the art add to them a certain long arithmetic. The problem is to combine many distant enterprises with the same accuracy and fidelity to fact that come easily in small and near transactions, and so to arrive at enormous results without the least loss of safety.
Napoleon liked to tell the story of a banker in Marseilles who, when a visitor expressed surprise at the contrast between the splendor of his chateau and his hospitality and the shabbiness of the counting-room in which he had met him, said to him: "Young man, you are too young to understand how masses are formed—that is the true and the only power—whether they are made of money, of water, or of men, it is all the same—a mass is an immense center of motion, but it must be begun, and it must be kept up"—and he might have added that the way to begin it and keep it up is obedience to the law of particles. Success consists in close obedience to the laws of the world, and since those laws are intellectual and moral, it consists in an intellectual and moral obedience. Political economy is as good a book as any Bible that has come down to us for reading in it human life and the supremacy of law over every private and hostile influence. Money is representative, and follows the nature and the fortunes of its owner. The coin is a delicate meter of civil, social, and moral changes. The farmer is sparing of his dollar, and with good reason: it is no windfall to him. He knows how many strokes of labor it stands for. His bones ache with the day's work that earned it. He knows how much land it represents—how much rain and frost and sunshine. He knows how much prudence and patience, how much hoeing and threshing, he is giving you in that dollar. Try lifting his dollar; you must lift all of its weight. In the city, where money follows the stroke of a pen or a lucky rise in exchange, it comes to be thought of as light. I wish the farmer would value it more, and spend it only for real bread. Force for force. The farmer's dollar is heavy, and the clerk's is light and nimble; it leaps out of his pocket and jumps onto cards and the faro table. But stranger still is its sensitivity to metaphysical changes. It is the finest barometer of social storms, and announces revolutions. Every step of civil progress makes every man's dollar worth more. In California, the country where it was born and grew up—what would it buy?
A few years ago it bought a shanty, dysentery, hunger, bad company, and crime. In vast countries like Siberia, even today it buys little more than a slight easing of suffering. In Rome it buys beauty and magnificence. Forty years ago a dollar did not buy much in Boston. Now, thanks to the railroad, the telegraph, the steamship, and the simultaneous growth of New York and of the whole country, it buys a great deal more, even in our old town. Even so, many articles that belong to a capital cannot yet be bought here; no, not with mountains of dollars. A dollar in Florida is not worth as much as a dollar in Massachusetts. A dollar is not value but the representative of value, and, at last, the representative of moral values. A dollar is rated by the corn it will buy. Strictly speaking, it is rated not by corn or by house-room but by Athenian corn and Roman house-room—that is, by the wit, the probity, and the power which we eat bread and live in houses in order to share and exercise. Wealth is spiritual; wealth is moral. The value of a dollar is to buy just things. A dollar goes on increasing in value with all the genius and all the virtue of the world. A dollar in a university is worth more than a dollar in a jail, and a dollar in a temperate, educated, law-abiding community is worth more than a dollar in some den of crime where dice and knives and arsenic are forever flying about. The "Bank-Note Detector" is a useful publication. But the circulating dollar, silver or paper, is itself a detector of the right and wrong of the place where it circulates. If justice increases, does it not at once rise in value? When a merchant refuses to sell his vote, or holds fast to some detested right, he adds that much to the justice of Massachusetts, and every acre in the State is worth more from the hour of his act. If you were to take the ten most honest merchants out of State Street and put in ten rogues handling the same amount of capital—the insurance rates would show it; the soundness of the banks would reveal it; the highways would be less safe; the schools would feel it; the children would carry home a little dose of the poison.
The judge would not sit quite so firmly on the bench, and his decisions would be less just. He has lost that much support and restraint—which everyone needs; and the pulpit will betray it in a looser rule of life. An apple tree, if every day for many days you took a load of loam away from around its roots and put in a load of sand—would notice it. The apple tree is a stupid kind of creature, but if this treatment were kept up for some time, I think it would begin to suspect something. And if you were to take a hundred good men out of the influential class engaged in commerce and put in a hundred bad ones, or, what comes to exactly the same thing, introduce a corrupting system, how could the dollar, which is not much stupider than an apple tree, fail to notice it before long? The value of a dollar is social, since it is created by society. Whoever moves into this town possessing any talent or skill that can be bought gives a new value to the labor of every man in the town. When a talent is born into the world anywhere, the community of nations is enriched; and when a new degree of honesty is born, it is enriched still more. The cost of crime, one of the chief burdens of every state, is to that extent stopped. In Europe it has been observed that crime rises and falls with the price of bread. If the Rothschilds in Paris do not accept bills, the people of Manchester, Paisley, and Birmingham are driven into the highway, and in Ireland the landlords are shot down. The police records bear witness to it. The vibration is soon felt in New York, New Orleans, and Chicago as well. In a manner not very different, economic power reaches the masses through their political rulers. If Rothschild refuses the Russian loan, there is peace, and the harvests are saved. If he takes it, there is war, and an agitation through a great part of mankind, ending, with all its horrible consequences, in revolution and a new order. Wealth carries its own checks and balances within itself. The basis of political economy is non-interference. The only safe rule is found in the self-adjusting meter of demand and supply. Do not make laws. Interfere, and you cut the sinews with sumptuary laws. Give no bounties. Make equal laws. Secure life and property, and you need not give alms. Open the doors of opportunity to talent and virtue.
Then they will do themselves justice, and property will not fall into bad hands. In a free and just commonwealth, property rushes from the idle and the incapable to the industrious, the brave, and the persevering. The laws of nature work through commerce just as a toy battery displays the action of electricity. The sea keeps its level with no greater certainty than that with which the balance of values in society is kept by demand and supply; and artifice and legislation punish themselves by reactions, gluts, and bankruptcies. The sublime laws work without distinction in the atom and in the galaxy. Whoever gets a loaf of bread and a pint of beer and knows what happens when he uses them, who knows that no amount of wishing will alter the strict limits of a pint or of a penny loaf, that whatever is consumed leaves that much less in the basket or the pot, but that what has gone out of them, if it nourishes his body and enables him to finish his work, has not been wasted but well spent—knows all the political economy that the budgets of empires can teach him. The interest of a small household lies in the way it thus symbolizes the great economy: in the way the conduct of one house and one private man corresponds to the solar system and to the laws of give and take throughout all of nature. And however watchful we may be against the falsehoods and petty tricks we so self-defeatingly play on one another, every man feels a certain satisfaction when his dealings touch unavoidable fact; when he sees the thing itself setting the price. Things always tend to do so, and in large manufactures one sees them actually doing it. Your paper is not fine enough or not coarse enough—it is too heavy, or too thin. The manufacturer says: I will supply it as thick or as thin as you like; the pattern makes no difference to me whatever; here is my price list—every kind of paper, from the cheapest to the dearest, with its price attached. A pound of paper costs so much, and you may have it made up in any pattern you fancy. In all our dealings a self-adjustment is at work that makes haggling unnecessary. You are going to rent a house, but you say it must be cheap. The landlord can lower the rent, but then he cannot make the proper repairs, and the tenant gets not the house he wanted but a worse one.
Besides, a somewhat harmful relation springs up between landlord and tenant. You dismiss your hired man and say, "Patrick, I'll send for you as soon as I can't do without you." Patrick goes off content, for he knows that the weeds will grow with the potatoes, that the vines must be set out next week, and that however unwilling you may be, the cantaloupes, crooknecks, and cucumbers will send for him. Who would not wish that all labor and all value might stand in the same simple, surly market? If it is the best of its kind, it will. We need the joiner, the locksmith, the planter, the priest, the poet, the doctor, the cook, the weaver, the hostler; each in his turn, through the year. If a St. Michael's pear sells for a shilling, it costs a shilling to raise it. If the best securities in Boston offer twelve _per cent_ for money, they carry exactly six _per cent_ of insecurity. You may not see that the fine pear costs you a shilling, but it costs the community that much. The shilling represents the number of enemies the pear has and the amount of risk in bringing it to ripeness. The price of coal shows the narrowness of the coal fields and the compulsory confinement of the miners to certain districts. Every salary is reckoned not only on the actual service but on service against contingencies. "If the wind were always southwest by west, a woman could take a ship to sea," said the captain. It might be said that all things are of one price; that nothing is cheap and nothing dear, and that the apparent differences that catch our eye are only the shopkeeper's trick for concealing the loss hidden in your purchase. The young man who comes up to the city from his native New Hampshire farm, with the memory of its plain fare still fresh, boards at a first-class hotel, and, since luxuries are cheap, believes he must somehow have outwitted Dr. Franklin and Malthus. But he is paying for the single convenience of a better dinner with the loss of some of the richest social and educational advantages. What a safeguard he has lost! What a spur!
He may soon find that he left the Muses at the door of the hotel and met the Furies inside. Money is often bought too dear, and power and pleasure are not cheap. The ancient poet said, "The gods sell all things at a fair price." There is an instance of compensation in the commercial history of this country. When the European wars, from 1800 to 1812, threw the carrying trade of the world into American ships, an American ship was now and then captured. The loss, of course, was a serious one for the owner, but the country as a whole was indemnified; for we charged threepence a pound for carrying cotton, sixpence for tobacco, and so on, which paid for the risk and the loss and brought the country immense prosperity, early marriages, private wealth, the building of cities, and the building of States. And after the war was over, we received by treaty, on top of all that, compensation for every capture. Well, the Americans grew rich and great. But the day of payment comes round. England, France, and Germany, impoverished by our extraordinary profits, and drawn by the report of our advantages, send first thousands and then millions of their poor to share in the harvest. At first we employ them and increase our prosperity. But in the artificial system of society and of protected labor, which we too have adopted and enlarged, stagnation and deadlock come in time.
