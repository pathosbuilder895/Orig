The secret ballot would enable him to let himself go, freed from any sense of shame or responsibility. "Not so very long ago, the upper and wealthy classes held complete control of the government. Their power was the chief grievance of the country. The habit of voting at the bidding of an employer or a landlord was so firmly rooted that hardly anything could shake it except a strong popular enthusiasm, and such enthusiasm seldom arises except in a good cause. A vote cast against those influences was therefore, in general, an honest and public-spirited vote. But in any case, and whatever the motive that prompted it, it was almost certain to be a good vote. For it was a vote against a monstrous evil, namely the overwhelming influence of oligarchy. Had the voter of that day been enabled to exercise his privilege freely while keeping himself safe, then even if he had exercised it neither honestly nor wisely, it would have been a great gain to reform. For it would have broken the yoke of the power that then ruled the country—the power that had created and maintained every evil in the institutions and administration of the State—namely, the power of the landlords and the borough-mongers. "The secret ballot was not adopted. But the progress of events has, in this respect, been doing the work of the ballot, and is doing it more and more. Both the political and the social condition of the country, so far as they bear on this question, have changed greatly, and are changing every day. The upper classes are no longer the masters of the country. Anyone who could suppose that the middle class is as subservient to the upper class as it was a quarter of a century ago, or that the working class is as dependent on the upper and middle classes as it was then, must be shutting his eyes to every sign of the times. The events of that quarter century have not only taught each class to know its own collective strength, but have put the individual members of the lower classes in a position to show a far bolder front toward those of the higher. In the great majority of cases, the votes of the electors, whether they go against or along with the wishes of their superiors, are today no longer the result of a coercion that can no longer be applied by the means once used, but the expression of their own personal or political preferences. The very abuses of the present electoral system are proof of this. The growth of bribery, so loudly complained of, and the spread of the contagion to places that were once free of it, are evidence that local influences are no longer paramount: that the electors now vote not to please others but to please themselves. It is true that in the counties and the small boroughs a great deal of servile dependence still remains. But the temper of the times is against it, and the force of circumstances is constantly working to diminish it. A good tenant can now feel that he is as valuable to his landlord as his landlord is to him. A prosperous tradesman can afford to feel independent of any particular customer. With every election, the votes become more and more the voters' own. What now needs to be emancipated is far more their minds than their personal circumstances. They are no longer passive instruments of other men's will—mere organs for placing power in the hands of a ruling oligarchy. The electors themselves are becoming the oligarchy. "Exactly in proportion as the elector's vote is determined by his own will and not by the will of someone who is his master, his position comes to resemble that of a member of Parliament, and publicity becomes indispensable. So long as any portion of the community is unrepresented, the Chartists' argument against a secret ballot coupled with a restricted suffrage is unanswerable. The present electors, and the bulk of those whom any likely Reform Bill would add to their number, are the middle class, and they have a class interest distinct from that of the working classes, just as the landlords and the great manufacturers do. Even if the suffrage were extended to all skilled laborers, they too would still have, or might have, a class interest distinct from that of the unskilled. Suppose it extended to all men—suppose that what was once called by the misused name of universal suffrage, and is now called by the foolish title of manhood suffrage, became law. The voters would still have a class interest as distinguished from women. Suppose that a question particularly affecting women were brought before the legislature—whether, for example, women should be allowed to take degrees at the universities. Whether the light penalties inflicted on ruffians who beat their wives nearly to death every day should be replaced by something more effective. Or suppose someone were to propose in the British Parliament what state after state in America is now establishing, not by mere legislation but by provisions of their revised state constitutions, namely that married women should have a right to their own property—would not a man's wife and daughters then have a right to know whether he is voting for or against a candidate who would support these proposals? "It will of course be objected along the following lines. These arguments derive all their weight from the supposition of an unjust state of the suffrage. If the opinion of the non-electors is likely to make the elector vote more honestly, or more beneficially, than he would if left to himself, then they are fitter to be electors than he is, and ought to have the franchise. Whoever is fit to influence electors is fit to be an elector. Those to whom voters ought to be accountable should themselves be voters, and, being so, should have the protection of the secret ballot to shield them from the undue influence of powerful individuals or classes to whom they ought not to be accountable. "This argument is plausible, and I myself once thought it conclusive. It now seems to me mistaken. Not everyone who is fit to influence electors is, for that reason, fit to be an elector himself. The latter is a far greater power than the former, and people may already be ripe for the lesser political function who cannot yet safely be entrusted with the higher. The opinions and wishes of the poorest and roughest part of the working classes may be extremely useful as one influence among others on the minds of the voters, as well as on the mind of the legislature. And yet it might be extremely harmful to give them a preponderant influence by admitting them, in their present moral and intellectual condition, to the full exercise of the suffrage. It is precisely this indirect influence of those without the franchise over those who have it that, by its gradual growth, softens every transition to a fresh extension of the suffrage, and becomes the means by which, when the time is ripe, that extension is peacefully brought about. But there is another, still deeper consideration, which should never be left out of the account in political speculation. The notion that publicity, and the sense of being answerable to the public, are of no use unless the public is qualified to form a sound judgment, is itself groundless. To suppose that public opinion does good only when it succeeds in compelling servile conformity to itself is a very superficial view of its usefulness. To be under the eyes of others—to have to defend oneself to others—matters most of all to those who act against the opinion of others. For it obliges them to have firm grounds of their own. Nothing has so sobering and steadying an influence as working against pressure. Except when temporarily carried away by passionate excitement, no one will do what he expects to be greatly blamed for unless it comes from a settled purpose of his own, thought out beforehand. And such a purpose is always evidence of a thoughtful and deliberate character, and, except in the case of thoroughly bad men, generally springs from sincere and strong personal convictions. Even the bare fact of having to give an account of their conduct is a powerful inducement to people to keep to conduct of which at least some decent account can be given. If anyone thinks that the mere obligation of keeping up appearances is no great check on the abuse of power, he has never paid attention to the conduct of those who do not feel the need to observe that restraint. Publicity is of inestimable value. This holds even when it does no more than prevent what cannot by any means be plausibly defended—even when it does no more than compel deliberation, and force everyone, before he acts, to settle what he will say if called upon to account for what he does. "But (someone may say) if not now, then at least in the future, when everyone has become fit to have a vote and every man and woman is admitted to vote by virtue of that fitness, _then_ there can no longer be any danger of class legislation. The electors will then be the nation itself, and so cannot have any interest apart from the general interest. Even if individuals still vote according to private or class motives, the majority will have no such motives. And since there will then be no non-electors to whom they ought to be accountable, the effect of the ballot, which shuts out only bad influences, will be wholly beneficial. "Even on this point I do not agree. Even if the people were fit for universal suffrage and had obtained it, I cannot think the secret ballot would be desirable. In the first place, because under such circumstances I cannot think it would be needed. One need only picture the state of things the supposition implies: a people universally educated, and every adult in possession of a vote. If, even when only a small fraction are electors and the great majority of the population is barely educated, public opinion is already, as everyone now sees, the ultimate ruling power, then it is a delusion to imagine that, over a community in which all can read and all have votes, landlords and rich people could exercise, against the community's own inclinations, any power that it would find the least difficulty in throwing off. But though the protection of secrecy would then be unnecessary, the control of publicity would be as necessary as ever. If the mere fact of being a member of the community, and of not being in a position of marked conflict of interest with the public at large, were enough to ensure the performance of public duty without either the stimulus or the restraint that comes from the opinion of one's fellows, then the universal observation of mankind must have been very much mistaken. It has been found that a man's own particular share in the public interest, even when there is no private interest drawing him the other way, is not, as a general rule, enough to make him do his duty to the public without other, external inducements. Nor can it be granted that, even if all had votes, they would vote as honestly in secret as in public. "The proposition that when the electors make up the whole of the community they can have no interest in voting against the interest of the community will be found, on examination, to have more sound than meaning in it. Though the community as a whole can have (as the word implies) no interest other than its collective interest, any or every individual in it may. A man's interest consists of everything in which he _takes_ an interest. Everyone has as many different interests as he has feelings. That is, likings and dislikings, whether of a selfish kind or of a better one. None of these can be said, by itself alone, to constitute 'his interest.' He is a good man or a bad man according as he chooses one class of his interests or another. A man who is a tyrant at home will be apt to sympathize with tyranny (when it is not exercised over himself), and will almost certainly not sympathize with resistance to tyranny. An envious man will vote against Aristides because he is called the Just. A selfish man will prefer some trifling personal advantage to his share of the benefit his country would derive from a good law. For the interests peculiar to himself are the ones his habits of mind incline him to dwell on, and which he is best able to estimate. A great number of the electors will have two sets of preferences—those on private grounds and those on public grounds. The latter are the only ones the elector would wish to avow. What people like to show is the best side of their character, and this is so even toward people who are no better than themselves. People will cast dishonest or mean votes, from lucre, from malice, from pique, from personal rivalry, and even from the interests or prejudices of class or sect, more readily in secret than in public. And there are cases—and they may become more frequent—in which almost the only restraint upon a majority of knaves lies in their involuntary respect for the opinion of an honest minority. In a case like that of the repudiating states of North America, is there not some check on the unprincipled voter in the shame of looking an honest man in the face? Since all this good would be sacrificed by the ballot, even under the circumstances most favorable to it, a far stronger case than can now be made for its necessity (and that case is constantly growing weaker) is required to make its adoption desirable."[4] On the other disputed points connected with the mode of voting, there is no need to spend so many words. The system of personal representation, as organized by Mr. Hare, requires the use of voting papers. But it seems to me indispensable that the elector's signature be affixed to the paper at a public polling place, or, where no such place is easily accessible, at some office open to all, in the presence of a responsible public officer. The proposal that has been put forward, to let voting papers be filled up at the voter's own residence and sent by post, or else collected by a public officer, I should regard as fatal. The act would be done in a place where salutary influences are wholly absent and every harmful one is present. The briber, shielded by privacy, could see with his own eyes that his bargain was fulfilled, and the intimidator could see the obedience he had extorted rendered then and there in irrevocable form.
