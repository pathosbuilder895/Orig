It is a doctrine fit only for swine, and from a very early date the followers of Epicurus have been contemptuously likened to those swine. Modern people who hold this doctrine, too, are from time to time made the object of equally polite comparisons by its German, French, and English assailants. When attacked in this way, the Epicureans have always replied as follows: it is not they who portray human nature in a degrading light, but their accusers, since the accusation assumes that human beings are capable of no pleasures other than those swine can enjoy. If this assumption were correct, the charge could not be rebutted; but in that case it would no longer be a charge. For if the sources of pleasure were exactly the same for humans and for swine, a rule of life good enough for the one would have to be good enough for the other. Likening the Epicurean life to the life of beasts is felt to be degrading precisely because the pleasures of beasts do not satisfy the idea of happiness that human beings hold. Humans have faculties more exalted than animal appetites, and once they become aware of them, they do not regard as happiness anything that does not include the satisfaction of those faculties. I do not, to be sure, think that the Epicureans were by any means beyond reproach in deriving their system of consequences from the principle of utility. To do this in an adequate way, one must take in many Stoic elements along with Christian ones. But so far as is known, there is not a single Epicurean theory of life that fails to grant the pleasures of the intellect, of the feelings and imagination, and of the moral sentiments a far higher value, as pleasures, than the pleasures of mere sensation. It must be admitted, nonetheless, that utilitarian writers have generally located the superiority of mental over bodily pleasures chiefly in the former's greater permanence, safety, cheapness, and so on—that is, in their incidental advantages rather than in their intrinsic nature. And on all these points the utilitarians have amply proved their case. But they could also, while remaining perfectly consistent, have taken another and, so to speak, higher position. To recognize the fact that some _kinds_ of pleasure are more desirable and more valuable than other kinds is entirely compatible with the principle of utility. It would be absurd if, while quality as well as quantity is taken into account when we evaluate everything else, the evaluation of pleasure alone were thought to depend on quantity alone. If I am asked what is meant by a difference of quality in pleasures, or what, apart from being greater in quantity, makes one pleasure more valuable than another when regarded simply as a pleasure, there is only one conceivable answer. Of two pleasures, if there is one that all or nearly all of those who have experienced both clearly prefer, independently of any feeling of moral obligation to choose it, that is the more desirable pleasure. If one of the two is ranked so far above the other by those who know both well that they choose it even knowing it is accompanied by greater discontent, and would not give it up even if offered any quantity, however large, of the other pleasure their nature is capable of enjoying, we are justified in ascribing to the preferred enjoyment a superiority in quality so great that it far surpasses quantity and renders quantity, by comparison, insignificant. Now, it is an undoubted fact that those who know both equally, and are equally able to appreciate and enjoy both, show a most marked preference for a way of life that exercises their higher faculties. Few human beings would agree to be turned into some lower animal in exchange for the promise of the fullest measure of a beast's pleasures. No intelligent human being would agree to become a fool, no cultivated person would be willing to become ignorant, and no person with feeling and a conscience would be willing to become selfish and base, even if they were persuaded that the fool, the dullard, or the scoundrel is more satisfied with his lot than they are with theirs. They would not give up what they have more of than he does, even in exchange for the most complete satisfaction of every desire they share with him. If there is ever a time when they think they might be willing to give it up, it is only when they have fallen into unhappiness so extreme that, to escape it, they would exchange their lot for almost any other, however undesirable it might look in their own eyes. A being with higher faculties needs more to be happy than a being of an inferior type, is probably capable of feeling more intense suffering, and is certainly vulnerable to suffering at more points. But in spite of these weaknesses, he can never truly wish to sink down to what he feels is a lower grade of existence. We are free to give whatever explanation we like for this reluctance. We may put it down to pride, a name bestowed indiscriminately on some of the most honorable and some of the least honorable feelings that humankind can have. We may ascribe it to the love of liberty and personal independence; for the Stoics, appealing to this love was one of the most effective means of instilling it. Or we may ascribe it to the love of power or the love of excitement, both of which really do enter into it and contribute to it. But the name that best suits it is a sense of dignity. Every human being has it in some form, and it is to some degree, though never exactly, proportioned to that person's higher faculties. And it forms so essential a part of the happiness of those in whom it is strong that nothing which clashes with it could ever be, except momentarily, an object of desire for them. Anyone who thinks this preference is made at the expense of happiness—that is, who thinks that a superior being, in roughly equal circumstances, is no happier than an inferior one—is confusing two entirely different ideas, happiness and contentment. It is beyond dispute that a being with a low capacity for enjoyment has the greatest chance of having that capacity fully satisfied. And a highly gifted being, the world being constituted as it is, will always feel that any happiness he can hope for is imperfect. But he can learn to bear its imperfections, if they are at all bearable. And those imperfections will not make him envy a being who, it is true, is unconscious of them, but only because he does not feel at all the good that those imperfections limit. It is better to be a dissatisfied human being than a satisfied pig. It is better to be a dissatisfied Socrates than a satisfied fool. And if the fool or the pig is of a different opinion, it is because they know only their own side of the question. The other party to the comparison knows both sides. It may be objected that many people capable of the higher pleasures sometimes, under the influence of temptation, put them off in favor of the lower ones. But this is fully compatible with a complete recognition of the intrinsic superiority of the higher pleasures. People often, out of weakness of character, choose the nearer good even while knowing it to be the less valuable. And this happens when the choice lies between two bodily pleasures just as much as when it lies between a bodily and a mental pleasure. They pursue sensual indulgence to the point of damaging their health, though fully aware that health is the greater good. It may further be objected that many who set out with a youthful enthusiasm for everything noble sink, as they grow older, into indolence and selfishness. But I do not believe that those who undergo this very common change voluntarily choose the lower kind of pleasure over the higher. I believe that, before they come to devote themselves exclusively to the one, they have already become incapable of enjoying the other. The capacity for nobler feelings is, in most natures, like a very delicate plant, easily killed not only by hostile influences but by mere lack of nourishment. And in the great majority of young people, if the occupations their station in life has led them into, and the society their station has thrown them into, are not favorable to keeping that higher capacity in exercise, it quickly dies out. People lose their high aspirations in the same way they lose their intellectual tastes: because they have neither the time nor the opportunity to indulge them. And they come to indulge in lower pleasures not because they deliberately prefer them, but because these are either the only pleasures within their reach or the only ones they are still capable of enjoying. It may be doubted whether anyone who has remained equally sensitive to both kinds of pleasure has ever knowingly and calmly chosen the lower, though in every age many have broken down in the vain attempt to combine the two. From this verdict of the only qualified judges, I hold, there can be no appeal. On the question of which of two pleasures is most worth having, or which of two ways of life is most pleasant to the feelings, apart from its moral attributes and its consequences, the judgment of those qualified by knowing both, or, where they disagree, the judgment of the majority among them, must be accepted as final. And there is all the less need to hesitate in accepting this judgment on the quality of pleasures, since even on the question of quantity there is no other tribunal to appeal to. What means is there of deciding which of two pains is the sharpest, or which of two pleasant sensations is the most intense, other than the general vote of those who are well acquainted with both? Neither pains nor pleasures are homogeneous, and pain is always heterogeneous with pleasure. What is there to decide whether a particular pleasure is worth buying at the price of a particular pain, other than the feelings and judgment of the experienced? When, therefore, those feelings and judgment declare that the pleasures arising from the higher faculties are preferable _in kind_, apart from the question of intensity, to those that the animal nature, cut off from the higher faculties, is capable of feeling, they are entitled to the same respect on this question. I have dwelt on this point at length because it forms a necessary part of a perfectly correct conception of utility or happiness, considered as the rule guiding human conduct. But it is by no means an indispensable condition for accepting the utilitarian standard, since that standard is not the agent's own greatest happiness but the greatest amount of happiness altogether. And even if it may be doubted whether a noble character is always happier because of its nobility, there can be no doubt that it makes other people happier and that the world as a whole gains immeasurably from it. Utilitarianism, therefore, could achieve its aim only through the general cultivation of nobility of character, even if each individual benefited only from the nobility of others, and his own nobility, so far as happiness is concerned, were a sheer deduction from that benefit. But the mere statement of an absurdity like this last one makes refutation unnecessary. According to the Greatest Happiness Principle as explained above, the ultimate end, with reference to which and for the sake of which all other things are desirable (whether we are considering our own good or that of other people), is an existence as free as possible from pain and as rich as possible in enjoyments, in respect of both quantity and quality. And the standard of quality, and the rule for measuring quality against quantity, is the preference felt by those who are best equipped with the means of comparison, having been favored with opportunities for experience and having, in addition, habits of self-consciousness and self-observation. Since, according to the utilitarian view, this is the end of human action, it is necessarily also the standard of morality. Morality may therefore be defined as the rules and precepts for human conduct through the observance of which an existence such as has been described might be secured, to the greatest extent possible, to all mankind. And not to mankind alone: they are rules and precepts by which it might be secured, so far as the nature of things allows, to the whole of sentient creation. But against this doctrine there arises another kind of opponent. They say that happiness, in any form, cannot be the rational purpose of human life and action: first, because it is unattainable. And they ask contemptuously, "What right hast thou to be happy?" Mr. Carlyle clinches the point by adding to this question the phrase, "What right, until a short while ago, hadst thou even to _be_?" Next, they say that people can do _without_ happiness. All noble human beings have felt this, they say, and could not have become noble except by learning the lesson of Entsagen, or renunciation. And they maintain that thoroughly learning this lesson and submitting to it is the beginning and the necessary condition of all virtue. The first of these objections, if it were well founded, would go to the root of the matter. For if human beings could obtain no happiness at all, the attainment of happiness could be neither the end of morality nor the end of any rational conduct. Even in that case, though, there would still be something to say for the utilitarian theory, since utility includes not only the pursuit of happiness but also the prevention or mitigation of unhappiness. And if the former aim is a fantasy, there will be all the greater scope and all the more urgent need for the latter, at least so long as mankind thinks fit to live and does not take refuge in the act of collective suicide that Novalis recommended under certain conditions. But when it is asserted so categorically that it is impossible for human life to be happy, the assertion, if not something of a verbal sophistry, is at the least an exaggeration. If by happiness is meant a continuation of intensely pleasurable excitement, it is obvious enough that this is impossible. A state of exalted pleasure lasts only a fleeting moment, or in some cases, with some interruptions, a few hours or days; it is the occasional dazzling flash of enjoyment, not its lasting and steady flame. The philosophers who have taught that happiness is the end of life knew this as well as those who mock them. The happiness they meant was not a life of rapture, but an existence that contains such moments of rapture: an existence made up of few and transitory pains and many and varied pleasures, with a clear predominance of the active over the passive, and having, as the foundation of the whole, an attitude of not expecting more from life than life is able to give.
