Pagett—with a guilty secret! Splendid! Chapter Thirteen. It has been a very strange evening. The only costume in the barber's shop that fit me was a teddy bear's. I don't object to playing at bears with nice young girls on a winter evening in England—but it is hardly the costume for the equator. Even so, I was the life of the party and took first prize in the "brought on board" category—a ridiculous name for a costume hired for the evening. Not that it mattered, since nobody seemed to have the least idea whether a costume was homemade or brought on board. Mrs. Blair refused to dress up. Apparently on this matter she is of one mind with Pagett. Colonel Race followed her lead. Anne Beddingfeld had made herself a gypsy costume and looked exceptionally lovely. Pagett did not put in an appearance, pleading a headache. In his place I invited a peculiar little man named Reeves. He is a leading member of the South African Labour Party. A dreadful little man, but he gives me information I need, so I want to stay on good terms with him. I want to understand this Rand question from both sides. The dancing was a spirited affair. I danced twice with Anne Beddingfeld, and she had to pretend to enjoy it. I danced once with Mrs. Blair, who didn't even bother to pretend. After that I made victims of various other maidens whose looks I liked. Then we went below for supper. I had ordered champagne. The steward recommended the 1911 Clicquot as the best they had on board, and I fell in with his suggestion. It seems I had stumbled upon the one thing that loosens Colonel Race's tongue. So far from being silent, the man grew positively talkative. For a while I found this amusing, but then it struck me that it was Colonel Race, and not I, who was becoming the center of the table. He chaffed me endlessly about keeping a diary. "One of these days that diary will give away every one of your indiscretions, Pedler." "My dear Race," I said, "I venture to suggest that I am not quite the fool you take me for. I may commit indiscretions, but I don't put them down in black and white. After my death my executors will learn my opinion of a great many people, but I doubt they'll find anything to add to or subtract from their opinion of _me_. A diary is useful for recording other people's eccentricities—but not one's own." "Still, there is such a thing as unconscious self-revelation." "To the psychoanalyst, all things are unclean," I replied pompously. "You must have led a terribly interesting life, Colonel Race?" said Miss Beddingfeld, gazing up at him with big eyes shining like stars. That's how they do it, these girls! Othello won Desdemona by telling her stories, but ah, did not Desdemona win Othello by the way she listened? At any rate, the girl got Race nicely going. He began telling lion stories. A man who has shot a great many lions has an unfair advantage over other men. It seemed to me that it was high time I told a lion story myself. Something of a lighter kind. "By the way," I said, "that reminds me of a rather exciting story I heard. A friend of mine was out on a hunting trip somewhere in East Africa. One night he went out of his tent for something or other and was startled by a low growl. He spun round and saw a lion crouching, all ready to spring. He had left his rifle inside the tent. Quick as thought, he ducked, and the lion went sailing straight over his head. Furious at having missed its prey, the beast growled and crouched to spring again. Again he ducked, and again the lion flew right over him. The same thing happened a third time, but by then he had worked his way close to the opening of the tent, so he dived inside and snatched up his rifle. When he came out with the rifle in his hands, the lion had disappeared. This puzzled him greatly. He crept round to the back of the tent, where there was a little clearing. And there, sure enough, was the lion, hard at work practicing low jumps." This was received with a storm of applause. I drank a little champagne. "On another occasion," I said, "this same friend had a second odd experience. He was on a trek across country, and as he wanted to reach his destination before the heat of the day, he had his boys harness up while it was still dark. They had a good deal of trouble, because the mules were very restive, but at last they managed it, and off they went. The mules raced along like the wind, and when day broke he found out why. In the darkness, the boys had harnessed a lion into the left-hand shaft." This too was a success, and a ripple of laughter went round the table, but I rather think the highest compliment came from my friend the Labour member, whose face had stayed pale and serious throughout.
