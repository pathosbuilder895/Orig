Without knowing how, or why. There are whole families that have inherited legendary hatreds along with the suit. The little plaintiffs and defendants who were promised a new rocking-horse when Jarndyce and Jarndyce should be settled have grown up, possessed themselves of real horses, and galloped straight off into the next world. Fair wards of court have faded into mothers and grandmothers. A long procession of Lord Chancellors has come and gone, one after another. The suit's innumerable bills have turned into nothing more than weekly bills of mortality. Since old Tom Jarndyce, in his despair, blew his brains out in a coffee-house in Chancery Lane, there are perhaps not three Jarndyces left upon the earth. And still Jarndyce and Jarndyce, as hopeless as ever, goes on dragging its long, dismal body before the court. Jarndyce and Jarndyce has by now become a joke. That is the only good that has ever come of it. To many people it has meant death, but in the legal profession it is a joke. Every Master in Chancery has had a reference out of it. Every Lord Chancellor, in his days at the bar, was "in it" for somebody or other. Blue-nosed old benchers in bulbous shoes have said clever things about it at select port-wine gatherings after dinner in the hall of their Inn. Articled clerks have made a practice of trying out their legal wit on it. The previous Lord Chancellor handled it splendidly. When the eminent Queen's Counsel Mr. Blowers remarked that such a thing might happen if potatoes were to rain down from the sky, his lordship corrected him thus: "Or when Jarndyce and Jarndyce is settled, Mr. Blowers"—a pleasantry that particularly tickled the maces, bags, and purses. How many people outside the suit Jarndyce and Jarndyce has stretched forth its unwholesome hand to spoil and corrupt would be a very large question indeed. From the Master, upon whose spikes the dusty writs of Jarndyce and Jarndyce have been impaled by the ream, writhing dismally and twisting into all manner of shapes, down to the copying-clerk in the Six Clerks' Office who has copied out tens of thousands of Chancery folios under that eternal heading, not one person's nature has been improved by it. Under trickery, evasion, procrastination, plunder, botheration, and false pretenses of every kind, there lurks an influence that can never come to any good. Even the solicitors' errand boys, who since time out of mind have kept miserable suitors at bay by insisting that Mr. Chizzle, or Mr. Mizzle, or whoever it might be, was particularly engaged and had appointments until dinner, may have acquired an extra measure of moral twist and shuffle thanks to Jarndyce and Jarndyce. The receiver in the cause has gotten a good deal of money out of it, but has gotten along with it a distrust of his own mother and a contempt for his fellow men. Chizzle, Mizzle, and the rest have fallen into the habit of vaguely promising themselves that they will look into that little outstanding matter and see what can be done for Drizzle—the man was badly treated—once Jarndyce and Jarndyce is off the office's hands. Shirking and swindling, in all their many varieties, have been scattered broadcast by this ill-fated suit. And even those who have only watched its history from the outermost circle of the evil have been drawn, without knowing it, into the slack habit of letting bad things alone to run their bad course, and into the slack belief that if the world goes wrong, it was somehow never meant to go right in the first place. Thus, in the midst of the mud and at the heart of the fog, the Lord Chancellor sits in his High Court of Chancery. "Mr. Tangle," says the Lord Chancellor, who for some time now has seemed rather restless under that learned gentleman's eloquence. "Mlud," says Mr. Tangle. Mr. Tangle knows more about Jarndyce and Jarndyce than anybody. He is famous for it—he is supposed never to have read anything else since he left school. "Are you nearly done with your argument?" "Mlud, no—variety of points—feel it my duty to submit—'ludship," is the reply that slides smoothly out of Mr. Tangle's mouth. "There are several more of you gentlemen still to be heard, I think?" says the Lord Chancellor with a faint smile. Eighteen of Mr. Tangle's learned friends, each armed with a little summary of eighteen hundred sheets, pop up like the eighteen hammers of a piano, make eighteen bows, and sink back into eighteen inconspicuous seats. "We will continue the hearing on Wednesday fortnight," says the Lord Chancellor. For the point at issue is merely a question of costs, a single bud on the great forest tree of the parent suit, and it really will be settled one of these days. The Lord Chancellor rises. The bar rises. The prisoner is hurriedly brought forward. The man from Shropshire cries out, "My lord!" Mace, bag, and purse indignantly call for silence and glare at the man from Shropshire. "Now," the Lord Chancellor goes on, still upon Jarndyce and Jarndyce, "as to the young girl—" "Beg pardon, m'lud—boy," says Mr. Tangle, prematurely. "Now," the Lord Chancellor continues with particular distinctness, "as to the young girl and the boy, the two young people"—Mr. Tangle, flattened—"whom I directed to appear today, and who are now in my private room, I will see them and satisfy myself as to whether it is proper to make the order for their residing with their uncle." Mr. Tangle is on his feet again. "Beg pardon, m'lud—deceased." "Then with their"—the Lord Chancellor looking through his double eyeglass at the papers on his desk—"grandfather." "Beg pardon, m'lud—victim of rash act—brains." Suddenly, from the remote depths of the fog, a very little counsel with a terrifically deep voice rises, puffed up to his fullest, and says, "Will your lordship allow me? I appear for him. He is a cousin, several times removed. I am not at this moment prepared to inform the court in exactly what degree he is removed, but a cousin he most certainly is." Leaving this address (delivered like a message from the bottom of a grave) ringing in the rafters of the roof, the very little counsel sinks down, and the fog knows him no more. Everybody looks for him. Nobody can see him. "I will speak with both of the young people," the Lord Chancellor says once more, "and satisfy myself about their residing with this cousin. I will mention the matter tomorrow morning when I take my seat." Just as the Lord Chancellor is about to bow to the bar, the prisoner is presented. Nothing whatever can come of this prisoner's tangled case except his being sent back to prison, and that is soon done. The man from Shropshire ventures one more protesting "My lord!", but the Lord Chancellor, having been aware of him, has skillfully disappeared. Everyone else, too, swiftly vanishes. A battery of blue bags is loaded with heavy charges of papers and carried off by the clerks. The little mad old woman marches off with her documents. The empty court is locked. If only all the injustice this court has done and all the misery it has caused could be locked up along with it, and the whole lot burned away on one great funeral pyre—why, so much the better for other parties than the parties in Jarndyce and Jarndyce! Chapter II. The Fashionable World. On this same muddy afternoon, all we need is a brief glimpse of the fashionable world. The fashionable world is not so very unlike Chancery, and so we may pass from the one scene to the other in a straight line, as the crow flies. Both the fashionable world and Chancery are things of precedent and custom. They are, that is to say, Rip Van Winkles who have played at strange games through a great deal of thundery weather and overslept themselves, and sleeping beauties whom some knight will one day awaken. When that day comes, every spit that has stood still in the kitchen will begin to turn with prodigious force! It is not a large world. Even this world of ours has its limits (as Your Highness will discover when you have gone the round of it and reached the edge of the void beyond), and yet compared even with this world of ours, it is no more than a very tiny speck. There is much good in it. There are many good and true people in it. It has its appointed place. But the trouble with it is that, being a world wrapped up far too thickly in jewelers' cotton and fine wool, it cannot hear the roar of the larger worlds as they rush along, and cannot see them as they circle round the sun. It is a world whose senses have gone numb, and for want of air its growth is sometimes unhealthy. Lady Dedlock has returned to her London house to spend a few days before leaving for Paris. Her Ladyship intends to stay in Paris for some weeks, after which her movements are uncertain. So the society column reports, for the comfort of the Parisians, and the society column knows everything there is to know about the fashionable world. To know things otherwise would hardly befit the fashionable world. Lady Dedlock has been at her house in Lincolnshire, which in casual conversation she calls her "place in the country." There are floods in Lincolnshire. One arch of the bridge in the park has been soaked through, undermined, and swept away. The adjoining low ground has turned into a stagnant river half a mile wide, in which melancholy trees stand like islands, and its surface is pitted all over, all day long, by the falling rain. Lady Dedlock's house has been exceedingly dreary. It has rained for days and nights on end; the trees look as if they were soaked to the core, and the soft branches lopped off by the woodman's axe make no crack or snap as they fall. The deer look drenched through and leave quagmires behind them where they pass. The crack of a rifle loses its sharpness in the damp air, and its smoke drifts off in a small cloud, sluggishly, toward the green hill crowned with a copse that forms the background for the falling rain. The view from the windows of Lady Dedlock's own rooms is by turns a view in lead color and a view in India ink. The urns along the stone terrace in the foreground take the rain all day, and the heavy drops fall—drip, drip, drip—all night long upon the broad flagged pavement known of old as the Ghost's Walk. On Sundays the little church in the park smells musty. The oak pulpit breaks out in a cold sweat, and the whole place smells and tastes like the old Dedlocks in their graves. Lady Dedlock (who has no children), looking out at twilight from her boudoir toward the keeper's lodge, and seeing the firelight on the latticed panes, the smoke rising from the chimney, and a child, chased by a woman, running out into the rain to meet the shining figure of a well-wrapped man coming in at the gate, has been put quite out of temper. Lady Dedlock says she has been "bored to death." And so Lady Dedlock has come away from her place in Lincolnshire, leaving it to the rain, the crows, the rabbits, the deer, the partridges, and the pheasants. As the housekeeper went round the old rooms closing the shutters, the portraits of the departed Dedlocks seemed to fade away into the damp walls out of sheer low spirits. And when they will next appear again, the society column—which, like the Devil, is omniscient as to past and present, but not as to the future—cannot yet undertake to say. Sir Leicester Dedlock is only a baronet, but there is no mightier baronet than he. His family is as old as the hills, and infinitely more respectable. He is, broadly speaking, of the opinion that the world might get on without hills but would be done for without Dedlocks. He would on the whole be quite ready to admit that Nature is a good idea (a little vulgar, perhaps, when not enclosed by a park fence), but an idea, mind you, whose carrying out depends on the great county families. He is a gentleman of strict conscience, disdainful of all pettiness and meanness, and prepared at a moment's notice to die any death you may care to name rather than give the smallest occasion for a doubt to be cast on his integrity. He is an honorable, obstinate, truthful, high-spirited, intensely prejudiced, perfectly unreasonable man. Sir Leicester is a full twenty years older than my Lady. He will never see sixty-five again, nor probably sixty-six, nor even sixty-seven. He has a twinge of gout now and then and walks a little stiffly. With his light gray hair and whiskers, his fine shirt frill, his pure white waistcoat, and his blue coat with bright buttons always properly buttoned, he cuts an imposing figure. He is punctilious, dignified, supremely courteous to my Lady on every occasion, and holds her personal charms in the very highest esteem. His chivalrous courtesy toward my Lady has not changed in the least since the days when he courted her, and it is the one little romantic fancy in him. Indeed, he married her for love. There is a whisper still going round that she had not even family. Be that as it may, Sir Leicester had family enough and to spare, so perhaps that was sufficient, and he could do without any more. But she had beauty, pride, ambition, insolent resolve, and sense enough to portion out among a legion of fine ladies and still have some left over. With wealth and station added to these, she soon floated upward, and for years now Lady Dedlock has stood at the center of the society column and at the top of the fashionable tree. Everybody knows that Alexander the Great wept when he had no more worlds to conquer—or ought to know it by now, the story having been told rather often. Lady Dedlock, having conquered her world, fell not into a melting mood but rather into a freezing one. An exhausted composure, a worn-out placidity, an equanimity of fatigue that neither interest nor satisfaction can ruffle, are the trophies of her victory. She is perfectly well-bred. If she were to be called up to heaven tomorrow, she would probably ascend without any show of rapture. She still has beauty, and if it is no longer in its prime, it has not yet reached its autumn. She has a fine face—originally of the kind that would be called very pretty rather than handsome, but raised to something classical by the expression she has acquired as befits her station in the fashionable world. Her figure is elegant, and she looks tall. Not that she actually is tall, but, as the Honorable Bob Stables has often sworn, "she makes the most of all her points." The same authority remarks that she is perfectly got up, and, singling out her hair for praise, declares her the best-groomed woman in the whole stable.
