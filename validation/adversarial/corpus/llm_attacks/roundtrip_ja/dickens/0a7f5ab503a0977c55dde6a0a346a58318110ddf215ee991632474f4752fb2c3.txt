An old doll that had been buried in the garden for a long time. Chapter X. The Law-Writer. On the eastern borders of Chancery Lane, or, to be more precise, in Cook's Court, Cursitor Street, Mr. Snagsby, law-stationer, pursues his lawful calling. In the shade of Cook's Court, which is in shadow nearly all the time, Mr. Snagsby has dealt in every kind of blank form for legal proceedings, in skins and rolls of parchment, in paper—foolscap, brief, draft, brown, white, whitey-brown, and blotting—in stamps, office quills, pens, ink, India-rubber, pounce, pins, pencils, sealing-wax, and wafers, in red tape and green ferret, in pocket-books, almanacs, diaries, and law lists, in string boxes, rulers, and inkstands—glass and leaden alike—in penknives, scissors, bodkins, and other small office cutlery; in short, in articles too numerous to mention. He has done so ever since his apprenticeship ended and he went into partnership with Peffer. On that occasion the words "Peffer and Snagsby," in brand-new paint, displaced the old sign that read "Peffer" alone, time-honoured but none too easy to make out, and something like a revolution took place in Cook's Court. For smoke, which may be called the London ivy, had so wound itself about Peffer's name and clung so closely to his dwelling that the affectionate parasite had quite overpowered its host tree. Peffer is never seen in Cook's Court now. Nor is he expected there. For he has lain this quarter of a century in the churchyard of St. Andrew's, Holborn, past which the wagons and hackney coaches go roaring all day and half the night like one great dragon. If he does slip out while the dragon is resting, to take the air once more in Cook's Court until the crowing of the sanguine cock in the cellar of the little dairy in Cursitor Street admonishes him to go back—though what notions this cock holds about daylight I should very much like to find out, since, so far as his own eyes have shown him, he knows next to nothing about it—if Peffer ever does revisit the pale glimpses of the moon in Cook's Court (and no law-stationer in the trade can positively deny it), he comes invisibly, so that nobody is the worse for it, and nobody the wiser. In Peffer's lifetime, and all through the long seven years of Snagsby's "time," there lived with Peffer, in the same law-stationering premises, a niece—a short, shrewd niece, laced in a little too violently about the waist, whose nose was as sharp as a keen autumn evening and inclined to be frosty toward the tip. A rumour went round among the courtiers of Cook's Court that this niece's mother, when the girl was a child, had been moved by an over-zealous anxiety to bring her daughter's figure as near perfection as possible, and so every morning had planted her maternal foot against the bedpost, braced herself firmly, and laced the child up with all her strength. It was further said that she had given her pints of vinegar and lemon juice to take internally, and the acid, they held, had mounted to the patient's nose and temper. From whichever of Rumour's many tongues this frothy report issued, it either never reached young Snagsby's ears or, if it did, made no impression on him; for as soon as he came of age he courted the fair heroine of the rumour and won her heart, and so entered into two partnerships at once. And so now, in Cook's Court, Cursitor Street, Mr. Snagsby and the niece are one. And the niece still cherishes her figure. Tastes may differ, but there can be no doubt at least that the figure is precious in the sense of there being very little of it. Mr. and Mrs. Snagsby are not only one bone and one flesh but, in the opinion of the neighbours, one voice as well. That voice, which appears to proceed entirely from Mrs. Snagsby, is very often heard in Cook's Court. Mr. Snagsby's own voice is rarely heard except as it finds expression through these dulcet tones. He is a mild, bald, timid man with a shining head and a scrubby tuft of black hair sticking out at the back. He tends toward meekness and fat. Whether he is standing at his door in Cook's Court in his grey shop-coat and black calico sleeves, looking up at the clouds, or standing behind a desk in his dark shop with a heavy flat ruler in his hand, cutting and paring parchment in company with his two apprentices, he is unmistakably a retiring and unassuming man. At such times there often rise up from beneath his feet, as if from some shrill-voiced ghost that cannot rest in its grave, complaints and lamentations in the voice already mentioned. And when these reach a sharper pitch than usual, Mr. Snagsby will perhaps say to his apprentices, "I think my little woman is giving it to Guster!" This proper name, as Mr. Snagsby pronounces it, has before now stirred the wit of the courtiers of Cook's Court to remark that it ought rather to be Mrs. Snagsby's name, since in tribute to her stormy temper she, of all people, deserves to be called Guster (as in a gust of wind), with great force and expressiveness. The name, however, belongs to a lean young woman from a workhouse, and, apart from fifty shillings a year in wages and one very small box with a few clothes carelessly stuffed into it, it is the only thing she owns (she is supposed by some to have been christened Augusta). During her growing years she was put out to nurse, or rather farmed out by contract, with an amiable benefactor of the human race living at Tooting, so she must have grown up under the most favourable circumstances imaginable; nevertheless she "has fits," which the parish cannot account for. Guster is really twenty-three or twenty-four, but looks a good ten years older, and she is to be had cheaply on account of this unaccountable failing of fits. And she lives in such dread of being sent back into the hands of her patron saint that she is always at work, except when she is discovered with her head in the pail, or the sink, or the copper, or the dinner, or whatever else happened to be near her when the fit came on. She is a satisfaction to the parents and guardians of the apprentices, who feel there is little danger of her kindling tender feelings in the youthful breast. She is a satisfaction to Mrs. Snagsby, who can always find fault with her. She is a satisfaction to Mr. Snagsby, who thinks it a charity to keep her. In Guster's eyes, the law-stationer's establishment is a temple of plenty and splendour. The little drawing-room upstairs, which is always, so to speak, kept with its hair in curl-papers and its apron on, she believes to be the most elegant apartment in Christendom. The view it commands of Cook's Court at one end (not to mention a sidelong glimpse of Cursitor Street) and of the back yard of Coavinses, the sheriff's officer, at the other, she regards as a prospect of unequalled beauty. The oil portraits that hang in that room—and the oil has been laid on generously—the portraits of Mr. Snagsby looking at Mrs. Snagsby and of Mrs. Snagsby looking at Mr. Snagsby, appear in her eyes as masterpieces by Raphael or Titian. Guster, then, has some compensations for her many privations. Mr. Snagsby leaves to Mrs. Snagsby everything that does not belong to the practical mysteries of the business. She keeps the money, scolds the tax-collectors, appoints the time and place of Sunday worship, licenses Mr. Snagsby's amusements, and acknowledges no responsibility whatever for what she sees fit to put on the table for dinner. As a result she has become a high standard of comparison among the neighbouring wives a long way down both sides of Chancery Lane, and even out toward Holborn. Whenever there is a domestic skirmish, these wives invariably call on their husbands to look at the difference between their (the wives') position and Mrs. Snagsby's, and between their (the husbands') behaviour and Mr. Snagsby's. Rumour, which is forever flitting about Cook's Court like a bat and skimming in and out at everybody's windows, has it that Mrs. Snagsby is jealous and prying, that Mr. Snagsby is sometimes so harried that he can scarcely bear to stay in the house, and that if he had the spirit of a mouse he would never put up with it. It is even said that the very wives who hold him up as a shining example to their own headstrong husbands in fact look down on him, and that none looks down on him more loftily than a certain lady whose lord is more than suspected of wielding an umbrella on her as an instrument of correction. These vague whisperings, however, may arise from the fact that Mr. Snagsby is, in his way, a rather meditative and poetical man. In summer he likes to walk in Staple Inn and observe how rural the sparrows and the leaves are; and on Sunday afternoons he likes to loiter about the Rolls Yard and remark (when he is in good spirits) that there were fine old times in the old times, and that if you was to dig under that chapel, you'd be sure to turn up a stone coffin or two even now, and no mistake. He also comforts his imagination by thinking of the many Lord Chancellors, Vice-Chancellors, and Masters of the Rolls who have departed this life. And he tells his two apprentices that he has certainly heard it said that back when the Turnstile really was a turnstile, a brook "as clear as crystal" ran right down the middle of Holborn and led straight on into the meadows, and from this he gets a thoroughly rustic relish—gets so generous a relish of the country from it that he has not the slightest wish ever to go into the country itself. The day is drawing to a close and the gas has been lit, but as it is not yet fully dark, the gas is not yet doing its full work. Mr. Snagsby, standing at his shop door looking up at the clouds, sees a crow, out late, skim westward across the slip of sky that belongs to Cook's Court. The crow flies straight across Chancery Lane and Lincoln's Inn Garden into Lincoln's Inn Fields. Here, in a large house that was once the mansion of a person of rank, lives Mr. Tulkinghorn. It is let out in sets of chambers now, and in those shrunken fragments of its former greatness lawyers lie hidden like maggots in nuts. But the spacious staircases, passages, and antechambers still remain, and so do even the painted ceilings, where Allegory, in a Roman helmet and celestial linen, sprawls among balustrades and pillars, flowers and clouds, and big-legged boys, and makes the head of anyone who looks at her ache—which, more or less, seems always to be the object of Allegory. Here, among many boxes labelled with illustrious names, Mr. Tulkinghorn lives, when he is not lounging in speechless ease at the country houses where the great ones of the earth are bored to death. Today he is here, sitting quietly at his desk. He is an oyster of the old school, whom nobody can open. And just as his appearance is wholly himself, so his room, in the dusk of this afternoon, is just like him: rusty, out of date, withdrawn from notice, and able to afford it. Heavy, broad-backed, old-fashioned chairs of mahogany and horsehair, not easily lifted; obsolete tables on thin spindle legs, with dusty baize covers; presentation prints of the great noblemen of the last generation, or the one before it—these are what surround him. On the floor where he sits, a thick, dingy Turkey carpet muffles every footstep, and beside him stand two candles in old-fashioned silver candlesticks that light his large room very poorly. The titles on the backs of his books have retreated into the bindings. Everything that can have a lock has one. No key is anywhere in sight. Very few loose papers lie about. He has some manuscript near him, but he is not looking at it. With the round top of an inkstand and two broken bits of sealing-wax, he is silently and slowly working out some train of indecision in his mind. Now the inkstand top is in the middle, now the red bit of wax, now the black bit. That won't do. Mr. Tulkinghorn must gather them all up and begin again. Here, beneath the painted ceiling, with a foreshortened Allegory glaring down at his intrusion as if she were about to pounce on him, and he paying her no attention whatever, Mr. Tulkinghorn keeps both his home and his office. He has no servants. There is only one middle-aged man, generally a little out at elbows, who sits in a high pew in the hall and is seldom overburdened with work. Mr. Tulkinghorn is not a man of the common type. He has no need of clerks. He is a great reservoir of secrets, not to be drawn off by any such means. What his clients want is the man himself, and he is all in all. The drafts he needs drawn up are prepared by special pleaders in the Temple on mysterious instructions; the fair copies he needs are made at the stationers', expense being no object. The middle-aged man in the pew knows scarcely more about the affairs of the peerage than any crossing-sweeper in Holborn. The red bit, the black bit, the inkstand top, the other inkstand top, the little sand-box. So! You in the middle, you on the right, you on the left. This train of indecision must be worked out now, or it never will be. Now, then! Mr. Tulkinghorn rises, adjusts his spectacles, puts on his hat, puts the manuscript in his pocket, goes out, and tells the middle-aged man out at elbows, "I shall be back presently." He very seldom tells him anything more definite than that. Mr. Tulkinghorn goes, as the crow came—not quite so straight, but nearly—to Cook's Court, Cursitor Street. His destination is Snagsby's, Law-Stationer, Deeds Engrossed and Copied, Law-Writing Executed in All Its Branches, &c., &c., &c. It is somewhere about five or six o'clock in the afternoon, and a fragrant smell of hot tea hangs over Cook's Court. It hangs about Snagsby's door. The hours of this house are early: dinner at half past one and supper at half past nine. Mr. Snagsby was on the point of going down into the subterranean regions to take his tea, and had only just looked out of his door and seen the belated crow. "Master at home?" Guster is minding the shop, for the apprentices are taking their tea in the kitchen with Mr. and Mrs. Snagsby. Consequently, the robe-maker's two daughters, combing their curls before two looking-glasses at the two second-floor windows of the house opposite, are not captivating the two apprentices, as they fondly suppose, but merely awakening the fruitless admiration of Guster, whose hair will not grow, never has grown, and, it is firmly believed, never will. "Master at home?" says Mr. Tulkinghorn. The master is at home, and Guster will fetch him. Guster disappears, glad to be out of the shop, which she regards with a mixture of dread and veneration as a storehouse of the terrible instruments of the great torture of the law—a place not to be entered after the gas is out. Mr. Snagsby appears: greasy, warm, smelling of herbs, and chewing. He hurriedly swallows a bit of bread and butter. And he says, "Bless my soul, sir! Mr. Tulkinghorn!" "I want a word with you, Snagsby." "Certainly, sir! Dear me, sir, why didn't you send your young man round for me? Pray walk into the back shop, sir." Snagsby had brightened in an instant. The cramped little room, heavy with the smell of greasy parchment, is warehouse, counting-house, and copying-office all in one. Mr. Tulkinghorn sits down on a stool by the desk, facing round. "Jarndyce and Jarndyce, Snagsby." "Yes, sir." Mr. Snagsby turns up the gas and coughs behind his hand, modestly anticipating profit. Being a timid man, Mr. Snagsby is in the habit of coughing with a variety of expressions, and so saving words. "You copied some affidavits in that cause for me lately." "Yes, sir, I did."
