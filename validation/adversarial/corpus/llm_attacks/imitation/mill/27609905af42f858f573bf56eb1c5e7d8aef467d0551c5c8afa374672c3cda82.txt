There is a mode of reasoning, much in favour with those who are content with things as they are, which deserves a more particular examination than it commonly receives; I mean the inference that because a class of persons do not complain of their condition, they have no just ground of complaint. It is the argument of every privileged order against every subject one, and it has been urged with equal confidence, and I believe with equal sincerity, by the slaveholder, the feudal lord, and the head of a family. The serfs, we are told, were attached to their masters; the peasantry of half Europe would have been bewildered, and not gratified, by the offer of political rights; and the persons now excluded from the higher occupations of life are, for the most part, the very last who would wish to see the exclusion removed. I do not dispute the facts. I dispute the conclusion which is drawn from them.

For what, after all, is the value of the testimony? A desire cannot be felt for a thing of which there is no conception; and it is precisely the effect of a long-continued subjection to prevent the conception from being formed. Those who have never known any condition but one, cannot compare it with another; those who have been taught from their earliest years that their lot is the appointed one, and that to wish it otherwise is to rebel against nature, or against Providence, will seldom give utterance to a wish which they have been trained to regard as a sin. The absence of complaint, under such circumstances, proves nothing but the completeness of the training. When it is further considered that the few who do complain are exposed to every species of social penalty, from ridicule to ostracism, and that the many who might sympathise with them have the strongest motives for keeping their sympathy to themselves, the silence of a subject class is seen to be exactly what we should expect, whether their grievance be real or imaginary. A test which yields the same result on either supposition is no test at all.

It will be said, perhaps, that this reasoning proves too much; that it would license any reformer to set aside the expressed wishes of mankind, on the plea that they do not know what is good for them. I answer that it licenses nothing of the kind. I do not ask that any one should be compelled into a mode of life which he does not desire; I ask only that he should not be compelled out of one which he might desire, if he were left free to discover it. The two claims are not merely different; they are opposite. The one substitutes the judgment of the reformer for that of the individual; the other removes the obstacles which prevent the individual from forming any judgment of his own. Let the doors be opened, and those who prefer to remain within will remain; there is no fear that the customary dwelling will be forsaken by any who are sincerely attached to it. But if, when the doors are opened, a considerable number are found to pass through them, it will then appear what value was to be set upon the assurance that nobody wished to go.

There is, besides, a consideration which those who use this argument seldom face. If the existing arrangement is indeed agreeable to all whom it concerns, what need is there of laws and usages to enforce it? Nobody thinks it necessary to forbid people from doing what nobody wishes to do. The elaborate apparatus of disabilities, prohibitions, and penalties, by which the condition in question is maintained, is itself the strongest evidence that, in the opinion of those who maintain it, the condition would not maintain itself. Men do not build dykes against a sea which they believe to be at rest.
