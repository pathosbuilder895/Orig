It is commonly supposed that the chief obstacle to the improvement of mankind lies in the wickedness of some, or the ignorance of many; and that if the one could be restrained and the other enlightened, nothing further would be wanting. I believe the obstacle to be of another kind, and more difficult of removal, because it is not felt to be an obstacle at all. It is the disposition, which every generation inherits from the last and transmits to the next, to take the established opinion upon any subject as the measure of what is reasonable to be thought upon it. Wickedness is at least recognised for what it is; ignorance, when it is pointed out, is not generally defended as a merit. But the habit of accepting what is received because it is received, is not only not condemned; it is praised, under the names of sobriety, modesty, and a becoming deference to the wisdom of our ancestors.

I do not undervalue the wisdom of ancestors. The experience of mankind is a great inheritance, and he would be a very foolish person who should set out to examine every practice of life afresh, as if nobody had ever lived before him. But there is a wide difference between using the experience of others as evidence, and using it as a substitute for thought. In the first case it is weighed, and may be found wanting; in the second it is not weighed at all. The man who has adopted a rule of conduct because his reason, informed by the experience of others, approves it, holds that rule in a manner wholly different from him who holds it only because it is the rule of the persons among whom he lives. The former knows on what grounds it rests, and can tell when those grounds have ceased to apply; the latter knows nothing of the matter, and will continue to observe the rule long after every reason for it has passed away, and to resent, as an outrage on decency, any suggestion that it should be reconsidered.

Our modern education does much to confirm this tendency, and very little to correct it. The young are taught a great many opinions; what they are scarcely ever taught is the art of forming an opinion for themselves. They learn what is thought, not how thinking is to be done. They are furnished, at the most impressible period of life, with conclusions on every subject of importance—morals, politics, religion, the relations of the sexes and of classes—and are carefully kept from the knowledge that any of these conclusions has ever been, or is now, seriously disputed by persons as competent as their instructors. It is then a matter of surprise to the instructors that their pupils, when they come to maturity, either cling to what they were taught with a blind and irritable obstinacy, or, upon the first serious encounter with an opposing argument, throw the whole of it away together. Neither result ought to surprise anybody. A belief which was never defended can neither be defended nor understood; and the mind which has received it passively has acquired, along with the belief, no faculty by which to judge between it and any other.

If it be asked what remedy I propose, I answer that the remedy is not a new set of opinions, to be taught in the same manner as the old. That would be merely to exchange one dominion for another. The thing required is that the grounds of every opinion should be laid open, and the objections to it stated in their strongest form, by those who believe them, or by those who can represent them as such persons would. Until this is done, it cannot be said that any one, however correct his conclusions, holds them as a rational being ought to hold them. He holds them as a prejudice, which happens, it may be, to be true.
