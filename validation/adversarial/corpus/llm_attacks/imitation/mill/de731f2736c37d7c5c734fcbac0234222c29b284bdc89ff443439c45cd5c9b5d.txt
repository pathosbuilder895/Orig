It is commonly urged, as if it were decisive of the whole question, that those who live under any established usage do not, for the most part, complain of it; and that it is officious, if not impertinent, in persons who are not themselves aggrieved, to discover grievances on behalf of others who profess to feel none. I do not deny that there is a certain plausibility in this. Where a burthen is really intolerable, it may be expected that those who bear it will sooner or later make their sufferings known; and the absence of complaint is, in ordinary cases, some presumption that the evil is not very great. But the presumption is of the weakest kind, and it vanishes altogether when we consider what the complaint would cost, and what the persons concerned have been taught to think of it. No one remonstrates against a yoke which he has never been permitted to imagine himself without. He has heard from his infancy that it is the natural and appointed condition of his lot; every person whose good opinion he values has assured him that to murmur against it would be ingratitude, and to resist it, something worse; and he has seen, in the fate of the few who have murmured or resisted, a sufficient illustration of what such conduct is likely to bring upon himself. The silence which results from all this is not contentment. Or if it be contentment, it is the contentment of those who have ceased to desire what they have been told is beyond their reach; which is no more a proof that they would not be happier with it, than the indifference of a man born blind to the beauty of colours is a proof that sight is of no value.

Consider, too, by whom the testimony is collected, and under what conditions it is given. The persons who report that the subject class are satisfied are almost invariably members of the class which rules; and the occasions on which they receive this assurance are precisely those on which it would be most imprudent to offer any other. The planter was never weary of informing the world that his slaves were the most cheerful labourers on earth; the Russian proprietor said as much of his serfs; and these accounts were believed by many honest persons at a distance, until the event disclosed how much had been kept from them. It is not that the masters were in every case deceivers. They were more often deceived; for nothing is so easy to believe as that others are pleased with an arrangement which is agreeable to ourselves, and nothing is so carefully hidden from a superior as the feelings which he would resent if he saw them.

I am far from asserting that all who acquiesce in a custom are secretly in revolt against it. Most, doubtless, acquiesce sincerely--and many would be shocked to hear that they had anything to complain of. But the question is not whether they are now discontented; it is whether they would remain content if the alternative were fairly placed before them, and they were at liberty to choose it without forfeiting the esteem of every one about them. This is a question which experience alone can decide, and which experience can never decide so long as the trial is forbidden. To prohibit the experiment, and then to cite the want of any result as evidence that none would follow, is to reason in a circle of which the defenders of every ancient abuse have in their turn availed themselves. There is but one test which is not a begging of the question, and that test is freedom. Let the door be opened, and the burthen be made voluntary; it will then appear, and it can appear in no other way, how many prefer to remain where they are.
