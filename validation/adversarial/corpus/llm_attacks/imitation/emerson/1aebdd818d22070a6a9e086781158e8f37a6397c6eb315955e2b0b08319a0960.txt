We read, it is said, to be instructed; but I suspect we read rather to be reminded. The book that delights me is that which says, with a better tongue, what I had already half-heard in my own breast, and had not the courage to say aloud. Every great writer is a kind of confessor of the race; he steals our secret, and publishes it, and we thank him for the theft. I open Montaigne, and find there my own Tuesday; I open Plato, and he tells me, as if he had lodged under my roof, what I thought at dawn and forgot by noon. This is the secret of the reverence paid to literary genius. It is not his learning we admire, which a diligent clerk might equal, but his audacity in trusting what all men privately know.

Yet there is a danger in this sweet commerce, and it is a danger proportioned to the excellence of the book. The vigorous reader is a creator; the indolent reader is a captive. A strong mind, a Bacon or a Milton, goes into a library as a bee into a garden, and comes out with honey of his own making; but the most of us go in as sheep go into a fold, and are counted, and shut up. We love to lean. It is easier to quote than to see. Hence the tyranny of the classic, the long dominion of a few names over the general thought, which nature, ever impatient of a fixed estate, takes pains to break. She sends a new man, who has not read the books, or has read them to no purpose of submission; he speaks from the facts, and the old shelf trembles.

I would not, therefore, set a man to his books before I had set him to his fields. Let him first plant and reap, and keep a few sheep, and bargain at the market, and learn what the weather is, and what a debt is, and what a neighbor is. Then let him read, and he shall know what he reads. The farmer who has sat up three nights with a sick ox knows more of Job than the commentator. Experience is the dictionary by which all books are construed; without it, the noblest page is a cipher, and the reader a pedant who carries the key-ring and has no door.

There is a second rule, which is, read with a pencil, and quarrel with your author. He is not your master, but your provocation. Whatever page does not stir you to answer it, has done you no service. I like a reader who goes away vexed and stung, better than one who goes away satisfied; for the vexation is the first sign of an independent mind. The best praise a book can have is that it set us to write another.

And at last, the use of books is to make books unnecessary. The child spells his letters that he may forget them in the sense; the scholar reads his library that he may outgrow it. When the soul is awake, a single sentence suffices; when it sleeps, the Bodleian is a desert. We are not to hoard the thoughts of other men, as a miser his coins, but to spend them, and strike new ones from our own metal. Plato and Shakspeare are the dew, and not the harvest; they quicken the ground, but the corn must be ours. Whoso reads aright shall find in every author a door opening outward, upon the same sky which shone on the author himself; and he shall go through that door, and not return to thank the porter.
