The day is a plain messenger, and we seldom suspect what he carries. He knocks at the door in the same gray coat he wore yesterday, with the same parcels under his arm,—the wood to be split, the letter to be answered, the neighbor to be civilly put off, the field to be looked to,—and we take our share from his hands with a yawn, as if nothing of value could travel in so homely a livery. Yet the gods have no other road to our houses. They do not come in chariots; they come on errands. The farmer who stands at evening in his furrow, and sees the shadow of the woodlot lengthen across the meadow, has had his audience, though he cannot report it, and would be ashamed to try.

We are always waiting for the great occasion, and the great occasion is always waiting for us to cease waiting. The young man supposes that his life will begin when he has a profession, or a wife, or a house of his own, or a hearing in the newspapers. He puts off his manhood, as a debtor puts off his creditor, with fair words and a date. But the creditor is patient, and the date never comes. Meantime the hours go over him like geese over the marsh, each carrying southward something he will not see again. Who told him that the hour of his greatness would be announced? Greatness is not announced. It slips in at the back door in the dress of a duty, and sits down unnoticed among the children.

I find it everywhere true that the best is hid in the most common. The diamond lies in the clay, and the clay is not ashamed. The cobbler at his bench, if he be honest, practises a geometry which Euclid would not disown; the woman at the loom weaves her own temper into the cloth, and the buyer, who never saw her face, is the better or the worse clothed for it. Every trade is a text, if we had the wit to read it. There is a theology in the plough, an ethics in the ledger, and a whole cosmogony in the potter's wheel, which turns the shapeless lump into the vessel by a law that the potter obeys and did not make. What is the secret of the master workman? Only this,—that he has consented to be taught by his material. He does not quarrel with the grain of the wood. He waits upon it, and it serves him.

Why then should the scholar shut himself in his chamber, and go on all fours among the folios, as if wisdom were a fox gone to earth there? The books were written by men who went out of doors. Socrates talked by the Ilissus; Montaigne rode his horse and gossiped with his neighbors; the Psalmist kept sheep. I would not undervalue the library; it is a good servant. But it is a good servant only to a master. Let a man first be at home in his own perception, and then every volume is his diary, written beforehand in a better hand. If he is not at home, the whole alcove of the ancients is but a waiting-room, where he sits with his hat on his knees, expecting to be called.

The law is simple, and therefore hard. Put your hand to the work, and the thought will meet you there. Withhold the hand, and the thought withholds itself. The muse is a strict housekeeper, and sets no plate for the idler. But to him who comes in from the field with honest dust upon his shoes, she brings what she has, and it is always enough. He shall know, without a teacher, that the soul is never hurried, that there is time for all which it is fit to do, and that the day, which seemed so poor a messenger, was all the while the ambassador of a king.
