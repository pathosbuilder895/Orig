Prudence is the virtue of the senses. It is the science of appearances, the outmost action of the inward life; and as such it deserves neither the contempt of the saint nor the worship of the shopkeeper, but a just place in the house, where it may sweep the floor and keep the accounts. I would not speak ill of it. The man who pays his bills, who knows the price of wood and the hour of the tide, who mends the fence before the cattle are in the corn, is so far in harmony with the laws of the world, and the world will bear him out. Yet there is a prudence which is only a polite name for fear. It counts the cost of every generous act, and finds the cost too high. It insures the barn, and lets the soul burn. It is wise in trifles, and a fool in the main chance. Of this sort is the man who lays up his money against old age, and contrives that he shall have nothing else to lay up; who will not walk in the rain lest he take cold, and so never sees the woods in their best hour.

The true prudence begins where the false ends,—namely, in a deference to the nature of things. Nature is the great economist. She wastes nothing, and she spares nothing. She will have her pound of flesh for every excess; the glutton pays in the liver, the sluggard in the joints, the hoarder in the heart. But she pays as punctually as she exacts. Whoso obeys her, to him she gives health, and a clear eye, and the free use of his hands. I have seen a farmer in our town who read no books, and could hardly sign his name, whose whole life was a treatise on prudence. He rose with the sun, and went to bed with it. He planted when the oak-leaf was as big as a squirrel's ear. He knew his land as a mother knows the faces of her children, which field was cold, which was sour, which would bear rye and which would bear nothing but huckleberries. He never ran in debt, nor ran after a bargain. What he had he had paid for, in coin or in sweat. There was a dignity in him which a senator might envy, for he stood on his own ground, and owed no man an apology.

The prudence of the farmer is admirable because it is the shadow of a higher law, and not because the barn is full. Let a man once see that his corn and his credit are symbols, and that the same justice which balances his ledger balances the stars, and his thrift becomes a kind of worship. Then he is not prudent from fear, but from love. He keeps his house in order, as the planets keep their orbits, because order is the condition of power. The spendthrift and the miser are alike blind. The one thinks the world owes him a living; the other thinks he can buy it off. Both are bankrupt in the coin that alone passes current with the gods.

Hence the paradox, that the most prudent man is often thought the most rash. The hero who throws away his life for a principle has calculated better than the actuary. He has seen that the life is not worth keeping on the terms offered, and that nothing is lost which is given to the right. Socrates drinking the hemlock is the shrewdest bargainer in Athens. He trades a few years of old age for the reverence of all ages. The martyr is the true economist. Let us learn of him that the soul has its own arithmetic, in which to lose is often to gain, and to spend is to save; and that the highest prudence is to be faithful to our own thought, and let the event take care of itself.
