The car set us down before the porch of Harlowe Grange a little after ten. It was a long, low house of grey stone, very quiet in the morning sun. Inspector Crowle, a big, slow-spoken man with a pair of very shrewd blue eyes, met us in the hall, and greeted Poirot with a respect which I thought did him credit.

“I’ll not pretend I sent for you, M. Poirot,” he said frankly. “It was Mrs. Harlowe’s notion. But I’m not above taking help where it’s offered, and that’s the truth.”

“You are wise, monsieur,” said Poirot, bowing. “And the affair, it is as they told me on the telephone?”

“Pretty much. Old Mr. Harlowe was found in the library at seven this morning by the housemaid. Sitting at his desk, as natural as you please, and stone dead. Doctor says it was his heart, and very likely he’s right. But the window was unfastened, and there’s a drawer of the desk been forced. That’s what I don’t like.”

“No,” said Poirot thoughtfully. “No, that is not pleasant.”

We followed the Inspector down a short passage and into the library. It was a sombre room, lined from floor to ceiling with books. The body had already been removed. Poirot stood for a moment in the doorway, his head a little on one side, surveying the scene like a bird. Then he trotted across to the desk.

“This is the drawer?”

“That’s it. Prised open with a paper-knife, by the look of it. The knife’s there on the blotter.”

Poirot examined the drawer, the knife, and the blotter with the utmost care. Then, to my astonishment, he turned his attention to the inkstand, a massive affair of brass and glass, and lifted the lid of each well in turn.

“Poirot,” I said, a little impatiently, “surely the inkstand can have nothing to do with it?”

“You think not, Hastings? See, then. This well is full to the brim. And this one, it is empty, and quite dry—yet the pen that lies beside it has been used this morning. Look, the nib is still wet.”

“Well?” I said, puzzled.

“Well, my friend, a man does not dip his pen in an empty well. Either the ink was poured away after the pen was used, or the pen was dipped elsewhere. In either case, somebody has been writing at this desk since Mr. Harlowe died—and has taken some pains that we should not know it.”

The Inspector scratched his chin. “I don’t see that it gets us much further, M. Poirot.”

“Perhaps not. Perhaps it gets us a great deal further. We shall see.” He moved to the window, and examined the catch, and then the sill, and finally the gravel path beneath. When he turned back to us, his eyes were as green as a cat’s.

“Tell me, Inspector, who in this house was on bad terms with Mr. Harlowe?”

Crowle laughed shortly. “You’d do better to ask who wasn’t. His nephew had words with him only last night—the butler heard them right across the hall. And there’s the secretary, Miss Vane, who was to be given her notice at the end of the month.”

“Ah!” said Poirot softly. “And the nephew, he is in the house now?”

“In the morning-room, and none too pleased at being kept there.”

“Then we will not keep him waiting longer than is necessary.” Poirot paused by the mantelpiece, and with a little frown set straight a china shepherdess that stood a fraction out of line with its fellow. “But first, Hastings, I should like a word with the housemaid who found him. It is always the servants, you comprehend, who see the little things—and it is the little things, _mon ami_, that matter.”

I could not help smiling. I had heard that maxim, in one form or another, a great many times. But I was to have reason, before the day was out, to remember it.
