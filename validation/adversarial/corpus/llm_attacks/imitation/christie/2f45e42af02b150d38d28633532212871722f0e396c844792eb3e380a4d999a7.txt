It was close upon eleven when Poirot and I presented ourselves once more at the Villa. The morning had turned grey and blustering, and a fine rain was driving in from the sea, so that I was glad enough to step into the warmth of the hall. Françoise, the old maid-servant, admitted us. Her eyes were red, and she clutched a handkerchief in one hand, but she seemed, I thought, rather gratified than otherwise to see us.

“Monsieur le juge is not here,” she informed us. “He has gone into the town with M. Bex. And the other one—the detective from Paris—he is in the garden, crawling about upon his knees like a dog.”

“Ah!” said Poirot, with a twinkle. “Then we shall not disturb him. It is you, mademoiselle, to whom I wish to speak.”

Françoise, who could not have been a day under sixty, bridled visibly at the “mademoiselle,” and led the way into the little sitting-room behind the dining-room. Poirot seated himself, and arranged the cushions at his back with great care before he spoke.

“Tell me, Françoise, on the night of the crime, at what hour did you lock up the house?”

“At ten o’clock, monsieur, as always. Monsieur Renauld did not like the house to be left open.”

“And the windows of the study? You fastened them yourself?”

“Every one, monsieur. I swear it.”

“And the little door that gives on to the kitchen garden?”

The old woman hesitated. It was the merest flicker, but I saw it, and I knew that Poirot saw it too.

“That door—yes, monsieur, I locked it.”

“You are quite sure?”

“Quite sure,” said Françoise, but her fingers twisted the handkerchief round and round.

Poirot said nothing for a moment. Then he leaned forward and patted her hand in the kindliest manner imaginable.

“Listen, my good Françoise. I am not of the police. Nothing that you say to me shall bring you into trouble. But the truth, it is very important. Did you, perhaps, leave that door open for some one?”

There was a long pause. Then, to my astonishment, the old woman burst into tears.

“It was for my nephew, monsieur,” she sobbed. “He works at the hotel in Merlinville, and he comes sometimes late in the evening for a little supper. Madame knew nothing of it. I left the bolt drawn back for him, and when he did not come I forgot all about it. I was so tired, monsieur. And then in the morning—oh, the horror of it! I dared not say.”

“Your nephew did not come, then?”

“No, monsieur. He told me afterwards that he had been kept late at the hotel.”

Poirot thanked her gravely, and assured her once more that she need fear nothing. When she had gone, he sat for some minutes in silence, staring at the opposite wall.

“Well,” I said at last, unable to contain myself, “that explains everything, doesn’t it? The door was left open by accident, and the murderers simply walked in.”

“You think so, Hastings?”

“Surely it’s obvious.”

“It is obvious, yes. That is precisely what troubles me.” He rose and went to the window, through which the figure of Giraud could be seen, bent double over a flower-bed in the rain. “Consider, my friend. Here is a door, unbolted by the merest chance, on one night of all the nights in the year. And the assassins, they go straight to it. They do not try the windows. They do not try the front door. They know.”

“They may have been watching the house.”

“Possibly.” Poirot turned from the window, and there was a gleam in his green eyes that I had learned to recognize. “Or possibly some one told them. And that, _mon ami_, is a very different affair.”

I confess I did not altogether follow his reasoning, but I was too proud to say so.
