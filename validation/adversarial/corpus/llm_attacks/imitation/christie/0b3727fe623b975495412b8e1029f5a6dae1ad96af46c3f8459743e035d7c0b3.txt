It was not until the following morning that I had an opportunity of speaking to Poirot alone. I found him in the little sitting-room of the inn, carefully brushing a speck of dust from his hat, and he greeted me with every appearance of cheerfulness.

“Ah, Hastings! You have not slept, I perceive. You have been reflecting. And to what conclusion have you arrived?”

I hesitated. The truth was that I had arrived at a great many conclusions, each of which had seemed admirable at two o’clock in the morning, and none of which survived the light of day.

“It seems to me,” I said at last, “that everything turns upon the library window. If it was fastened when the housemaid went round at eleven, then someone inside the house must have opened it afterwards.”

“Precisely. And was it fastened?”

“She swears that it was.”

“Then she swears it. That is one thing. Whether it was so is another.” He set down the hat with great care. “Tell me, did you observe the curtains?”

“The curtains? No.”

“They were drawn, my friend. Most closely drawn. Now, Mr. Maitland sat in that room every evening with his papers—he was a man of habit, all the household agree upon it—and never once did he trouble about the curtains. Yet on this one night someone draws them very carefully. Why?”

“Because it was cold, I suppose.”

Poirot sighed. “You suppose. Always you suppose, and always the simplest thing. Sometimes, I grant you, the simplest thing is the true one. But not, I think, this time.”

He would say no more, and presently we set out for Fernley Lodge. The lane was full of puddles, and Poirot picked his way among them with an expression of acute distress, pausing more than once to examine his patent-leather boots.

We were admitted by the parlourmaid, a pale, frightened girl named Ellen. Poirot was at his most benevolent with her.

“Do not alarm yourself, _mon enfant_. Last night, when you went round to lock up, the master was not in the library?”

“No, sir. He’d gone up. The lamp was out.”

“Then how did you see to fasten the window?”

The girl stared at him. “Why—there was the moon, sir. Shining right in.”

“Right in,” repeated Poirot softly. “So at eleven o’clock the curtains were not drawn?”

“No, sir. I remember thinking how bright it was.”

She withdrew, and I turned to him eagerly.

“Then someone drew them after eleven!”

“Evidently. Someone who did not wish to be seen from the garden—or who did not wish something in this room to be seen.” He crossed to the writing-table and stood looking down at it, his head a little on one side, like a bird. “Observe, Hastings. The inkstand, the blotter, the pens in their tray. Everything is in order. It is too much in order.”

“I don’t see anything wrong in that.”

“No? But a man who is struck down at his desk does not first tidy his pens.” He lifted the blotter and held it to the light, turning it this way and that, and gave a little grunt of satisfaction. “Fresh,” he murmured. “Quite fresh. And written in haste.”

“What is it?” I cried, craning over his shoulder.

But Poirot had already detached the sheet, folded it, and slipped it into his pocket-book.

“Patience, _mon ami_. It is not yet the moment to speak. But this much I will tell you—the curtains were not drawn against the cold.”
