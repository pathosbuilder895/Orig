It was on a raw Saturday in November, with the fog lying in the streets like wet wool, that I first presented myself at the house of Mr. Snaggs, in Coldbath Square. I had been told by my uncle, in a letter of three lines and a great blot, that Mr. Snaggs was “a warm man, and particular,” and that I was to wipe my boots. I wiped them, I think, for a full minute upon the scraper and another upon the mat, and was still wiping them when the door was opened by a small, hard, elderly woman in a cap like a jelly-bag, who looked first at my boots and then at me, as if she had the poorer opinion of the two.

“You’re the young man,” said she.

I said I believed I was.

“Then you’re to come in sideways,” said she, “on account of the hall.”

The hall, when I had come in sideways, proved to be entirely given over to umbrellas. There were umbrellas in a stand, and umbrellas against the wall, and umbrellas laid out along a bench like a row of drowned bats; and every one of them—as the old woman informed me in passing—had been left behind by somebody who had owed Mr. Snaggs money, and had never come back for it. “He keeps ’em,” said she, with grim satisfaction, “against the day.”

What day that might be, I did not venture to inquire.

Mr. Snaggs received me in a back parlour so low and so dark that the fire seemed to have been lighted chiefly to be looked at. He was a lean gentleman of sixty or thereabouts, with a long upper lip and a pair of very small grey eyes set close together, like two buttons sewn on in a hurry; and he sat in a chair with arms, with his hands folded upon his waistcoat, and his thumbs going round and round each other, slowly, as though they were winding something up.

“Name?” said Mr. Snaggs.

I gave it.

“Age?”

I gave that too, and added, from a nervous wish to be agreeable, that I should be eighteen at Candlemas.

“Never mind Candlemas,” said Mr. Snaggs. “Can you write a fair hand?”

I said I hoped so.

“Hoping,” said Mr. Snaggs, with his thumbs going round, “is not writing. Many a man has hoped himself into the Fleet. Show me.”

He pushed towards me a sheet of paper and a pen so worn that it seemed to have spent many years in signing away other people’s property, and I wrote my name, and then, at his bidding, the words “Received with thanks,” which he appeared to consider the finest in the language. He held the paper very close to one of his eyes, and then very close to the other, and then laid it down and put his hand flat upon it, as if it might otherwise get up and go away.

“It’ll do,” said Mr. Snaggs. “Mrs. Pike, the young man will take a glass of water.”

I have often reflected since, that this glass of water was the only refreshment I ever received under that roof; and it was handed to me by Mrs. Pike with such solemn and reluctant generosity, as if it had been drawn at great expense from a private well, that I could scarcely bring myself to drink it. I drank it, however, every drop; Mr. Snaggs watching me with his thumbs at rest, which I afterwards learnt was the closest attention he was capable of bestowing.

When I came out again into the fog, sideways, past the umbrellas, I found that I had engaged myself, at fifteen shillings a week, to a man of whom I knew nothing but that he was warm and particular; and I had walked half the length of Holborn before it occurred to me that he had never once smiled, nor told me whether I was to come on Monday.
