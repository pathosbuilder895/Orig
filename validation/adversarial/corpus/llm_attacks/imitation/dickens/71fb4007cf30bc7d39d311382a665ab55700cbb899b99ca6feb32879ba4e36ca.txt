It was a raw, black evening at the beginning of November when I first presented myself at Mr. Tumbrell's door in Gower Place; and I remember that the knocker, which was a brazen lion with a ring in its mouth, appeared to me to have swallowed something that disagreed with it, and to be brooding over its revenge upon the first comer. I knocked, notwithstanding, and was admitted by a very small servant in a very large cap, who looked at me as if I were a bill that had fallen due before its time, and showed me upstairs.

Mr. Tumbrell was a gentleman of sixty or thereabouts, with a bald head that shone as though it were rubbed up every morning along with the rest of the mahogany, and two grey tufts of whisker that stood out on either side of his face like the brushes of a chimney-sweep. He had been in the hop trade, I understood, and had retired from it with a competency, a cough, and a settled persuasion that every person who came into his house came there to borrow. This persuasion he never put into words; but he put it into everything else, and most particularly into his manner of shaking hands, which he did by giving me two fingers and immediately taking them back again, as if he had lent them on doubtful security.

His sister, Miss Jemima Tumbrell, kept his house, and agreed with him. I do not mean that she agreed with him occasionally, or upon reflection, or after argument; I mean that she agreed with him before he had well finished speaking, and sometimes before he had begun, so that I came in the course of the evening to regard her as a kind of echo that had got into the wrong end of the room and was trying to work its way round to the front.

We dined upon a leg of mutton, which Mr. Tumbrell carved with the gravity of a man dividing an estate among undeserving relations.

'You'll take a little of the outside, Mr. Lorrimer?' said he, poising the knife. 'Most people prefer the outside. I never could tell why. There's no more nourishment in it.'

'Not a particle,' said Miss Tumbrell.

'Whichever is most convenient, sir,' said I.

'Convenient!' repeated Mr. Tumbrell, laying down the knife and looking across at his sister. 'Do you hear him, Jemima? Convenient!'

'I hear him, brother,' said Miss Tumbrell; and she looked at me as though I had said something in Latin, and something not quite proper.

I received the outside, and was much ashamed of it.

Throughout the meal I observed that Mr. Tumbrell's eye travelled, at intervals, to a small oval picture which hung above the chimney-piece, between a pair of china shepherdesses who were evidently not on speaking terms. It was the likeness of a boy of nine or ten, in a blue jacket with a great white collar, holding a hoop; and whenever his eye rested on it, the hard lines about his mouth relaxed, and his hand, which had been drumming on the cloth, lay still. Once, when the small servant set the decanter too near the edge of the table, he put it back with an odd gentleness, and said, in quite another voice than any he had used to me, 'Mind, Susan. Mind.'

I did not know then—how should I have known?—what that picture was, or why the shepherdesses kept their faces turned from it, or why Miss Tumbrell, for the only time that evening, said nothing at all when her brother looked that way. I was young, and very full of myself, and I thought him a mean old man, and went home thinking so through the wet streets. But I have learned since that the meanest-seeming houses are often the most haunted; and that a man may hoard his candle-ends, and grudge his visitor the outside of the mutton, and yet keep, somewhere behind his waistcoat, a store of tenderness that he has never once been able to spend.
