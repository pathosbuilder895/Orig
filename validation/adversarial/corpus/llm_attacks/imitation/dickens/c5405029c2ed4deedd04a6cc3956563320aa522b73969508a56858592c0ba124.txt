It was a narrow house in a narrow street that ran down towards the river, and it had the look, from the pavement, of having been squeezed between its neighbours until it could scarcely draw breath; so that I fancied, as I stood upon the step, that the very knocker was gasping. Mr. Pinchbeck opened the door himself. He was a short, round, rosy gentleman, with a bald head that shone as if it had been polished for the occasion, and a pair of small bright eyes that went over me, from my hat to my boots, exactly as I have since seen a pawnbroker’s go over a watch.

“Come in, come in, my young friend,” said Mr. Pinchbeck, rubbing his hands. “Mind the step. Everybody minds the step in this house, and nobody has broke a leg upon it yet; which I take to be a great argument in favour of minding it.”

I minded it accordingly, and was shown into a little parlour where a fire was burning with such fierce determination that it seemed to resent the smallness of the grate. A round table was laid for four. Two ladies were already seated at it: the elder, Mr. Pinchbeck’s sister, a tall, bony, upright person in a cap with a great many lavender ribbons, which trembled whenever she spoke, as if they were afraid of her; and the younger, her niece, a pale little creature with large eyes, who looked at the tablecloth through the whole of that evening as though she expected something to be written there.

“My sister, Miss Pinchbeck,” said my host. “My niece, Miss Lavinia. Lavinia, my dear, this is the young gentleman from the counting-house.”

“Indeed!” said Miss Pinchbeck, before her niece could open her lips. “From the counting-house! Then he is very welcome, I am sure, if he can count. Most young men nowadays cannot count, Brother. They count _upon_ things; which is quite another matter.”

We had a boiled leg of mutton with caper sauce, and I observed that Mr. Pinchbeck carved it as though he were dividing an estate among a number of disappointed relations, with a great show of justice and a very small result. When he had done, he laid down the knife, and surveyed his own plate with profound satisfaction.

“I have always held,” said he, “that a man’s character may be known by his mutton. Show me a man who takes the fat, and I will show you a man who pays his debts.”

I had taken the fat—not upon principle, but because it had been put there—and I felt myself rise so sensibly in his estimation, that I had not the courage to leave any of it.

From mutton the conversation travelled, I hardly know how, to the wickedness of the age; and there Miss Pinchbeck kept it, through the pudding and the cheese. Once, and once only, Miss Lavinia ventured to look up from the tablecloth and say, in a small voice, that she had heard the new bridge was very beautiful by moonlight.

“By moonlight!” cried Miss Pinchbeck, and every ribbon on her cap rose up in arms. “And who, pray, has been telling you about moonlight?”

Miss Lavinia went back to the tablecloth directly, and the colour came into her face and went out of it again, like the fire in the grate when the door was opened. Mr. Pinchbeck, who had been helping himself to walnuts, said nothing at all; but he winked at me over the nutcrackers, in a manner so very knowing and so very kind, that I was quite at a loss to understand which of the two he meant it to be.

I walked home that night by the river, thinking a great deal more of the tablecloth than of the mutton, and wondering, as young men will, what Miss Lavinia had expected to find written there. I know now; but it was a long while before I learnt to read it.
