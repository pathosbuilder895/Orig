There is a certain sort of modern reformer who is never so pained as when he sees a poor man enjoying himself. He does not object to the poor man being poor; he has written several excellent pamphlets proving that it is inevitable. What he objects to is the poor man being extravagant; and the poor man is never so extravagant as at the moment when he is happy. I have heard an eminent Fabian lament, with real emotion, that a family in Battersea had spent upon one Christmas dinner a sum which, laid out scientifically, would have kept them in nourishing lentils for a month. It never seemed to occur to him that the family did not want lentils for a month. They wanted a goose for a day. And in wanting a goose for a day they were in agreement with every civilization that has ever existed, while he was in agreement only with the lentils.

The argument against the feast is always that it is wasteful; and the argument is perfectly true. A feast is wasteful in exactly the sense in which a poem is wasteful, or a wedding, or a cathedral. It is the one thing in the calendar that is not done for the sake of something else. Economy is a means; and a means is a very respectable thing, like a ladder. But the modern economist has fallen into the queer habit of climbing the ladder for the sake of climbing it, and of being shocked when anybody stops at the top. He saves in order to save. He would have the poor man put by his pence, not that he may some day spend them gloriously, but that he may some day have put by more pence.

Then the reformer falls back upon a gentler proposal. He says that the poor man may have his pleasure, so long as he spreads it evenly over the year; a little plum in every slice of bread, instead of a whole pudding on the twenty-fifth of December. But a thing spread evenly is not a thing at all. A festival diffused is a festival destroyed. You cannot have Christmas every day, any more than you can have a birthday every morning; a man who had a birthday every morning would be the oldest man in England by Wednesday. The whole point of a holiday is that it is a holy day; that is, a day set apart; and you cannot set a thing apart by mixing it in with everything else.

Lastly, the reformer appeals to health; he says that rich food is bad for the digestion. Very likely it is. But nobody, not even a hygienist, really wants to be healthy. He wants to be healthy enough to do something; and the something, if you trace it back far enough, is generally something very like eating a goose. A man wants good legs in order to walk to the inn. If the doctor then forbids him the inn, his legs have become as meaningless as the legs of a table.

The truth is that the old world understood a thing which the new world has forgotten: that the fast and the feast are two halves of one human act. The man who never fasts cannot really feast; he can only eat. Christendom flung its whole weight upon the one day by denying itself through the days before it; and the denial was not the enemy of the pleasure, but its sauce. The modern man neither fasts nor feasts. He dines. He has abolished at once the abstinence and the riot, and has left nothing in their place but a moderate and perpetual meal.

And so the reformer, who thinks he is rescuing the poor from their follies, is only rescuing them from their festivals. But a man who has been rescued from all his festivals has been rescued from the only days he could call his own.
