There is a kind of modern man who is very proud of having no walls to his mind. He tells us, with the air of one confessing a virtue, that he is open to every view and closed to none; that he has no prejudices, no dogmas, and no fixed opinions; that he stands, as it were, in the middle of a great plain and lets every wind of doctrine blow through him. I do not doubt his sincerity; I only doubt his description. For a thing through which every wind can blow is not a house at all, but a ruin; and nobody ever admired the view from a ruin except because some other man had once built it and put windows in it.

The truth is that a window is the most dogmatic thing in architecture. It exists only because there is a wall; take away the wall, and you have not made a larger window, you have abolished the window altogether. The man who sits in a field does not see the sunset more truly than the man who looks at it through a casement; he sees it, if anything, less, for he has nothing to frame it with, and a sunset that fills the whole sky is merely weather. A cottage with one small window looking west has more of the glory of evening in it than all the open moors of Yorkshire; because the cottager has chosen where to look, and the moor has not.

Now this is exactly the mistake of what is called the broad mind. It supposes that because conviction limits vision, the absence of conviction must enlarge it. But it does not enlarge it; it dissolves it. A man who believes that some things are true can look at all the things that are false with a lively and even affectionate curiosity, as a man safe in his own house can look with pleasure at a storm. But the man who believes nothing cannot even enjoy the storm; he is out in it. He has no hearth from which to watch the lightning; he has only the lightning.

I have often noticed that the people who boast of tolerance are the least able to tolerate anything in particular. They can tolerate Buddhism in Tibet and Mahometanism in Arabia, because those things are a long way off and look picturesque in a magazine. But let a Methodist grocer at the corner of the street express a strong opinion about the Sabbath, and they are filled with a horror very like the horror of the Inquisition, except that the Inquisition had the decency to give reasons. Tolerance of this kind is not a virtue but a distance. It is the charity of the telescope; it loves mankind exactly in proportion as mankind is too small to be seen.

The old tolerance rested upon something quite different; it rested upon the fact that a man was sure of something. Because he was sure that his own soul was worth saving, he was sure that his neighbour's soul was worth saving; and therefore he could argue with his neighbour, and quarrel with him, and even (in extreme cases) agree with him. You cannot quarrel with a man unless you both believe there is something to quarrel about. Two sceptics can never have a real argument; they can only have a polite exchange of shrugs. It was the believers who made the universities and the long, loud, beery disputations of the mediaeval taverns; for they alone thought that the answer mattered. The modern world has abolished the quarrel and called it peace; but it is the peace of men who have nothing to defend.

Let us then, by all means, have open minds; but let us remember what a thing is opened for. A gate is opened that the cattle may come home; and a gate that is never shut is not a gate at all, but a gap in the hedge. The man who boasts that his mind will never close has only confessed that it was never hung upon its hinges.
