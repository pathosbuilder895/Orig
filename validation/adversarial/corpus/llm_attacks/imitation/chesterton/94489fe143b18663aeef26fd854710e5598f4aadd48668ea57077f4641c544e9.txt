There is a phrase continually upon the lips of our reformers, and it is the phrase "leave it to the expert." It is commonly uttered in a tone of great humility, as if the speaker were confessing his own ignorance; but it is really uttered in a tone of great pride, for he always means that somebody else ought to confess it. The man who says that education must be left to the expert never means that he will not have opinions about education; he means that the mother shall not have them. Now I have no quarrel with the expert in his own place, and his own place is a very honourable one. If my house is on fire I would rather it were put out by a fireman than by a philosopher; though I should not object to the philosopher holding the hose, provided he held it the right way round. But there is a whole class of human things in which the expert is not merely unnecessary but impossible; and they are precisely the things that matter most.

Consider for a moment what it would mean to have an expert in friendship. A man who was professionally qualified to be a friend would be, by the very nature of the qualification, no friend at all; he would be a sort of solicitor who called on Tuesdays. The essence of a friend is that he is not chosen for his efficiency; he is chosen for himself, which is a much more mysterious and much less marketable thing. It is the same with a father, a lover, or a citizen. No man can be a specialist in being a citizen, any more than he can be a specialist in being alive. The moment we begin to talk of trained citizens, as some of our more eager educationists already do, we have really ceased to believe in citizens, and have begun to believe in a sort of civil service with votes.

This is the real and rather terrible truth behind the cry for efficiency. Efficiency means getting a thing done; but the things most worth doing are worth doing not because they get done, but because somebody does them. A child who makes a mud pie is not trying to supply the market with pies; and the baker, who could make a much better pie, cannot make that one. A man who sings in his bath is not competing with the opera; if he were, he would be wise to stop. The amateur is the man who loves the thing; that is what the word means, and it is significant that we have turned it into a word of contempt. We have come to think that a man who loves a thing must do it badly, and that a man who does it well must do it for money. It would be truer to say that only the man who loves the thing can be trusted to do it at all.

I do not deny that the amateur is often clumsy. He is clumsy because he is free. The professional cricketer must keep his eye upon the averages; the small boy on the common may keep his eye upon the ball, or upon the sky, or upon the remarkable dog that has run off with the stumps. He loses the game; but he has had what the professional has long forgotten, which is a game. Nor is this a trivial instance; all the great instances are like it. Democracy itself is only the amateur in politics. It is the doctrine that the ordinary man should govern, not because he governs well, but because it is his own affair; just as he should propose to his own wife, though a hired matchmaker might propose much more gracefully. The modern world would hire a man to do the proposing. It has very nearly done so already.
