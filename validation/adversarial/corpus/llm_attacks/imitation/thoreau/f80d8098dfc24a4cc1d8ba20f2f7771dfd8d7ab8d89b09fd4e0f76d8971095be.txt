I have lately been employed by a neighbor to run the lines of his woodlot, which lies on the farther side of the Sudbury road, where it borders on the land of three other men, none of whom, it appeared, was quite satisfied with the opinion of his fathers as to where his own possessions ended. The old deed, which he brought out to me from a tin box under the bed, spoke of a heap of stones, and a white oak marked, and a stake standing in the corner of a swamp; but the oak had been cut these forty years, the stake had long since rotted and gone to feed the cranberries, and as for the heap of stones, there were a dozen such in the neighborhood, every one of which, in the opinion of the man who owned the land beside it, was the true and original monument. So I set up my compass, and sighted along the needle, which points, as men say, to the north,—though it is notorious that in Concord it points a little to the west of north, and a little more every year, as if even the magnet had caught something of the westward fever. It is the only instrument I know of that is perfectly honest and yet never quite right.

All the forenoon I went chaining through the woods, with a boy to carry the forward end, crossing brooks and blazing the trees as I went, which were thus made to bear witness, against their will, to a quarrel in which they had no interest. A chickadee followed us a good part of the way, and seemed to think that we had come out for his entertainment; and I suspect that he had the better title to the lot than any of the four claimants, for he had lived on it, and gotten his living out of it, and paid no tax. When we came out at last upon the meadow, and found the heap of stones where the bearing said it should be, my employer was as pleased as if he had found a pot of money; for it gave him about a rod and a half more of swamp than he had thought he owned, all of it, so far as I could see, fit only for frogs and red maples.

I do not wish to make light of a man's property. I know that it is to him what his shell is to the tortoise, and that he would sooner part with a limb than with an acre. But it seemed to me, as I sat on the stone wall and ate my lunch, that the lines which I had been at such pains to draw were drawn on nothing but paper, and on men's minds; and that the woods knew nothing of them. The squirrel did not inquire whose nut he was burying, nor the partridge whose log he drummed upon; and the snow which will fall there next month will fall alike on both sides of the line, and lie as white on my neighbor's rod and a half as on the rest. We say that a man owns his land; but I think it is oftener the land which owns the man,—keeps him at work upon it, sends him to the town-meeting to defend it, and buries him at last in a corner of it, under a slate stone, as if to make sure of him. The farmer who has walked his bounds for fifty years is hardly more free than the stake at the corner of his swamp.

For my part, I would rather survey the sky, where no lines have yet been run, and where the stars keep their places without a deed. Yet I took my fee, which was two dollars, and was glad of it; for a man must eat, even while he philosophizes, and I have found that surveying feeds me as well as beans, and with less hoeing.
