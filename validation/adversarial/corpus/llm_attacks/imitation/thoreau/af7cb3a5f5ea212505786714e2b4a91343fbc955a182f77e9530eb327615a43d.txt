Last week I was hired by a neighbor to survey a woodlot which he had lately bought of the heirs of another man, and which, as is common in these parts, no one living could bound with any certainty. The old deed spoke of a heap of stones, a white oak marked with three notches, and “the corner of Jonas Brown’s land,” as if Jonas Brown and his corner were as lasting as the hills; but Jonas had been in the graveyard these fifty years, the white oak had long since gone up some kitchen chimney, and the heap of stones, when at length we found it, was overgrown with moss and brambles, and a woodchuck had made his house beneath it, and held it, I thought, by a better title than either of us. So I spent two days dragging my chain through the swamp and up the side of the hill, while my employer followed at my heels, anxious lest he should lose a rod of what he had paid for, and caring very little what grew upon it. He wished to know how many acres he owned; I wished rather to know what owned the acres.

A farmer will walk the bounds of his lot perhaps once in his life, and then only with a surveyor at his side and a lawsuit in his eye; but the fox walks them every night, and the partridge drums upon the old wall in April as if he were the true proprietor, and would have the neighbors witness it. The deed lies in the registry at Cambridge, but the title is written all over the lot in a hand which no lawyer has learned to read. There were lichens on the bounds older than the Commonwealth, and a little company of the stemless lady’s-slipper (_Cypripedium acaule_) blushing under the pines, which not one of the heirs had ever seen, though they had quarrelled over the timber for twenty years. I found where the deer-mouse had laid up his acorns in a hollow stump, a granary more honestly filled than many in the village.

When I had run the last line and driven my stake, my neighbor asked, with some concern, whether it came out as the deed said, forty acres, more or less. I told him it was more or less, and that I thought it would bear a good deal more if he would only let it alone. He did not take my meaning, and I did not press it,—for he had his note at the bank to think of, and I had none.

It is remarkable how little of the land in this town is owned by anybody, in the sense in which a man owns his coat or his thoughts. We are called a nation of freeholders; but I see that most of my townsmen are held by their farms rather than holding them, and the true lord of many a manor in Middlesex is some gentleman in Boston who never set foot upon it, and could not tell a hemlock from a pine. A man buys a woodlot that he may cut it, and when he has cut it he sells the stumps for what they will fetch, and calls the difference his gain; and the assessors agree with him. But who has reckoned what was lost,—the shade in which the brook kept cool, the thrush’s evening hymn, the slow work of a century which no man’s labor contributed to and no man’s money can restore? These do not appear in the inventory of his estate, yet they were the only part of it worth inheriting.

But let no man mistake the paper for the possession. It were well if we surveyed our lives as carefully as we survey our acres, and went back now and then to find where the old corners were, before the stones are buried and the marked trees cut down. For most of us, I fear, have long since lost the bounds of what is truly our own, and are content to hold it, like my neighbor his woodlot, more or less.
