Every autumn the county holds its cattle-show, and the farmers come in from Acton and Carlisle and Stow with their best oxen, their swollen squashes, and their wives’ quilts, to be weighed and measured and set in rows under a tent, as if the harvest were a thing that could be summed up in a committee’s report. I went over one year, partly from curiosity and partly because the road lay that way,—for I seldom go anywhere on purpose,—and saw a yoke of steers which had been fed till they could hardly carry themselves, and a man who had raised a pumpkin of a hundred and thirty pounds standing beside it with as much pride as if he had begotten it. The premium was three dollars. I do not grudge him his three dollars; but I could not help thinking that the pumpkin had done the greater part of the work, and the sun the rest, and that neither of them was invited to the dinner afterward. There was a ploughing match also, and a brass band, and a minister who asked a blessing on the whole, though I did not learn that he had ever held a plough himself, unless it were the plough of the Lord, which turns a very shallow furrow in this county.

What struck me most was what was not there. There was no premium for the best huckleberry field, nor for the most melodious wood-thrush, nor for the man who had walked farthest that year and seen the most. No committee had been appointed on wild apples. Virgil, who wrote the best agricultural report that I know of, found room in it for the bees and the stars as well as for the oxen; but our reporters have no room for anything that cannot be fattened. Yet I know of a tree in a pasture beyond the Corner Road, which no man ever pruned or grafted, whose fruit is sourer than vinegar in October and sweet as cider in February, if you can find it under the snow; and I would not exchange a peck of it for all the Baldwins on the tables. The farmer measures the worth of his land by what he can cart away from it; but the best crops are those which cannot be carted, and which grow only for him who comes on foot. They are not to be had at the store, nor sent to Boston by the cars. You must go to them, and go often, and at the right season, or not at all.

I would have a cattle-show of another kind,—a show of men rather than of cattle, where the premium should go, not to the fattest ox, but to the leanest and soundest life. Let us see who has lived most simply and most freely this year; who has owed least and given most; who heard the first bluebird in March, and kept his appointment with the sunrise. It would be a thin exhibition, I fear, and the tent would not need to be large. Perhaps a single bench would serve, and that not crowded. But the committee would have a rare and difficult task, for the virtues of such men are not easily weighed, and do not show well in a pen.

As I came home in the dusk, I passed a field where an old farmer was still digging his potatoes by lantern-light, having missed the show altogether; and I thought that he had perhaps chosen the better part. The band was still playing faintly behind me over the hills, like a swarm of gnats in the evening air; but before me the frost was already settling in the hollows, and the stars came out one by one over Nawshawtuct, as they have done every night since the Indians named it, and no premium was offered for them either. They did not seem to require it.
