What shall I say, O Lord my God, of those tablets of wax whereon as a boy I first learned to form my letters? For this also I remember, and it is not unprofitable to confess it unto Thee, that the remembrance may become a praise. My master set before me the shapes of the letters, and I, with the stylus pressed into the wax, essayed to follow them; and when I had erred, I turned the instrument, and with its blunt end smoothed the wax again, that I might write anew. And this I did often, and wept often, and was beaten often; and yet I loved not the smoothing so much as I feared the stripes which my errors would bring upon me. Nor did I then perceive, poor child, what Thou wert even then writing in me, Thou who hadst set within my breast that very desire to make known by signs what was hidden in my heart.

But now, O my Light, I consider those tablets, and I find in them a figure of my soul, which Thou didst fashion soft and fit to receive impression, that it might bear Thy image. And what did I write therein, in all those years? Not Thy law, nor Thy name, but the names of vanities; and I pressed them so deep, by custom upon custom, that the wax grew hard about them, and would no longer yield. For the wax, while warm, is easily smoothed; but grown cold, it cracks rather than receives a new form. So was it with me. What I had written in my youth, I could not unwrite by mine own hand, for the hand that should have smoothed it was the same hand that had pressed it in, and it loved what it had written.

Who then shall make the wax warm again? Who, but Thou, O Fire that warmest all things, and art Thyself never spent? Thou didst hold my hardened heart near unto Thee, and by Thy heat it softened, not suddenly, but by little and little, that it might not melt away altogether and be lost. And then didst Thou turn Thy stylus, as it were, and smooth what was crooked, and write again upon me the letters which I had forgotten, or rather had never learned. Yet I confess to Thee, Lord, that the marks of my former writing are not wholly gone. For in my memory, which is as a vast storehouse, I find them still, faint, but legible, as when a tablet has been smoothed in haste, and the old lines show through beneath the new. And these traces trouble me in the night, and in the day solicit me, saying, "Read us again."

Shall I then complain, that Thou hast not utterly blotted out what I wrote? Far be it. For Thou knowest wherefore Thou leavest these traces; perhaps that I may not glory, as if I had never written thus; perhaps that, reading what I was, I may the more love Him who made me otherwise. For he that sees no scar forgets that he was wounded; but he that sees it remembers the Physician. Let them remain, then, if so Thou willest; only let them not rule; let them be read, not obeyed; and let Thy writing, which is above them, be the clearer by the contrast, as letters stand out the better upon a tablet that is not new.

And wherefore do I set down these things before Thee, who knewest them ere they were done? That my brethren, who are yet boys in Thy school, may not despair, though their wax be hardened and their letters crooked. For Thou art the Master who beatest not to wound, but to teach; and Thy cane is mercy, and Thy smoothing is pardon, and Thy writing is life eternal.
