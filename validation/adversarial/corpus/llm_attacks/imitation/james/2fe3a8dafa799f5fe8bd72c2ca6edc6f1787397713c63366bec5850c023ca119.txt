There is a phenomenon so common in the religious life that it is seldom thought worthy of remark, and yet it seems to me to deserve a moment’s attention before we pass on. I mean the gradual cooling of the convert’s ardor. The man who has come through his crisis, and for whom, as he will tell you, the heavens were for a season opened, finds after a few months or years that the glow has departed from his days. He still holds his creed; he still attends upon the ordinances; he would be scandalized to hear himself called a backslider; but the sense of a living presence, which once made every hour significant, has shrunk to a mere memory, and his religion has become, if I may so express it, a matter of book-keeping rather than of fire. “I know that I was changed,” one of my correspondents writes, “and I would not go back to what I was for all the world. But I can no more feel to-day what I felt that winter than I can feel the warmth of a stove that was put out ten years ago. I live on the credit of it.”

Now the critic of religion is apt to seize upon such confessions with a certain satisfaction. See, he says, how your vaunted regeneration was nothing but an explosion of the nerves—a fever which ran its course and left the patient very much where it found him. If the experience had revealed anything real, it would surely have abided; that it fades proves it to have been an affair of the organism and nothing more.

But let us look at the argument a little more closely, for it is one of those which seem forcible only so long as we refrain from applying them elsewhere. What experience of our lives, I should like to ask, does _not_ fade in just this way? The lover’s rapture subsides into the habitual tenderness of the household; the young man’s first sight of the sea, or of the Alps, can never be had a second time; the discoverer who once sat up all night trembling over his new law comes at last to teach it to sophomores as a matter of course. We do not on that account conclude that the woman was not lovable, or the mountains not sublime, or the law untrue. We say rather that the emotion was the door by which the truth came in, and that, the truth once housed, the door may well be shut. Our whole mental economy is so constructed that what was at first an acute perception sinks by degrees into a chronic disposition; and the disposition, though it no longer thrills, may be the more efficacious of the two in the long run, for it shapes conduct silently and without the expenditure of fresh excitement.

It is just so, I think, with the cooling of the convert. The heat was needed to melt the old habits and to fuse the scattered elements of his character into a new mould; but a mould once set does not require to be kept forever at the melting-point. The fruits, if they are there, are the test. If the man is still more patient, more honest, more serviceable to his neighbors than he was before, then the vanished glow has done its work, and done it, perhaps, better than if it had lingered on to exhaust him.

I do not say that nothing is lost. Something is lost, and the sufferer knows it—there is a real poverty in living on the credit of past hours, and the saints themselves have bitterly lamented their “dry” seasons. But a loss of feeling is one thing, and a refutation of what the feeling disclosed is another; and the two must not be confounded by any one who wishes to judge these matters fairly.
