It will be asked, perhaps, what advantage can accrue to the individual student from the presence in a University of sciences which he does not himself pursue. He cannot master them all; he has neither the time, nor the strength, nor, it may be, the inclination; he is destined for the bar, or for medicine, or for the service of the State, and it is enough for him that he be well furnished for his own calling. Why then, Gentlemen, should we trouble ourselves about studies which lie beyond his horizon? I answer, that a man may profit by much which he does not directly make his own; and that the benefit of which I speak is not so much the acquisition of this or that portion of knowledge, as the formation of a certain habit of mind, which no single science can give, and which is the fruit, not of possession, but of companionship.

Consider how it is with a traveller who journeys through a country of which he knows nothing but the road before him. He may keep that road with exactness; he may note every milestone, every bridge, every turning; and yet, if he has never seen a map of the whole, he is at a loss the moment he is obliged to leave it. Give him, on the other hand, a general apprehension of the country, its rivers, its ranges, its provinces and their boundaries, and, though he travel by the same road, and see no more with his eyes than before, he travels with an intelligence which the other has not; he knows where he is, what he is passing by, and what he is not seeing. So it is with the student. He takes up one science, or two, as his calling requires; but, living in the midst of others, hearing them spoken of, meeting those who profess them, and gaining some notion, however imperfect, of their scope and method, he learns, almost without knowing that he learns, the place which his own pursuit holds in the general system of knowledge, and the limits beyond which it cannot pass without trespassing on ground not its own.

This is no small gain. It is the very safeguard against that narrowness and self-sufficiency which is the besetting peril of the man of one study, and which makes him at length the dupe of his own science. One who has been brought up among many studies will not lightly suppose that any one of them is the key of all; he has seen too much of their mutual dependence, and of the correction which one applies to another. He will distinguish between what his science proves and what it merely assumes; and, when it is put forward as answering questions which do not belong to it, he will be the first to detect the usurpation.

But now, Gentlemen, suppose one great province of knowledge to be withdrawn from this companionship; suppose the map put into the student's hands to be one from which an entire region has been cut away, not because it is barren or uninhabited, but because those who drew the map could not agree upon its boundaries. What follows? Not merely that he is ignorant of that region; that were a defect, but a tolerable one; but that he is taught, by the very form of the map, to think there is no such region at all. The rivers which flow out of it will seem to rise from nowhere; the roads which cross into it will seem to end in the void; and the whole geography of what remains will be thrown out of its true proportions. He will reason confidently about the parts he knows, and his reasonings will be correct as far as they go; but they will go farther than they ought, and he will not suspect the error, because the evidence of it has been put out of his sight.
