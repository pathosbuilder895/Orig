Of all the lessons which slavery teaches, there is none learned earlier, nor more thoroughly, than the art of reading the faces of those who hold power over us. The slave-child has no books; but he is not, on that account, without a study. His primer is the countenance of his master, and he cons it with a diligence which would do credit to the most ambitious scholar in any academy of the north. I had not been many weeks at old master's before I could tell, from the manner in which he came down the steps of a morning, whether the day was to be fair or foul for all of us. If he walked briskly, and looked about him, and spoke a word to the dogs, there was sunshine; but if he came slowly, with his eyes upon the ground, and his lips moving as though he were in earnest dispute with some one not present, the whole yard grew still, and the children crept away behind the kitchen, as young birds hide in the hedge when a hawk is seen wheeling in the sky. No one told me to do this. I did it because I saw others do it, and as the young of every creature learn, by example and by instinct, to fly from danger.

There was, about the place, an old man whom we children called Uncle Jacob, and who was held to be the wisest head on the plantation in such matters. He had been a field hand in his prime, but was now, by reason of a lame foot, kept about the yard, to mend harness, split kindling, and do such odd jobs as an old man may do. Uncle Jacob had a saying, which I have since found to contain more sound philosophy than many a learned volume—"Watch the hands, child; the mouth may lie, but the hands tell true." I did not then understand him; I have lived to understand him well. The master who smiles upon you while his fingers are fretting the whip-handle, is not a man whose smile is to be trusted.

It will be said, perhaps, that this is a small matter, and hardly worth the reader's notice. I think otherwise. It is a fact of some significance, that the first thing a slave learns is not to work, nor to pray, nor to love, but _to watch_. Slavery makes every slave a spy upon his master, as surely as it makes every master a tyrant over his slave. I have heard it gravely asserted, by men of high standing here in the north, that the negro is by nature artful, cunning, and given to dissembling. Let these gentlemen be placed where a frown may cost them a flogging, and a careless word may send them to the far south, and I venture to say they will soon acquire the same accomplishment, and practice it with no less skill. Deception is not the vice of the slave; it is the shield of the slave—the only one his master has left him. Take away the lash, and you will take away the necessity of the lie.

But I was yet a child, and knew nothing of these reflections. I only knew that there was a face up at the great house which I must study as I studied the sky before a storm, and that upon its changes hung, in some mysterious way, the peace of every cabin in the quarter. It was a hard lesson, and an early one; and I do not remember a time, from that day to the day of my escape, when I was wholly free from it.
