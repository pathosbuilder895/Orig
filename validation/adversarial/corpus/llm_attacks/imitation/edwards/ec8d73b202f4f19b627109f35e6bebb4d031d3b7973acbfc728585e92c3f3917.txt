In the 55th chapter of Isaiah, God by the prophet expostulates with a people that were diligent and laborious, but laborious to no purpose: verse 2, "Wherefore do ye spend money for that which is not bread? and your labor for that which satisfieth not?" They were not idle; they spent money, and they spent labor; but that which they laid out for was not bread, it could not nourish them; so that after all their pains they remained hungry and empty. In the foregoing verse God had been calling them to the waters, to buy wine and milk without money and without price; and here he reasons with them upon the folly of seeking their food elsewhere.

From hence I would observe this doctrine, viz., _That men, in seeking their happiness in any thing else besides God, do spend their labor for that which cannot satisfy them._

1. The soul of man is of such a nature that it stands in need of some good that is answerable to its capacity. God has made the soul capacious: he has given it an understanding that can look far, and desires that reach a great way, even to eternity. And as the soul is capacious, so it is restless till it be filled. 'Tis as natural to it to crave happiness as 'tis to the body to crave food; and the craving is no more satisfied with that which is only in show, than hunger is satisfied with the picture of bread.

2. Earthly enjoyments are not answerable to this capacity. They are things of a vastly inferior nature to the soul itself. The soul is a spiritual and immortal being; but these things are corporeal, and fading, and perishing. How should that which is less than the soul fill the soul? When a man has gotten one thing, he finds a void still, and reaches after another; and when he has gotten that, the void remains as before. "He that loveth silver shall not be satisfied with silver," Eccl. v. 10. The more men have of these things, the more do they commonly desire; so that their thirst is rather inflamed than allayed, as it is with one in a dropsy, who the more he drinks, the more he craves.

3. These things are not only insufficient in their measure, but uncertain in their continuance. If they could satisfy for a while, yet they can't satisfy for ever; and the soul looks forward, and can't be at rest in that which it knows it must shortly leave. 'Tis the foresight of losing that embitters the possession. Riches make themselves wings and fly away; health decays; friends are taken from us; and the man himself must quickly go out of the world, and leave all behind him, and carry nothing away.

4. But God is a good every way answerable to the soul's capacity. He is an infinite good, and therefore can fill a finite capacity, and more than fill it. He is a spiritual good, and so is suited to a spiritual nature. He is an eternal good, and so the soul that has him for its portion need never fear the losing of it. In him the understanding finds an object that it can never see to the bottom of; and the will finds that which it may rest in and delight in, and never be weary of. All the good that is in the creature is but a stream, and a small one; but God is the fountain and the ocean. "Whom have I in heaven but thee? and there is none upon earth that I desire besides thee," Psal. lxxiii. 25.

Let this put us upon considering how unreasonably we have laid out our labor. How many years have some of you been seeking rest, and what have you found? Have your pleasures, or your gains, or the esteem of your neighbors, made you happy? Have they not rather left you as empty as they found you, and more weary? Why will you still spend money for that which is not bread? Turn now to him who is the fountain of all good, and who freely offers himself to you: "Hearken diligently unto me, and eat ye that which is good, and let your soul delight itself in fatness."
