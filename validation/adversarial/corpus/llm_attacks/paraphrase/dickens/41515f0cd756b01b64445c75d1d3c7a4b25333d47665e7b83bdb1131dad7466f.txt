CHAPTER X

The Law-Writer

Along the eastern edge of Chancery Lane, or to be more exact, in Cook's Court, Cursitor Street, Mr. Snagsby the law-stationer carries on his honest trade. In the gloom of Cook's Court, which is a gloomy spot at most hours, Mr. Snagsby has sold every kind of blank legal form; parchment in skins and in rolls; paper of every sort, whether foolscap, brief, draft, brown, white, whitey-brown, or blotting; stamps; office quills, pens, ink, India-rubber, pounce, pins, pencils, sealing-wax, and wafers; red tape and green ferret; pocket-books, almanacs, diaries, and law lists; string boxes, rulers, inkstands of glass and of lead, pen-knives, scissors, bodkins, and all the other little cutlery of an office; in a word, more goods than could ever be listed. He has done so ever since he finished his apprenticeship and became Peffer's partner. That event brought something like a revolution to Cook's Court, when the freshly painted words PEFFER AND SNAGSBY replaced the old and venerable sign, by then very hard to make out, that read only PEFFER. For the smoke of London, which is the city's ivy, had twined itself so thickly about Peffer's name and clung so closely to his house that the fond parasite had wholly smothered the tree that bore it.

Peffer is no longer to be seen in Cook's Court. Nobody looks for him there, since for twenty-five years he has been lying in the churchyard of St. Andrew's, Holborn, while wagons and hackney-coaches thunder past him all day and through half the night like a single enormous dragon. Should he ever creep out while the dragon sleeps, to take the air once more in Cook's Court until the crowing of the hopeful cock in the cellar of the little dairy in Cursitor Street warns him back (and what that cock imagines daylight to be would be worth finding out, for he has seen next to none of it himself)—should Peffer ever return to haunt the dim glimmerings of Cook's Court, a thing no law-stationer in the trade can flatly rule out, he comes unseen, and nobody is harmed or enlightened by it.

While he lived, and also through the seven long years of Snagsby's apprenticeship, a niece of Peffer's lived with him on the same stationery premises: a small, sharp niece, rather too fiercely squeezed in at the waist, with a pointed nose like a crisp autumn evening that turns frosty as it closes. A rumour went the rounds among the people of Cook's Court that this niece's mother, when the girl was small, had been so anxious that her daughter's figure should come as near perfection as possible that she laced her up every morning with one foot braced against the bed-post to get a better grip and leverage; and moreover that she dosed her with pints of vinegar and lemon-juice, acids which, in the Court's opinion, had risen into the patient's nose and temper. From whichever of Rumour's many tongues this light gossip first came, it either never reached young Snagsby's ears or made no difference to him, for when he came of age he courted and won the lady it concerned and so entered into two partnerships at the same time.

And so, in Cook's Court, Cursitor Street, Mr. Snagsby and the niece are now one; and the niece still prizes her figure, which, whatever anyone's taste, is certainly valuable to the extent that there is remarkably little of it. Mr. and Mrs. Snagsby are not merely one bone and one flesh but, as the neighbours see it, one voice as well. That voice, which seems to come from Mrs. Snagsby only, is heard very often in Cook's Court. Mr. Snagsby himself, except insofar as he finds expression through those sweet tones, is seldom heard at all. He is a gentle, bald, nervous man with a gleaming head and a scrubby tuft of black hair poking out behind. He leans toward meekness and toward stoutness. When he stands in his doorway in Cook's Court in his grey shop-coat and black calico sleeves, gazing up at the clouds, or stands at a desk in his dim shop with a heavy flat ruler, cutting and trimming sheepskin alongside his two apprentices, he is very decidedly a modest and self-effacing man. At such moments there often rise from beneath his feet, as if from a shrill ghost that cannot rest in its grave, complaints and wailings in the voice already described; and now and then, when these climb to a sharper pitch than usual, Mr. Snagsby observes to the apprentices, "I think my little woman is a-giving it to Guster!"

This name, as Mr. Snagsby uses it, has in the past prompted the wits of Cook's Court to say that it ought really to belong to Mrs. Snagsby, since she might very aptly and forcibly be called a Guster, as a tribute to her tempestuous nature. It belongs, however, to a thin young woman from a workhouse (thought by some to have been baptised Augusta), and it is the only thing she owns apart from fifty shillings a year and a very small box poorly stocked with clothes. Although she was farmed out, or contracted for, during her growing years by a kindly benefactor of mankind living at Tooting, and must therefore have been raised under the best possible conditions, she "has fits," which the parish cannot explain. Guster, who is in fact twenty-three or twenty-four but looks a good ten years older, comes cheap on account of this mysterious drawback, and she is so frightened of being sent back into the care of her patron saint that she is always working, except when she is discovered with her head in the pail, or the sink, or the copper, or the dinner, or whatever else happens to be close by when the fit takes her. She satisfies the parents and guardians of the apprentices, who are confident she is unlikely to stir tender feelings in a young man's heart; she satisfies Mrs. Snagsby, who can always find something wrong with her; she satisfies Mr. Snagsby, who considers it an act of charity to keep her on.

To Guster the law-stationer's house is a temple of abundance and magnificence. She thinks the little drawing-room upstairs, which is always kept, so to speak, with its hair in curl-papers and its pinafore on, the most elegant room in Christendom. She considers the view it gives of Cook's Court at one end (to say nothing of a sidelong peep into Cursitor Street) and of the backyard of Coavinses', the sheriff's officer, at the other to be a scene of matchless beauty. The oil portraits that hang there—and the oil is laid on generously—showing Mr. Snagsby gazing at Mrs. Snagsby and Mrs. Snagsby gazing at Mr. Snagsby, are to her as fine as anything by Raphael or Titian. So Guster has some compensations for her many hardships.

Mr. Snagsby leaves everything outside the practical mysteries of the trade to Mrs. Snagsby. She handles the money, scolds the tax-collectors, fixes the hours and places of Sunday worship, gives permission for Mr. Snagsby's amusements, and admits no accountability for whatever she chooses to put on the table at dinner, so much so that she is held up as the model among the neighbouring wives for a long way down Chancery Lane on either side, and even out as far as Holborn; and these wives, in any household skirmish, are in the habit of calling on their husbands to observe the contrast between their own position and Mrs. Snagsby's, and between their husbands' conduct and Mr. Snagsby's. Rumour, which is forever flitting like a bat around Cook's Court and darting in and out of everyone's windows, does whisper that Mrs. Snagsby is jealous and nosy, that Mr. Snagsby is now and then harried out of house and home, and that if he had as much spirit as a mouse he would not put up with it. It is even remarked that the wives who hold him up to their headstrong husbands as a shining model actually look down on him, and that none does so more scornfully than one particular lady whose husband is strongly suspected of using his umbrella on her by way of correction.

These vague murmurs, though, may spring from the fact that Mr. Snagsby is, after his fashion, a thoughtful and rather poetic man, who likes to stroll in Staple Inn in summer and notice how countrified the sparrows and the leaves look there, and to idle about the Rolls Yard on a Sunday afternoon and remark (when he is in good spirits) that there were old times once, and that you'd find a stone coffin or two under that chapel now, he'll be bound, if you was to dig for it. He feeds his fancy, too, by thinking of all the Chancellors and Vice-Chancellors and Masters of the Rolls who have died; and he draws such a taste of the countryside from telling the two apprentices how he HAS heard it said that a stream "as clear as crystal" once flowed straight down the middle of Holborn, back when Turnstile really was a turnstile, opening right out into the meadows—draws such a taste of the country from this that he never feels any need to go there.

Evening is coming on and the gas has been lit, though it does not yet do much, since it is not fully dark. Mr. Snagsby, standing in his shop-doorway and looking up at the clouds, sees a crow that is out late glide westward across the strip of sky that belongs to Cook's Court. The crow flies straight over Chancery Lane and Lincoln's Inn Garden and into Lincoln's Inn Fields.

Here, in a large house that was once a stately mansion, lives Mr. Tulkinghorn. It is now let out in sets of chambers, and in those shrunken pieces of its former grandeur lawyers lodge like maggots in nuts. Yet its spacious staircases, corridors, and anterooms are still there; so are its painted ceilings, where Allegory, in a Roman helmet and heavenly linen, lolls among balustrades and columns, flowers, clouds, and stout-legged boys, and gives the onlooker a headache, which seems, more or less, to be what Allegory is always after. Here, surrounded by his many boxes labelled with lofty names, Mr. Tulkinghorn lives when he is not staying, wordlessly, in country-houses where the great of the earth are bored to death. Here he sits today, quietly at his table. An oyster of the old school, whom no one can open.

His room, in the dusk of this afternoon, looks just as he does. Rusty, behind the times, shrinking from notice, and able to afford to. Around him are heavy, broad-backed, old-fashioned chairs of mahogany and horsehair, not easy to lift; outmoded spindle-legged tables with dusty baize covers; and presentation engravings of the holders of great titles from the last generation or the one before. A thick, dingy Turkey carpet deadens the floor where he sits, lit by two candles in old-fashioned silver candlesticks that throw far too little light across his big room. The titles on the spines of his books have sunk back into the bindings; everything that can be locked is locked; no key is anywhere in sight. Hardly any loose papers lie about. He has a manuscript close at hand but is not consulting it. With the round lid of an inkstand and two broken pieces of sealing-wax, he is slowly and silently working through whatever line of indecision occupies his mind. Now the inkstand lid is in the centre, now the red piece of sealing-wax, now the black piece. That won't do. Mr. Tulkinghorn has to gather them all up and start over.

Here, under the painted ceiling, with foreshortened Allegory glaring down at him as an intruder as though it meant to pounce, and he ignoring it entirely, Mr. Tulkinghorn keeps both his home and his office. He employs no staff beyond a single middle-aged man, generally a bit shabby at the elbows, who sits in a high pew in the hall and is seldom weighed down with work. Mr. Tulkinghorn is not an ordinary practitioner. He has no need of clerks. He is a vast reservoir of secrets, not one to be drawn off in that way. His clients want HIM; he is everything to them. Any drafts he needs are drawn up by special pleaders in the Temple on cryptic instructions; any fair copies he needs are made at the stationers', with no regard for cost. The middle-aged man in the pew knows hardly more about the affairs of the peerage than any crossing-sweeper in Holborn.

The red piece, the black piece, the inkstand lid, the other inkstand lid, the little sand-box. There! You to the centre, you to the right, you to the left. This line of indecision must surely be settled now or never. Now! Mr. Tulkinghorn rises, settles his spectacles, puts on his hat, slips the manuscript into his pocket, goes out, and tells the middle-aged man with the worn elbows, "I shall be back presently." He very seldom tells him anything more precise.

Mr. Tulkinghorn makes his way, as the crow had come—not quite so directly, but nearly—to Cook's Court, Cursitor Street. To Snagsby's, Law-Stationer's, Deeds engrossed and copied, Law-Writing executed in all its branches, &c., &c., &c.

It is around five or six in the afternoon, and a gentle smell of warm tea hangs over Cook's Court. It hangs about Snagsby's door. They keep early hours there: dinner at half-past one and supper at half-past nine. Mr. Snagsby had been on the point of going down into the underground regions for his tea when he glanced out of his door a moment ago and saw the late crow.

"Master at home?"

Guster is minding the shop, because the apprentices take their tea in the kitchen with Mr. and Mrs. Snagsby; and so the robe-maker's two daughters, combing their curls before the two mirrors in the two second-floor windows of the house across the way, are not driving the two apprentices wild, as they like to imagine, but are only arousing the fruitless admiration of Guster, whose hair will not grow, never would, and, it is firmly believed, never will.

"Master at home?" asks Mr. Tulkinghorn.

The master is at home, and Guster will go and get him. Guster vanishes, glad to leave the shop, which she looks on with a mixture of fear and reverence as a storeroom of the dreadful instruments of the law's great torture—a place not to be entered once the gas has been turned off.

Mr. Snagsby appears, greasy, warm, smelling of herbs, and chewing. He gulps down a bit of bread and butter. He says, "Bless my soul, sir! Mr. Tulkinghorn!"

"I want half a word with you, Snagsby."

"Certainly, sir! Dear me, sir, why didn't you send your young man round for me? Pray step into the back shop, sir."

Snagsby has lit up in an instant. The cramped room, reeking of parchment-grease, serves as warehouse, counting-house, and copying-office all at once. Mr. Tulkinghorn sits on a stool at the desk, turned round to face him.

"Jarndyce and Jarndyce, Snagsby."

"Yes, sir." Mr. Snagsby turns up the gas and coughs behind his hand, modestly looking forward to a profit. Being a timid man, Mr. Snagsby is in the habit of coughing with a range of different meanings, and so sparing himself words.

"You copied some affidavits in that case for me recently."

"Yes, sir, we did."
