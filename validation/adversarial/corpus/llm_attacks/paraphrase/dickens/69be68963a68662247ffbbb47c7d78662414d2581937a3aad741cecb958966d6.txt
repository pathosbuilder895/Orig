...how or why; entire families have inherited fabled hatreds along with the suit. The small plaintiff or defendant who was promised a new rocking-horse for the day Jarndyce and Jarndyce should be settled has grown up, come into possession of a real horse, and ridden off into the next world. Lovely wards of court have faded into mothers and then grandmothers; a long line of Chancellors has arrived and departed; the host of bills in the suit has turned into nothing more than bills of mortality; perhaps not three Jarndyces remain alive on earth since old Tom Jarndyce, in despair, blew his brains out in a coffee-house in Chancery Lane; and yet Jarndyce and Jarndyce still drags its weary length before the court, forever without hope.

Jarndyce and Jarndyce has become a joke. That is the one good thing ever to come of it. It has been the death of many, but in the profession it is a joke. Every master in Chancery has had a reference out of it. Every Chancellor was "in it," on behalf of someone or other, back when he was counsel at the bar. Clever things have been said about it by blue-nosed old benchers in bulbous shoes, sitting in select port-wine committee after dinner in hall. Articled clerks have long been in the habit of trying out their first legal wit on it. The last Lord Chancellor handled it deftly when, correcting the eminent silk gown Mr. Blowers, who had said that such a thing might happen when the sky rained potatoes, he remarked, "or when we get through Jarndyce and Jarndyce, Mr. Blowers"—a joke that especially delighted the maces, bags, and purses.

How many people outside the suit Jarndyce and Jarndyce has reached out its sickly hand to spoil and corrupt would be a very broad question. From the master on whose impaling files reams of dusty warrants in Jarndyce and Jarndyce have grimly twisted into every shape, down to the copying-clerk in the Six Clerks' Office who has copied his tens of thousands of Chancery folio-pages under that unending heading, no man's character has been bettered by it. In trickery, evasion, delay, plunder, botheration, and false pretences of every kind, there are influences that can never lead to good. The very solicitors' boys who have held the miserable suitors at bay, insisting since time out of mind that Mr. Chizzle, Mizzle, or whoever it might be was particularly engaged and had appointments until dinner, may have picked up an extra moral twist and shuffle from Jarndyce and Jarndyce. The receiver in the cause has gained a handsome sum of money by it, but he has gained as well a distrust of his own mother and a scorn for his own kind. Chizzle, Mizzle, and the rest have slipped into a habit of vaguely promising themselves that they will look into that small outstanding matter and see what can be done for Drizzle—who was not fairly treated—once Jarndyce and Jarndyce is out of the office. Shirking and sharking of every variety have been scattered far and wide by the unlucky cause; and even those who have watched its history from the farthest edge of such evil have been drawn, without noticing it, into a careless habit of leaving bad things alone to run their own bad course, and a careless belief that if the world goes wrong, it was, in some offhand fashion, never meant to go right.

So, in the middle of the mud and at the heart of the fog, sits the Lord High Chancellor in his High Court of Chancery.

"Mr. Tangle," says the Lord High Chancellor, who has lately grown somewhat restless under that learned gentleman's eloquence.

"Mlud," says Mr. Tangle. Mr. Tangle knows more about Jarndyce and Jarndyce than anyone. He is famous for it, and is supposed never to have read anything else since leaving school.

"Have you nearly finished your argument?"

"Mlud, no—variety of points—feel it my duty tsubmit—ludship," is the answer that slips out of Mr. Tangle.

"Several members of the bar are still to be heard, I believe?" says the Chancellor, with a faint smile.

Eighteen of Mr. Tangle's learned friends, each equipped with a little summary of eighteen hundred sheets, pop up like eighteen hammers in a pianoforte, make eighteen bows, and sink back into their eighteen places of obscurity.

"We will continue the hearing on Wednesday fortnight," says the Chancellor. For the point in dispute is merely a question of costs, a mere bud on the great forest tree of the parent suit, and really will be settled one of these days.

The Chancellor stands; the bar stands; the prisoner is hurried forward; the man from Shropshire cries out, "My lord!" Maces, bags, and purses indignantly call for silence and scowl at the man from Shropshire.

"With regard," the Chancellor goes on, still on Jarndyce and Jarndyce, "to the young girl—"

"Begludship's pardon—boy," says Mr. Tangle, too soon.

"With regard," the Chancellor goes on, with extra distinctness, "to the young girl and boy, the two young people"—Mr. Tangle crushed—"whom I directed to attend today and who are now in my private room, I will see them and satisfy myself as to whether it is expedient to make the order for their living with their uncle."

Mr. Tangle on his feet again. "Begludship's pardon—dead."

"With their"—the Chancellor peering through his double eye-glass at the papers on his desk—"grandfather."

"Begludship's pardon—victim of rash action—brains."

Suddenly a very small counsel with a tremendous bass voice rises, fully puffed out, in the remote back settlements of the fog, and says, "Will your lordship permit me? I appear for him. He is a cousin, several times removed. I am not at this moment prepared to tell the court at exactly what remove he is a cousin, but he IS a cousin."

Leaving this announcement (delivered like a message from the tomb) ringing among the rafters of the roof, the very small counsel sinks down, and the fog knows him no more. Everyone looks for him. No one can see him.

"I will speak with both the young people," the Chancellor says again, "and satisfy myself on the question of their living with their cousin. I will mention the matter tomorrow morning when I take my seat."

The Chancellor is about to bow to the bar when the prisoner is brought before him. Nothing can possibly come of the prisoner's muddle of affairs except his being sent back to prison, which is quickly done. The man from Shropshire risks one more protesting "My lord!" but the Chancellor, aware of him, has nimbly disappeared. Everyone else promptly disappears as well. A battery of blue bags is loaded with heavy charges of papers and carried away by clerks; the little mad old woman marches off with her documents; the empty court is locked up. If only all the injustice it has done and all the misery it has caused could be locked up with it, and the whole lot burnt away on one great funeral pyre—why, so much the better for other parties than the parties in Jarndyce and Jarndyce!

CHAPTER II

In Fashion

It is only a glimpse of the world of fashion that we need on this same muddy afternoon. It is not so different from the Court of Chancery that we cannot pass from one scene to the other as the crow flies. Both the world of fashion and the Court of Chancery are creatures of precedent and custom: oversleeping Rip Van Winkles who have played at odd games through a great deal of stormy weather; sleeping beauties whom the knight will wake someday, when all the halted spits in the kitchen will start turning wonderfully!

It is not a large world. Even compared with this world of ours, which has its limits too (as your Highness will discover once you have toured it and arrived at the edge of the void beyond), it is a very small speck. There is a great deal of good in it; there are many good and honest people in it; it has its allotted place. But its fault is that it is a world wrapped in too much jeweller's cotton and fine wool, so that it cannot hear the rush of the larger worlds, and cannot see them as they wheel around the sun. It is a muffled world, and its growth is sometimes unhealthy for lack of air.

My Lady Dedlock has come back to her town house for a few days before she leaves for Paris, where her ladyship means to spend some weeks, after which her plans are uncertain. The fashionable intelligence says so, for the comfort of the Parisians, and it knows everything fashionable. To know things any other way would be unfashionable. My Lady Dedlock has been down at what she calls, in casual conversation, her "place" in Lincolnshire. The waters are up in Lincolnshire. One arch of the bridge in the park has been undermined and soaked away. The low ground nearby, for half a mile across, is a stagnant river with gloomy trees standing as islands in it and a surface pitted all over, all day long, by falling rain. My Lady Dedlock's place has been extremely dismal. The weather has been so wet for so many days and nights that the trees seem soaked right through, and the soft cuttings and loppings of the woodman's axe make no crash or crackle when they fall. The deer, looking drenched, leave bogs wherever they go. The crack of a rifle loses its sharpness in the damp air, and its smoke drifts in a slow little cloud toward the green rise, crowned with a coppice, that forms a backdrop to the falling rain. From my Lady Dedlock's own windows the view is by turns the colour of lead and the colour of Indian ink. The vases on the stone terrace in front catch the rain all day; and all night the heavy drops fall—drip, drip, drip—on the broad flagged pavement that has long been called the Ghost's Walk. On Sundays the little church in the park smells of mould; the oak pulpit breaks into a cold sweat; and there is a general smell and taste as of the ancient Dedlocks in their graves. My Lady Dedlock (who has no children), looking out in the early twilight from her boudoir toward a keeper's lodge, and seeing firelight on the latticed panes, smoke rising from the chimney, and a child, chased by a woman, running out into the rain to meet the gleaming figure of a muffled-up man coming in at the gate, has been thoroughly put out of temper. My Lady Dedlock says she has been "bored to death."

So my Lady Dedlock has left the place in Lincolnshire and abandoned it to the rain, and the crows, and the rabbits, and the deer, and the partridges and pheasants. The portraits of the departed Dedlocks have seemed to sink into the damp walls out of sheer low spirits as the housekeeper has gone through the old rooms closing the shutters. And when they will come out again, the fashionable intelligence—which, like the devil, knows all of the past and the present but nothing of the future—cannot yet undertake to say.

Sir Leicester Dedlock is merely a baronet, but no baronet is mightier than he. His family is as old as the hills and far more respectable. He holds the general view that the world could manage without hills but would be finished without Dedlocks. He would, on the whole, grant that nature is a good idea (a bit low, perhaps, when not shut in by a park fence), but an idea that depends for its execution on your great county families. He is a gentleman of strict conscience, contemptuous of all pettiness and meanness, and ready at a moment's notice to die any death you care to name rather than allow the faintest slur on his integrity. He is an honourable, stubborn, truthful, high-spirited, deeply prejudiced, perfectly unreasonable man.

Sir Leicester is a full twenty years older than my Lady. He will never see sixty-five again, nor perhaps sixty-six, nor even sixty-seven. He has a touch of gout now and then and walks somewhat stiffly. He has a dignified bearing, with his light-grey hair and whiskers, his fine shirt-frill, his spotless white waistcoat, and his blue coat with bright buttons, always buttoned. He is formal, stately, unfailingly courteous to my Lady, and values her personal charms very highly. His gallantry toward my Lady, unchanged since he courted her, is the one small trace of romantic fancy in him. In fact, he married her for love. There is still a whisper that she had no family at all; however that may be, Sir Leicester had so much family that perhaps he had enough of it and could do without more. But she had beauty, pride, ambition, insolent determination, and enough good sense to share out among a whole legion of fine ladies. With wealth and rank added to these, she soon rose to the surface, and for years now my Lady Dedlock has stood at the centre of the fashionable intelligence and at the top of the fashionable tree.

Everyone knows how Alexander wept when there were no more worlds left for him to conquer—or has some reason to know it by now, the matter having been brought up rather often. My Lady Dedlock, having conquered HER world, sank not into the melting mood but rather into the freezing one. A weary composure, a worn-out calm, an equanimity of fatigue that neither interest nor satisfaction can disturb: these are the trophies of her victory. She is perfectly well-bred. If she were carried up to heaven tomorrow, she might be expected to rise without the least rapture. She still has beauty, and if it is past its prime, it is not yet in its autumn. She has a fine face, originally of the kind one would call very pretty rather than handsome, but refined into something classical by the expression her fashionable position has taught it. Her figure is elegant and gives the impression of height. Not that she is tall, but that "the most is made," as the Honourable Bob Stables has often declared on oath, "of all her points." The same authority notes that she is perfectly turned out, and remarks in praise of her hair in particular that she is the best-groomed woman in the whole stud.
