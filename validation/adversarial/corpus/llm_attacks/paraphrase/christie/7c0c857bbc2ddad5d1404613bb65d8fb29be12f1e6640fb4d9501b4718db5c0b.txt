The one costume in the barber’s emporium that would fit me was a Teddy Bear. I have no objection to playing at bears with a few nice young girls on a winter evening in England—but it is hardly the ideal garment for the equator. Still, I caused a great deal of amusement, and carried off first prize in the “brought on board” class—a ridiculous name for a costume hired for the night. However, since nobody appeared to have the faintest notion which costumes had been made and which brought, it made no difference. Mrs. Blair declined to dress up. It seems she shares Pagett’s view on the subject. Colonel Race followed her lead. Anne Beddingfeld had put together a gipsy costume for herself and looked remarkably well in it. Pagett pleaded a headache and did not turn up. In his place I invited an odd little man named Reeves. He is a leading figure in the South African Labour party. A dreadful little fellow, but I wish to stay on good terms with him, since he supplies me with information I require. I want to grasp this Rand business from both points of view. The dancing was hot work. I danced twice with Anne Beddingfeld, and she was obliged to pretend she enjoyed it. I danced once with Mrs. Blair, who did not bother to pretend, and I inflicted myself on sundry other maidens whose looks appealed to me. Then we went down to supper. I had ordered champagne; the steward recommended Clicquot 1911 as the best on the boat, and I took his advice. I appeared to have stumbled on the one thing capable of loosening Colonel Race’s tongue. So far from being taciturn, the man grew positively chatty. For a time this entertained me; then it struck me that Colonel Race, rather than I, was turning into the life and soul of the party. He ragged me at some length about my keeping a diary. “One of these days it will expose all your indiscretions, Pedler.” “My dear Race,” I said, “allow me to suggest that I am not quite such a fool as you take me for. I may commit indiscretions, but I don’t set them down in black and white. When I am dead my executors will learn what I thought of a great many people, but I doubt whether they will find anything to raise or lower their opinion of _me._ A diary serves to record the peculiarities of others—not one’s own.” “There is such a thing as unconscious self-betrayal, all the same.” “To the psycho-analyst, all things are vile,” I answered sententiously. “You must have led a fascinating life, Colonel Race?” said Miss Beddingfeld, looking at him with wide, shining eyes. That is the way they manage it, these girls! Othello won Desdemona by telling her tales, but, oh, didn’t Desdemona win Othello by the way she listened? At any rate, the girl got Race going. He started on lion stories. A man who has shot lions in great numbers has an unfair edge over other men. It seemed to me high time that I told a lion story as well—one of a livelier sort. “Talking of that,” I observed, “it puts me in mind of a rather thrilling tale I once heard. A friend of mine was away on a shooting expedition somewhere in East Africa. One night he stepped out of his tent for some purpose or other and was startled by a low growl. Turning sharply, he saw a lion crouched ready to spring. He had left his rifle inside the tent. Quick as a flash, he ducked, and the lion leapt clean over his head. Vexed at having missed, the beast growled and made ready to spring once more. Again he ducked, and again the lion sailed right over him. The same thing happened a third time, but by now he had worked his way close to the mouth of the tent, so he dashed inside and grabbed his rifle. When he came out again, gun in hand, the lion had vanished. This puzzled him a good deal. He crept round to the back of the tent, where there was a small clearing. And there, sure enough, was the lion, hard at work practising low jumps.” This was greeted with a roar of applause. I drank some champagne. “Another time,” I went on, “this same friend had a second odd experience. He was trekking across country, and since he was anxious to reach his destination before the heat of the day, he told his boys to inspan while it was still dark. They had some difficulty about it, for the mules were very restless, but in the end they managed it and set off. The mules tore along like the wind, and when day broke they discovered why. In the dark the boys had inspanned a lion as the near wheeler.” This too went down well, a ripple of laughter running round the table, but I am not sure the highest compliment of all did not come from my friend the Labour Member, who stayed pale and solemn. “Good God!” he said anxiously. “Who un’arnessed them, then?” “I must go to Rhodesia,” said Mrs. Blair. “After everything you’ve told us, Colonel Race, I positively must.
