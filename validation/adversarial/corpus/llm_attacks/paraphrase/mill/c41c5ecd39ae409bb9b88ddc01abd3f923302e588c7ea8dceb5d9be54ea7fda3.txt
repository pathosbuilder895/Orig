Their power was the chief grievance of the nation. Voting as an employer or a landlord directed had become so deeply ingrained a habit that almost nothing could shake it except a powerful surge of popular enthusiasm, which rarely arises except in a good cause. A vote cast against those influences was therefore, as a rule, an honest and public-spirited one; but in any event, whatever the motive behind it, it was nearly certain to be a good vote, since it was a vote against the great evil of the day, the overpowering influence of oligarchy. Had the voter of that period been able to use his privilege freely and without danger to himself, even if neither honestly nor intelligently, reform would have gained greatly, for the yoke of the power then ruling the country would have been broken — the power that had created and kept up everything bad in the institutions and the administration of the state — the power of landlords and boroughmongers. "The ballot was not adopted; but the course of events has done, and continues more and more to do, the ballot's work in this respect. Both the political and the social condition of the country, insofar as they bear on this question, have changed greatly and go on changing daily. The upper classes are no longer masters of the country. Only someone blind to every sign of the times could believe that the middle classes are as subservient to the upper, or the working classes as dependent on the upper and middle, as they were twenty-five years ago. The events of those twenty-five years have not only taught each class the measure of its own collective strength, but have enabled individuals of a lower class to stand up far more boldly to those of a higher. In most cases the elector's vote, whether it goes against or along with the wishes of his superiors, is now not the product of coercion, since the means of applying it are no longer what they were, but the expression of his own personal or political leanings. The very defects of the present electoral system prove as much. The increase in bribery that is so loudly lamented, and its spread to places once untouched by it, show that local influences no longer reign supreme, and that electors now vote to please themselves rather than others. No doubt a great deal of servile dependence still lingers in the counties and the smaller boroughs; but the spirit of the age is against it, and the pressure of events keeps wearing it down. A good tenant can now feel that he is worth as much to his landlord as his landlord is to him; a thriving tradesman can afford to feel independent of any single customer. With each election the votes belong more and more to the voters themselves. What now needs emancipating is their minds far more than their personal circumstances. They are no longer passive tools of other men's will — mere channels for placing power in the hands of a controlling oligarchy. The electors themselves are turning into the oligarchy. "Precisely to the degree that the elector's vote is decided by his own will, and not by the will of someone who is his master, his situation resembles that of a member of Parliament, and publicity becomes indispensable. As long as any part of the community remains unrepresented, the Chartists' argument against combining the ballot with a restricted suffrage cannot be answered. The present electors, and most of those whom any likely Reform Bill would add to their ranks, belong to the middle class, and have a class interest separate from that of the working classes quite as much as landlords or great manufacturers do. If the suffrage were extended to every skilled laborer, even they would, or might, still have a class interest separate from that of the unskilled. Suppose it extended to all men — suppose that what used to be called, by a misapplied name, universal suffrage, and is now called by the silly title of manhood suffrage, became law; the voters would still have a class interest as against women. Suppose a question came before the Legislature that specially concerned women — whether women should be permitted to take university degrees; whether the light penalties imposed on brutes who beat their wives nearly to death every day should be replaced with something more effective; or suppose someone proposed in the British Parliament what one American state after another is enacting, not by mere statute but by a clause in its revised Constitution, that married women should have a right to their own property — do not a man's wife and daughters have a right to know whether he votes for or against a candidate who will back these proposals? "It will be objected, of course, that these arguments draw all their force from assuming an unjust state of the suffrage: that if the opinion of non-electors is likely to make the elector vote more honestly or more usefully than he would on his own, then they are better fitted to be electors than he is and ought to have the franchise; that anyone fit to influence electors is fit to be one; that those to whom voters should be answerable ought themselves to be voters, and, as voters, ought to have the protection of the ballot to guard them against the undue influence of powerful individuals or classes to whom they owe no accountability. "This argument is plausible, and there was a time when I thought it decisive. It now seems to me fallacious. Not everyone fit to influence electors is, on that account, fit to be an elector. Being an elector is a far greater power than influencing one, and people may be ready for the lesser political function who could not yet safely be entrusted with the greater. The opinions and wishes of the poorest and roughest class of laborers may be very valuable as one influence among several on the minds of voters, and on those of the Legislature too, and yet it could be extremely harmful to hand them the dominant influence by admitting them, in their present moral and intellectual state, to the full exercise of the suffrage. It is exactly this indirect influence of the unenfranchised over the enfranchised which, as it steadily grows, eases the passage to each new extension of the franchise, and serves as the means by which, when the moment arrives, that extension is achieved peacefully. But there is a further and still deeper consideration, one that political speculation should never leave out of account. The very notion that publicity, and the feeling of being answerable to the public, are worthless unless the public is competent to judge soundly is itself groundless. It takes a very shallow view of the usefulness of public opinion to suppose that it does good only when it manages to enforce a servile conformity to itself. Being under the eyes of others — having to justify oneself to others — matters most of all to those who act against the opinion of others, because it compels them to have firm ground of their own to stand on. Nothing steadies a person like working against pressure. Except under the passing sway of passionate excitement, nobody will do what he expects to be heavily blamed for unless he is acting on a settled purpose of his own, formed beforehand, which is always a mark of a thoughtful and deliberate character and, outside of thoroughly bad men, generally springs from sincere and strong personal convictions. Even the plain fact of having to account for their conduct is a powerful incentive to keep to conduct for which at least some decent account can be given. Anyone who thinks that the mere duty of keeping up appearances is not a very considerable check on the abuse of power has never had occasion to notice how those behave who feel no need to observe that restraint. Publicity is of inestimable value even when it does no more than prevent what could not possibly be plausibly defended — than compel deliberation, and force everyone to decide, before acting, what he will say if called to answer for what he has done. "But if not now (it may be said), then at least in the future, when everyone is fit to vote and all men and women are admitted to the vote by virtue of that fitness, _then_ there can be no further danger of class legislation; then the electors, being the nation itself, can have no interest apart from the general interest: even if some individuals still vote from private or class motives, the majority will have no such motive; and since there will then be no non-electors to whom they ought to be answerable, the ballot, shutting out nothing but sinister influences, will do nothing but good. "Even here I disagree. I cannot believe that the ballot would be desirable even if the people were fit for universal suffrage and had won it. First, because in that situation it could not reasonably be thought necessary. Just picture the state of affairs the hypothesis assumes: a people educated throughout, and every adult human being in possession of a vote. If, even now, when only a small share of the population are electors and most of it is barely educated, public opinion is already, as everyone can see, the ruling power in the last resort, it is a fantasy to suppose that landlords and rich people could wield any power over a community in which everyone reads and everyone votes, against that community's own wishes, that it would find at all hard to throw off. But although the shelter of secrecy would then be unnecessary, the check of publicity would be as necessary as ever. The common observation of mankind has been badly mistaken if simply being a member of the community, and not standing in a position of marked conflict of interest with the public at large, is enough to secure the performance of a public duty without either the spur or the restraint that comes from the opinion of our fellow creatures. A man's own particular portion of the public interest, even when no private interest is pulling him the other way, is not generally found sufficient to make him do his duty to the public without some further external inducement. Nor can it be granted that, even if everyone had a vote, people would vote as honestly in secret as in public. "The claim that electors who make up the whole community cannot have an interest in voting against the community's interest will turn out, when examined, to have more sound than sense in it. Although the community as a whole can have (as the very words imply) no interest other than its collective one, any individual in it, or every individual, may. A man's interest is made up of whatever he takes an interest _in_. Everyone has as many different interests as he has feelings, likings or dislikings, whether selfish or of a better sort. None of these, taken alone, can be said to make up 'his interest': whether he is a good man or a bad one depends on which class of his interests he prefers. A man who plays the tyrant at home will tend to sympathize with tyranny (so long as it is not practised on himself); he will almost certainly not sympathize with resistance to it. An envious man will vote against Aristides because he is called the Just. A selfish man will prefer even a petty personal benefit to his portion of the advantage his country would gain from a good law, because the interests peculiar to himself are the ones his habits of mind both incline him to dwell on and best fit him to estimate. A large number of electors will have two sets of preferences, one resting on private grounds and the other on public ones. The latter are the only ones an elector would care to acknowledge. People are eager to display the best side of their character, even to those who are no better than they are. Out of greed, spite, pique, or personal rivalry, and even out of the interests or prejudices of class or sect, people will cast dishonest or mean votes more readily in secret than in public. And there are cases — they may become more common — in which almost the only thing restraining a majority of rogues is their involuntary respect for the opinion of an honest minority. In a case like that of the repudiating states of North America, is there not some check on the unprincipled voter in the shame of having to look an honest man in the face? Since the ballot would sacrifice all this good, even under the conditions most favorable to it, a far stronger case for its necessity than can now be made (and the case grows weaker all the time) would be needed to make its adoption desirable." The system of personal representation as Mr. Hare has organized it requires the use of voting papers. But it seems to me essential that the elector sign the paper at a public polling-place, or, where no such place is conveniently within reach, at some office open to everyone, and in the presence of a responsible public official. The suggestion that has been put forward, of letting voting papers be filled in at the voter's own home and sent by post, or collected by a public official, I would consider fatal.
