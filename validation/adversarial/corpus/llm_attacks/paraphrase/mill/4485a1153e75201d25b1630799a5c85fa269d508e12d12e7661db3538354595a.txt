The notion that life has, in their phrase, no aim higher than pleasure — nothing worthier or more elevated to desire and strive for — they denounce as wholly base and degraded, a creed fit only for swine. It was to swine, indeed, that the disciples of Epicurus were scornfully compared from very early on, and present-day adherents of the doctrine are now and then treated to comparisons of equal courtesy by its critics in Germany, France, and England. Under such attacks the Epicureans have always replied that the degrading picture of human nature is painted not by them but by those who accuse them, because the charge assumes that people can enjoy no pleasures beyond those available to swine. Were that assumption correct, the charge could not be answered, yet it would also cease to be a reproach: if humans and swine drew their pleasures from exactly the same sources, whatever rule of life suited the one would suit the other just as well. The reason that likening the Epicurean life to the life of beasts strikes us as degrading is precisely that a beast's pleasures fall short of what a human being understands by happiness. People possess faculties loftier than animal appetite, and once they become aware of those faculties, they will count nothing as happiness that leaves them ungratified. I do not, to be sure, regard the Epicureans as having been at all free of error in working out the consequences of the utilitarian principle; doing that adequately requires bringing in a good many Stoic and Christian elements as well. Still, no Epicurean theory of life that we know of fails to rank the pleasures of the intellect, of the feelings and imagination, and of the moral sentiments far above those of bare sensation, considered simply as pleasures. It has to be granted, though, that utilitarian writers have generally rested the superiority of mental over bodily pleasures mainly on the former's greater durability, security, cheapness, and the like, on their incidental advantages, that is, rather than on anything in their intrinsic nature. On every one of these points the utilitarians have made their case completely; yet they could have occupied the other ground as well, the one that might be called higher, without any inconsistency at all. Nothing in the principle of utility prevents us from acknowledging that some _kinds_ of pleasure are more desirable and more valuable than others. It would be absurd if, when we assess everything else, we took quality into account along with quantity, but supposed that pleasures alone were to be judged by quantity only. Should someone ask me what I mean by a difference of quality among pleasures, or what makes one pleasure worth more than another purely as a pleasure, apart from its being larger in amount, only one answer is possible. Take two pleasures: if all or nearly all of those who have experienced both show a decided preference for one of them, independently of any sense of moral obligation to prefer it, then that one is the more desirable pleasure. If people who are competently familiar with both rank one of them so far above the other that they choose it even knowing it brings a greater measure of discontent, and would not give it up for any amount of the other pleasure their nature could hold, we are entitled to credit the preferred enjoyment with a superiority of quality that so far outweighs quantity as to make quantity, by comparison, of little importance. Now it is beyond question that those who are equally familiar with both, and equally able to appreciate and enjoy both, show a very marked preference for the way of life that calls their higher faculties into play. Hardly any human being would agree to be turned into one of the lower animals in exchange for the promise of a beast's pleasures in full measure; no intelligent person would agree to become a fool, no educated person an ignoramus, no one with feeling and conscience a selfish and base character, even if convinced that the fool, the dunce, or the scoundrel is more content with his lot than they are with their own. They would not surrender what they have beyond him for the fullest gratification of all the desires they share with him. If they ever imagine they would, it is only in situations of such extreme unhappiness that, to get away from it, they would trade their lot for nearly any other, however undesirable it might look to them. A being with higher faculties needs more to make him happy, is probably capable of keener suffering, and is certainly open to it at more points than a being of a lower type; yet despite these liabilities he can never truly wish to descend into what he feels to be an inferior grade of existence. We may explain this reluctance however we like. We may put it down to pride, a name applied without distinction to some of the most and some of the least admirable feelings human beings are capable of; we may trace it to the love of liberty and personal independence, an appeal to which was for the Stoics among the most effective ways of instilling it; or to the love of power, or to the love of excitement, both of which really do enter into it and contribute to it. But the most fitting name for it is a sense of dignity, which every human being has in some form, and in some proportion, though by no means an exact one, to their higher faculties, and which forms so essential a part of the happiness of those in whom it is strong that nothing at odds with it could be more than momentarily an object of their desire. Anyone who supposes that this preference involves a sacrifice of happiness — that the superior being, in anything like equal circumstances, is no happier than the inferior — confuses two very different ideas, happiness and contentment. It cannot be disputed that a being whose capacities for enjoyment are low has the best chance of having them completely satisfied, while a richly endowed being will always feel that whatever happiness he can hope for, the world being what it is, is imperfect. But he can learn to endure its imperfections, if they can be endured at all; and they will not make him envy the being who is, indeed, unaware of those imperfections, but only because he has no feeling whatever for the good that those imperfections qualify. It is better to be a dissatisfied human being than a satisfied pig; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. And if the fool or the pig thinks otherwise, that is because each of them knows only his own side of the question. The other party to the comparison knows both. It may be objected that many people capable of the higher pleasures now and then, under the pressure of temptation, put them behind the lower. But this is entirely consistent with a full appreciation of the intrinsic superiority of the higher. Through weakness of character, people often choose the nearer good while knowing it to be the less valuable one, and they do this just as readily when the choice lies between two bodily pleasures as when it lies between a bodily and a mental one. They chase sensual indulgence at the expense of their health, though perfectly aware that health is the greater good. A further objection may be raised: that many who start out with a youthful enthusiasm for everything noble sink, as they grow older, into idleness and selfishness. But I do not believe that those who go through this very common change deliberately choose the lower kind of pleasure over the higher. I believe that by the time they give themselves over wholly to the one, they have already lost the capacity for the other. In most natures the capacity for the nobler feelings is a very delicate plant, easily killed not only by hostile influences but by simple lack of nourishment; and in most young people it quickly withers if the occupations their station in life has assigned them, and the company it has thrown them into, do not favour keeping that higher capacity in use. People lose their lofty aspirations in the same way they lose their intellectual tastes, because they have neither the time nor the opportunity to indulge them; and they take to inferior pleasures not out of any considered preference for them, but because these are either the only ones within their reach or the only ones they are still able to enjoy. One may doubt whether anyone who has stayed equally responsive to both classes of pleasure has ever, knowingly and calmly, preferred the lower, although many, in every age, have broken down in a fruitless attempt to combine the two. From this verdict of the only competent judges there can, I take it, be no appeal. On the question of which of two pleasures is more worth having, or which of two ways of life is more gratifying to the feelings, setting aside its moral attributes and its consequences, the judgment of those qualified by knowing both, or, where they disagree, the judgment of the majority among them, must be accepted as final. And we need hesitate all the less to accept this judgment about the quality of pleasures, given that there is no other tribunal to turn to even on the question of quantity. What way is there to determine which of two pains is the sharper, or which of two pleasurable sensations the more intense, other than the general vote of those familiar with both? Neither pains nor pleasures are homogeneous, and pain is always heterogeneous with pleasure. What is there to settle whether a given pleasure is worth buying at the price of a given pain, other than the feelings and judgment of people with experience? When, then, those feelings and that judgment pronounce the pleasures that come from the higher faculties to be preferable _in kind_, quite apart from the question of intensity, to those of which animal nature, cut off from the higher faculties, is capable, they deserve the same respect on this matter. I have lingered on this point because it is a necessary part of a fully just conception of Utility, or Happiness, taken as the guiding rule of human conduct. It is by no means, however, an indispensable condition for accepting the utilitarian standard; for that standard is not the agent's own greatest happiness but the greatest sum of happiness overall. And if it can perhaps be doubted whether a noble character is always happier for being noble, there can be no doubt that it makes other people happier, and that the world at large gains enormously from it. Utilitarianism, then, could reach its goal only through the general cultivation of nobility of character, even if each individual benefited solely from the nobility of others, and his own, as far as happiness goes, were a pure subtraction from that benefit. But merely stating so absurd a proposition as this last one makes any refutation unnecessary. Under the Greatest Happiness Principle as explained above, the ultimate end, by reference to which and for the sake of which everything else is desirable (whether the good in question is our own or other people's), is a life as free as possible from pain and as rich as possible in enjoyments, in both quantity and quality; the test of quality, and the rule for weighing it against quantity, being the preference felt by those whose opportunities of experience, together with their habits of self-consciousness and self-observation, best equip them for making the comparison. Since this, on the utilitarian view, is the end of human action, it is necessarily also the standard of morality, which may therefore be defined as the rules and precepts for human conduct through whose observance a life such as I have described might be secured, as far as possible, to all mankind; and not to mankind alone, but, so far as the nature of things allows, to every sentient creature. Against this doctrine, however, another class of objectors rises up, who maintain that happiness in any form cannot be the rational aim of human life and action. In the first place, they say, it cannot be attained, and they ask with contempt what right you have to be happy, a question Mr. Carlyle drives home by asking further what right, a short while ago, you had even _to be_. Next, they claim that people can do _without_ happiness; that every noble human being has felt this, and could have become noble only by learning the lesson of Entsagen, or renunciation, a lesson which, once thoroughly learned and accepted, they declare to be the beginning and necessary condition of all virtue. The first of these objections would strike at the heart of the matter if it were well grounded; for if human beings can have no happiness at all, gaining it cannot be the aim of morality or of any rational conduct. Even then, something could still be said for the utilitarian theory, since utility covers not only the pursuit of happiness but the prevention or lessening of unhappiness; and if the first aim is a fantasy, there will be all the more room for the second and all the more urgent need of it, at least as long as mankind sees fit to go on living and does not escape into the collective act of suicide that Novalis recommended under certain conditions. When, however, it is flatly declared impossible for human life to be happy, the claim is, if not something close to a verbal quibble, at the very least an exaggeration. If happiness is taken to mean an unbroken succession of highly pleasurable excitement, it is plain enough that this cannot be had. A state of exalted pleasure lasts only moments, or in some cases, with some interruptions, hours or days; it is the occasional brilliant flash of enjoyment, not its steady and enduring flame. The philosophers who taught that happiness is the end of life knew this every bit as well as those who mock them. The happiness they had in mind was not a life of rapture but moments of it, set in an existence composed of few and passing pains and many and varied pleasures, with a clear predominance of the active over the passive, and resting, as the foundation of the whole, on not expecting more of life than it is able to give.
