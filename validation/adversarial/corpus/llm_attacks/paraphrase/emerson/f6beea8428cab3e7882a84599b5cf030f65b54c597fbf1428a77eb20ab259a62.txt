He scandalized the company by insisting that God had just two attributes—power and a sense of the ridiculous—and that every devout man was obliged to keep the comedy going. And I have known gentlemen with a large stake in the community, though their sympathies ran cold—college presidents, governors, senators—who considered themselves bound to sign every temperance pledge, to join with Bible societies, missions, and peacemakers, and to cheer on every good dog with a "Hist-a-boy!" We should not push courtesy too far, yet all of us feel kindly urges of this sort. When the boys come into my yard asking leave to gather horse-chestnuts, I confess I take part in Nature's game and pretend to grant permission grudgingly, half afraid that at any moment they will see through the sham of that glossy husk. But such tenderness is entirely needless; the spells are laid on very thick. Their young lives are roofed over with them. The children in the hovel I saw yesterday have a lot so bare and grim it could bring one to tears; yet they decked it out with tawdry romance just as children of the happiest fortune do, and spoke of "the dear cottage where so many joyful hours had flown." Well, this thatching of hovels is simply the custom of the country.

Women, above all, are the element and kingdom of illusion. Being enchanted, they enchant. They look at the world through Claude Lorrain glasses. And who would dare, even if he could, to tear down the side-scenes, the stage effects, and the ceremonies by which they live? The region of affection is too touching, too much to be pitied, and its air is always prone to mirage. We are not greatly at fault for our bad marriages. We live among hallucinations, and this particular snare is set to trip our feet, and every one of us is tripped, sooner or later. But the mighty Mother who has been so sly with us, as though she felt she owed us some amends, slips into the Pandora's box of marriage certain deep and serious benefits and some great joys. We take a delight in the beauty and happiness of children that makes the heart too large for the body. Even in the most ill-matched unions there is always some admixture of true marriage. Teague and his jade arrive at some fair relations of mutual respect, of kindly regard, of nurturing one another; they learn something, and would conduct themselves more wisely if they were beginning now.

It is easy for us to point at this or that splendid madman, as though anyone were exempt. The scholar in his library is not. I, who have heard countless orations and debates over my lifetime, have read poems and books of every kind, and have talked with many men of genius, am still at the mercy of any new page; and if Marmaduke, or Hugh, or Moosehead, or anyone else invents a new style or a new mythology, I imagine the world would be all fine and right if only it were dressed in these colors that had never occurred to me. At once I set about daubing with this new paint, but it will not stick. It is like the cement the peddler sells at the door: he makes broken crockery hold with it, but you can never buy from him a bit of cement that will make it hold once he has gone.

Men who make their mark on the world draw on a certain fate in their makeup, which they know how to put to use. Yet they never interest us deeply unless they lift a corner of the curtain, or reveal ever so slightly that they see what lies behind it. The charm of practical men is that beyond their practicality lie a certain poetry and playfulness, as though they led the good horse Power by the bridle and chose to walk, though they could ride so furiously. Bonaparte is a man of intellect, as Caesar was; and the best soldiers, sea-captains, and railway men have a gentleness about them when off duty, a good-humored acknowledgment that illusions exist, and who can say he is not their plaything? We brand the cast-iron fellows who cannot stand apart in this way as "dragon-ridden," "thunder-stricken," fools of fate, whatever powers they may possess.

Since we are taught through emblems and indirection, it is good to know there is method in it, a fixed scale, rank upon rank among the phantoms. We begin low with crude masks and climb toward the most subtle and beautiful. The red men told Columbus that "they had an herb which took away fatigue"; but he found the illusion of "arriving from the east at the Indies" more soothing to his lofty spirit than any tobacco. Is not our belief in the impenetrability of matter a stronger sedative than any narcotic? You amuse yourself with jackstraws, balls, bowls, horse and gun, estates and politics; but finer games lie ahead of you. Is not time a pretty toy? Life will show you masks worth more than all your carnivals. That mountain over there must move into your mind. The fine star-dust and nebulous haze in Orion, "the portentous year of Mizar and Alcor," must come down and be handled in your household thoughts. What if you should come to see that the stage and playground of all this grand history are rays sent out from yourself, and that the sun borrows his light? What fearful questions we are learning to ask! Men of former times believed in a magic that swallowed up temples, cities, and men and left no trace of them. We are approaching the secret of a magic that sweeps from men's minds every vestige of the theism and the beliefs that they and their fathers held and were shaped by.

There are deceptions of the senses, deceptions of the passions, and the structural, beneficent illusions of sentiment and intellect. There is the illusion of love, which credits the beloved with everything that person shares with family, sex, age, or station, and indeed with the human mind itself. It is these the lover loves, and Anna Matilda receives the credit for them—as if a man shut up forever in a tower with a single window, through which the face of heaven and earth could be seen, should imagine that all the wonders he saw belonged to the window. There is the illusion of time, which runs very deep; who has ever settled it, or come to be convinced that what looks like a succession of thoughts is only the distribution of wholes into causal series? The intellect perceives that every atom carries the whole of Nature; that the mind opens onto omnipotence; that in the endless striving and ascent the transformation is complete, so that the soul does not recognize itself in its own act once that act is perfected. There is illusion that will deceive even the elect. There is illusion that will deceive even the one who works the miracle. Though he makes his body, he denies that he makes it. Though the world exists out of thought, thought is daunted in the presence of the world. One after another we accept the laws of the mind, all the while resisting those that come next, which must nonetheless be accepted. But every concession only drives us to fresh profusion. And what does it profit us that science has come to regard space and time as mere forms of thought, and the material world as hypothetical, and that along with them our claims to property and even to selfhood are fading, if in the end even our thoughts are not final, but the ceaseless flowing and ascent reaches them too, and every thought that was final yesterday gives way today to a larger generalization?

Working in such volatile elements, it is no wonder our estimates are loose and drifting. We must work and affirm, yet we have no notion of the worth of what we say or do. The cloud is now the size of your hand, and now it covers a county. The tale of Thor, who was set to drain the drinking-horn in Asgard, to wrestle with the old woman, and to race against the runner Lok, and who soon discovered that he had been drinking up the sea, wrestling with Time, and racing against Thought, describes us, who among these apparent trifles are contending with the supreme energies of Nature. We imagine we have fallen into bad company and a squalid state of things: petty debts, shoe bills, broken glass to pay for, pots to buy, butcher's meat, sugar, milk, and coal. "Give me some great task, ye gods, and I will show my spirit." "Not so," answers good Heaven; "plod and plow, patch your old coats and hats, weave a shoestring; great affairs and the best wine will come by and by." Well, it is all phantasm; and if we weave a yard of tape in all humility and as well as we can, long afterward we shall see that it was no cotton tape at all but some galaxy we braided, and that its threads were Time and Nature.

We cannot set down the order of the changeable winds. How then can we fathom the law of our shifting moods and sensibilities? Yet they differ as everything differs from nothing. In place of yesterday's firmament, which our eyes require, today there is an eggshell that pens us in; we cannot even make out what or where our stars of destiny are. Day after day the chief facts of human life are hidden from our sight. Then suddenly the mist rolls back and discloses them, and we reflect on how much good time has been lost that might have been saved had any hint of these things been given us. A sudden rise in the road shows us the whole range of mountains and all their peaks, which have been just as close to us all year, yet entirely out of mind.

But these alternations are not without order, and we take part in our varied fortunes. If life seems a series of dreams, poetic justice is still done in dreams. Good men's visions are good; it is the undisciplined will that is scourged with bad thoughts and bad luck. When we break the laws, we lose our grip on the central reality. Like sick men in a hospital, we only change from bed to bed, from one folly to the next; and it can matter little what becomes of such castaways—wailing, dull, comatose creatures—lifted from bed to bed, from the nothing of life to the nothing of death. In this kingdom of illusions we feel about eagerly for supports and foundations. There is none except strict and faithful dealing at home, and a stern exclusion of all duplicity or illusion there. Whatever games are played on us, we must play no games with ourselves, but deal in private with the utmost honesty and truth. I regard the plain and childlike virtues of truthfulness and honesty as the root of everything sublime in character. Speak as you think, be what you are, pay your debts of every kind. I would rather be known as sound and solvent, my word as good as my bond, and be something that cannot be skipped, scattered, or undermined, than have all the éclat in the universe. This reality is the foundation of friendship, religion, poetry, and art.

At the top or the bottom of all illusions I place the cheat that still leads us to work and live for appearances, though we are convinced in every sane hour that it is what we really are that counts with friends, with strangers, and with fate or fortune. To hear men talk, one would suppose that riches and poverty were a great matter, and our civilization does chiefly respect it. But the Indians say they do not think the white man, with his care-lined brow, forever toiling, afraid of heat and cold, and staying indoors, has any advantage over them. Every man's lasting interest is never to be in a false position, but to have the weight of Nature behind him in all he does. Riches and poverty are a heavier or lighter garment, and our life—the life of all of us—is the same. For we continually rise above circumstance and taste the real quality of existence: in our occupations, which differ only in their handling but express the same laws, or in our thoughts, which wear no silks and taste no ice creams. We see God face to face every hour and know the savor of Nature.

The early Greek philosophers Heraclitus and Xenophanes tried their strength on this problem of identity. Diogenes of Apollonia said that unless the atoms were made of one substance, they could never mingle and act upon one another. But the Hindus, in their sacred writings, give the most vivid expression both to the essential identity and to that illusion which they take variety to be: "The notions 'I am' and 'This is mine,' which sway mankind, are but delusions of the mother of the world. Dispel, O Lord of all creatures, the conceit of knowledge which proceeds from ignorance." And they hold that man's blessedness lies in being set free from fascination. The intellect is quickened when truth is put in a figure, and the will when the laws of life are clothed in illusions. But the unities of Truth and Right are not broken by the disguise, and there need never be any confusion about them. In a crowded life of many parts and players, whether on a stage of nations or in the most obscure hamlet in Maine or California, the same elements present the same choices to each newcomer, and by his choosing he settles his fortune in absolute Nature. It would be hard to pack more mental and moral philosophy into a sentence than the Persians have:

"Fooled thou must be, though wisest of the wise:
Then be the fool of virtue, not of vice."

There is no chance and no anarchy in the universe. All is system and gradation. Every god sits there in his own sphere. The young mortal enters the hall of the firmament; there he is alone with them alone, while they pour blessings and gifts upon him and beckon him up to their thrones. At once, and without ceasing, snowstorms of illusion come down. He imagines himself in a vast crowd that sways this way and that, whose movements and actions he must obey; he imagines himself poor, orphaned, and of no account. The frantic crowd rushes to and fro, now fiercely demanding that this be done, now that. Who is he to resist their will and think or act for himself? Every moment brings new changes and new showers of deception to baffle and distract him. And when, after a while, the air clears for an instant and the cloud lifts a little, there are the gods, still seated around him on their thrones—they alone with him alone.
