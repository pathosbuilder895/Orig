In Europe, where feudal arrangements keep fortunes fixed within particular families, it is those families who purchase and keep such treasures and open them to the public. In America, however, where democratic institutions break up every estate into small shares within a few years, the public ought to take the place of those owners and supply the citizen with this culture and inspiration.

Man was made to be rich, or rather, he grows rich as a matter of course through the exercise of his faculties, through the marriage of thought and nature. Property is a product of the intellect. The game demands of its players composure, sound reasoning, quickness, and patience. Trained labor displaces brute labor. Countless shrewd men, over countless years, have worked out certain best and shortest methods of doing things, and this stored-up skill in the arts, in cultivation, harvesting, curing, manufacturing, navigation, and exchange, is what gives our world its value today.

Commerce is a game of skill that not every man can play, and that few play well. The true merchant possesses that fair average of faculties which we call common sense: he is a man strongly drawn to facts, who bases his decisions on what he has seen with his own eyes. He is wholly convinced of the truths of arithmetic. There is always a cause, located in the man himself, for his good or ill fortune, and the same holds for making money. People speak as though some magic were involved, and indeed they believe in magic in every department of life. But he knows that everything travels the old road, pound for pound and cent for cent—that every effect has its exact cause—and that good luck is simply another word for steadiness of purpose. He protects himself in every transaction and prefers small, certain profits. Honesty and a close grip on facts form the foundation, but the masters of the craft add to these a kind of long-range arithmetic. The task is to unite many distant and complicated operations with the same precision and faithfulness to fact that come easily in small, nearby dealings, and so to reach enormous results without sacrificing safety.

Napoleon liked to tell of the banker of Marseilles whose visitor was struck by the contrast between the splendor of the banker's chateau and table and the shabbiness of the counting-room where he had first met him. The banker told him: "Young man, you are too young to understand how masses are formed—the true and only power. Whether it is made of money, water, or men, it is all the same. A mass is a vast center of motion, but it has to be started, and it has to be sustained." He might have added that the way to start and sustain it is by obeying the law of particles. Success lies in close adherence to the laws of the world, and because those laws are intellectual and moral, success requires an intellectual and moral obedience. Political Economy is as fit a book for reading the life of man, and the supremacy of law over every private and hostile influence, as any Bible that has been handed down to us.

Money is a representative, and it takes on the character and fortunes of whoever owns it. The coin is a sensitive gauge of civil, social, and moral change. The farmer guards his dollar jealously, and he has good reason to. It did not come to him by chance. He knows how many strokes of labor stand behind it. His bones still ache from the day's work that earned it. He knows how much land it stands for—how much rain, frost, and sun. He knows that when he hands you that dollar, he hands you so much judgment and patience, so much hoeing and threshing. Try to pick up his dollar and you must pick up all that weight along with it. In the city, where money comes from the stroke of a pen or a fortunate turn in the exchange, people come to regard it as light. I wish the farmer valued it still more highly and would spend it only on real bread, force for force. The farmer's dollar is heavy; the clerk's is light and quick, springing out of his pocket and onto the card tables and faro boards.

Stranger still is the way money responds to changes that are not physical at all. It is the most delicate barometer of social storms, and it gives warning of revolutions. Each advance in civilization makes every man's dollar worth more. In California, the very land where it was dug, what could it purchase? Only a few years ago it would buy a shack, dysentery, hunger, bad company, and crime. There are vast regions, such as Siberia, where even now it would buy little beyond some slight relief from suffering. In Rome it buys beauty and grandeur. Forty years ago a dollar did not go far in Boston. Today it goes a good deal further in our old town, thanks to railroads, telegraphs, steamships, and the simultaneous growth of New York and of the whole nation. Still, there are many goods belonging to a capital city that cannot yet be bought here, not even with a mountain of dollars. A dollar in Florida is not worth a dollar in Massachusetts. A dollar is not value itself but a representative of value, and, in the end, of moral value.

A dollar is valued for the corn it can buy, or, to put it precisely, not for corn or shelter as such, but for Athenian corn and Roman shelter—for the wit, honesty, and power that we eat bread and live in houses in order to share and exercise. Wealth is a thing of the mind; wealth is a thing of morals. The worth of a dollar lies in buying the right things, and a dollar keeps gaining value with all the genius and all the virtue in the world. A dollar in a university is worth more than a dollar in a jail; more in a sober, educated, law-abiding community than in some den of crime where dice, knives, and arsenic are always at work. The "Bank-Note Detector" is a handy publication. But the dollar in circulation, whether silver or paper, is itself the detector of the right and wrong in the place where it passes. Does it not rise at once in value as fairness increases? If a trader declines to sell his vote, or holds firm to some unpopular right, he adds just that much equity to Massachusetts, and every acre in the State is worth more in the hour he acts.

Remove from State Street the ten most honest merchants and replace them with ten rogues commanding the same capital, and the insurance rates will register it; the soundness of the banks will reveal it; the highways will grow less safe; the schools will feel it; the children will carry home their small portion of the poison; the judge will sit less steadily on the bench and his rulings will be less just, for he has lost that much of the support and restraint everyone needs; and the pulpit will betray it in a looser standard of conduct. Take from around the roots of an apple tree a load of loam every day for several days and put a load of sand in its place, and the tree will discover it. An apple tree is a dull sort of creature, but if this went on for a little while, I believe it would begin to suspect something. And if you removed a hundred good men from the powerful class engaged in trade and put in a hundred bad ones, or, what amounts to the same thing, brought in some corrupting institution, would not the dollar, which is hardly duller than an apple tree, soon discover it?

The value of a dollar is social, since society creates it. Every man who moves into this city bringing any marketable talent or skill lends fresh value to the labor of every other man there. When a talent is born anywhere in the world, the whole community of nations is the richer for it; and still more so with each new measure of honesty. The cost of crime, among the largest expenses of every nation, is to that degree checked. In Europe it has been noticed that crime rises and falls with the price of bread. If the Rothschilds in Paris refuse to accept bills, the people of Manchester, Paisley, and Birmingham are driven onto the highways, and landlords are shot down in Ireland. The police records bear witness to it. The tremors are soon felt in New York, New Orleans, and Chicago. In much the same way, economic power reaches the masses by way of the political lords. Rothschild declines the Russian loan, and there is peace and the harvests are spared. He accepts it, and there is war, and a commotion through a great part of mankind, with every dreadful consequence, ending in revolution and a new order of things.

Wealth carries its own checks and balances. The foundation of political economy is non-interference. The only safe rule lies in the self-regulating gauge of supply and demand. Do not legislate. Interfere, and your sumptuary laws will cut the sinews. Grant no bounties; make the laws equal; protect life and property, and you will have no need to give alms. Throw open the doors of opportunity to talent and virtue, and they will see that justice is done to themselves, and property will not fall into the wrong hands. In a free and just commonwealth, property flows away from the idle and feeble toward the hardworking, the brave, and the persevering. The laws of nature operate through trade just as a toy battery displays the effects of electricity. The sea does not keep its level more surely than supply and demand keep the balance of value in society, and contrivance or legislation brings its own punishment in reactions, gluts, and bankruptcies. The sublime laws work impartially through atoms and through galaxies. Whoever understands what takes place in earning and spending for a loaf of bread and a pint of beer—that no amount of wishing will alter the strict limits of pints and penny loaves; that whatever is consumed leaves that much less in the basket and the pot; but that what has gone out of them is not wasted but well spent if it feeds his body and lets him finish his work—knows all the political economy that the budgets of empires could teach him.

What makes small economies interesting is the way they stand as a symbol of the great economy: the way a household, and one private man's methods, correspond to the solar system and to the laws of giving and taking that run through all of nature. However much we guard against the lies and petty tricks we play on one another to our own ruin, every man feels a certain satisfaction whenever his dealings come up against the inevitable facts, when he sees that things themselves set the price, as they always tend to do, and as can be seen plainly in large manufactures. Your paper is not fine enough, or not coarse enough; it is too heavy, or too thin. The manufacturer tells you he will supply exactly the thickness or thinness you want; the pattern makes no difference to him; here is his price list, every variety of paper, cheaper or dearer, with its price beside it. A pound of paper costs so much, and you may have it made up in whatever pattern you like.

There is in all our dealings a self-regulation that makes haggling unnecessary. You want to rent a house, but you insist on having it cheap. The owner can lower the rent, but in doing so he leaves himself unable to make proper repairs, so the tenant ends up not with the house he wanted but a worse one; and on top of that, a slightly harmful relation is set up between landlord and tenant. You let your laborer go, telling him, "Patrick, I'll send for you as soon as I can't get along without you." Patrick leaves content, because he knows the weeds will come up with the potatoes, the vines have to be planted next week, and, however reluctant you may be, the cantaloupes, crooknecks, and cucumbers will send for him. Who would not wish that all labor and all value rested on the same plain and unbending market? If it is the best of its kind, it will. We need the joiner, the locksmith, the planter, the priest, the poet, the doctor, the cook, the weaver, and the ostler, each in his turn over the course of the year.

If a St. Michael's pear sells for a shilling, it costs a shilling to grow. If the best securities in Boston pay twelve per cent for money, they carry exactly six per cent of insecurity. You may not notice that the fine pear costs you a shilling, but it costs the community that much. The shilling stands for the number of enemies the pear has and the amount of risk in bringing it to ripeness. The price of coal reveals how narrow the coal field is and how the miners are forcibly confined to a particular district. All salaries are calculated on services that may be needed as well as on services actually performed. "If the wind always blew southwest by west," said the skipper, "women could take ships to sea." One might say that all things carry one price; that nothing is cheap or dear; and that the apparent differences that catch our eye are only a shopkeeper's trick for hiding the loss in your bargain.

A young man arrives in the city from his family's New Hampshire farm, the meager fare there still fresh in his memory. He takes lodging at a first-class hotel and supposes he must somehow have got the better of Dr. Franklin and Malthus, since luxuries are so cheap. But he pays for the single comfort of a better dinner by giving up some of the richest social and educational advantages. What safeguards he has lost! What spurs to effort! He may discover in time that he left the Muses at the hotel door and met the Furies inside. Money often costs too much, and power and pleasure do not come cheap. The ancient poet said that the gods sell all things at a fair price.

The commercial history of this country offers an instance of these compensations. When the European wars, from 1800 to 1812, threw the carrying trade of the world into American ships, now and then one of them was seized. The loss was naturally heavy for the owner, but the country was made whole, since we charged threepence a pound to carry cotton, sixpence for tobacco, and so on, which covered the risk and the losses and brought into the country enormous prosperity, early marriages, private fortunes, and the building of cities and states. And when the war ended, we were paid, by treaty, compensation on top of all that for every seizure. So the Americans grew rich and powerful. But the day of reckoning comes around. Britain, France, and Germany, impoverished by our extraordinary profits and drawn by the reputation of our advantages, send over first their thousands and then their millions of poor people to share in the harvest. At first we put them to work and add to our prosperity; but in the artificial system of society and of protected labor, which we too have taken up and expanded, checks and stoppages soon appear.
