It occurred to me that the profile of this mountain at the southwest of the lake, and of a second one lying past it, resembled not only the enormous rock waves along Webster Stream but, broadly speaking, Kineo on Moosehead Lake, with a comparable though gentler cliff at its southeastern end; in a word, every conspicuous hill and ridge in the neighborhood seemed to me a Kineo of greater or lesser size, and I suspected some kinship might exist between Kineo and the rocks of Webster Stream. Our Indian was unsure of the outlet’s exact position, whether it lay in the far southwest corner or farther to the east; at our last halt he had asked to look at my plan, but it had slipped my mind to show him. As was his habit, he groped his way forward along a line midway between the two likely spots, so that in the end he could turn toward either without losing much distance. As we neared the southern shore, the clouds having a gusty look and the waves running fairly high, we held a course that put us partly in the shelter of an island, though we were a long way off from it. I could not make out the outlet until we had all but entered it and could hear the water pouring over the dam. There was a sizable fall here and a very solid dam, yet no trace of cabin or camp. The hunter we had met at Telos Lake had assured us that trout were plentiful at this spot, but at that hour none would take the bait except cousin trout, which came from the very heart of the rushing current. These rivers hold fewer fish than the Concord does. While we lingered, Polis seized the chance to shave some of the hair from his moose-hide with his big knife, making it lighter and readying it for drying. At a number of old Indian camps in the woods I had seen the heaps of hair cut from their hides. After carrying around the dam, he shot down the rapids and left us to go on foot for a mile or more, mostly with no path at all, over very dense and troublesome ground beside the stream. Now and then he would call out to tell us where he was waiting with the canoe, for the stream wound so much that we could not tell where the shore lay; but he did not call often enough, forgetting that we were not Indians ourselves. He seemed very sparing of his breath—and yet he would be astonished if we walked past him or failed to come out at the right place. This was no sign of disobligingness, but rather evidence of finer manners. Indians prefer to manage with the least possible talk and fuss. All along he was really paying us a high compliment, supposing that we would rather have a hint than a kick. At last, clambering over the willows and fallen trunks wherever that was easier than going around or beneath them, we caught up with the canoe and slid down the stream on smooth but rapid water for several miles. Here I noticed once more, as at Webster Stream, and on a grander scale still the following day, that the river formed a smooth, evenly sloping plane down which we coasted. Gliding along in this way, we flushed the first black ducks we had recognized as such. We resolved to camp early that night so as to have plenty of time before dark, and accordingly put in at the first suitable shore, a narrow gravelly beach on the west side about five miles below the lake’s outlet. It was an interesting place, where the river began a great bend toward the east, and the last of the curious moose-faced Nerlumskeechticook Mountains, not far southwest of Grand Lake, stood up dark in the northwest a little way behind us, showing its gray, steep southeastern face, though we could see it only by stepping out onto the shore. Take two steps from the water on either bank and you reach the sudden edge of the bank, bushy and full of roots if not of turf, four or five feet high, where the endless forest commences, as though the stream had only just sliced its way through. Wherever you step ashore in this unbroken wilderness, it is surprising how often you find, at least within a few rods of the river, axe marks left by lumbermen who camped there or drove logs past in earlier springs. Perhaps you will see where, bent on the same business as yourself, they hacked big chips from a tall white-pine stump to feed their fire. While we were setting up camp and preparing supper, the Indian trimmed the remaining hair from his moose-hide and then stretched it upright on a makeshift frame between two small trees, about six feet from the far side of the fire, binding and drawing it tight with arbor-vitæ bark, which was always handy and in this instance was peeled from one of the very trees it was fastened to. When we asked for a new sort of tea, he brewed us some, quite good, from the checkerberry (_Gaultheria procumbens_) that carpeted the ground, dropping a small bunch of it tied with cedar bark into the kettle; still, it did not quite match the _Chiogenes_. For this reason we named the place Checkerberry-Tea Camp. I was impressed by how plentiful the _Linnæa borealis_, the checkerberry, and the _Chiogenes hispidula_ were nearly everywhere in the Maine woods. The wintergreen (_Chimaphila umbellata_) was still flowering here, and clintonia berries were ripe and abundant; this attractive plant is among the commonest in that forest. Here too we first saw the moose-wood bearing fruit along the banks. The chief trees were spruce (usually black), arbor-vitæ, canoe birch (with black ash and elms starting to show), yellow birch, red maple, and a little hemlock lurking within the forest. The Indian told us that white-maple punk made the best tinder, and that yellow-birch punk was fairly good, though hard. After supper he set the moose’s tongue and lips on to boil, first cutting out the _septum_. He taught me how to write on the under side of birch bark with a black-spruce twig, which is hard and tough and will take a point. Just before nightfall the Indian strayed a little way into the woods, and when he came back he told us he had found a great treasure, worth fifty or sixty dollars. We asked what it was. Steel traps, he said, thirty or forty of them under a log; he hadn’t counted them; Indian work, he guessed, and worth three dollars apiece. It was a strange chance that, in that pathless forest, he should have happened to walk to that one particular log and look beneath it. When I washed my hands in the stream I saw chivin and chub, though my companion tried in vain to catch them. I heard bullfrogs, too, calling from a swamp on the far side, and took them at first for moose; a duck paddled quickly past; and as I sat in that shadowy wilderness beneath that dark mountain, beside the bright river brimming with reflected light, I still heard the wood thrush singing, as though no loftier civilization were possible. By now night had come upon us. As a rule you make camp right at sunset, and you are gathering wood, cooking supper, or putting up your tent while the darkness closes in around you and deepens the forest’s already heavy gloom. There is no time to explore or look about before night falls. You may push half a dozen rods deeper into that dim wilderness in search of dry bark for kindling, and wonder what secrets still lie concealed farther in, say at the close of a long day’s tramp; or you may hurry down to the bank for a dipper of water and get a clearer look a short way up or down the stream, and as you stand there you may see a fish jump, or a duck settle on the river, or hear a wood thrush or a robin singing in the woods. That is as though you had paid a visit to town or to settled country. But there is no strolling off to view the land; ten or fifteen rods feel like a great distance from your companions, and you return with the air of a seasoned traveler home from a long journey with adventures to tell, though the crackle of the fire may never have been out of your hearing—and at a hundred rods you might be hopelessly lost and obliged to camp out. Everything is mossy and _moosey_. In some of those thick fir and spruce woods there is scarcely space for the smoke to rise. The trees are a _standing_ night, and each fir or spruce you cut down is a feather plucked from the raven wing of night. Then after dark the prevailing silence is more striking than any noise, though now and then you hear an owl, sometimes far off and sometimes close by in the woods, and, if a lake is near, the half-human cry of the loons at their unearthly revels. That night the Indian lay down between the fire and his stretched moose-hide to escape the mosquitoes. He even made a small smoky fire of damp leaves at his head and another at his feet, and then, as usual, wrapped his head up in his blanket. With our veils and our wash we were fairly comfortable, but at this season it would be hard to carry on any sedentary work in the woods; in the evening you can scarcely read by firelight through a veil, nor manage pencil and paper well with gloved or greased fingers.

FRIDAY, July 31. The Indian told us that since he and I had killed the moose the night before, we ought to use the best wood on it, for moose-meat should always be cooked over hard wood. His “best wood” turned out to be rock maple. He tossed the moose’s lip into the fire to singe the hair off, then rolled it up with the meat to take along. Seeing that we were sitting down to breakfast with no pork, he said with a very solemn face, “Me want some fat,” and was told he could have as much as he cared to fry. For a good distance the water was smooth but swift, and we sped along, startling up ducks and kingfishers. But, as usual, our easy going soon came to an end, and we had to carry the canoe and everything else about half a mile down the right bank to get around some rapids or falls. At times it took keen eyes to tell on which side the carry lay before you went over the falls, but Polis never once failed to land us in the right place. Raspberries were especially plentiful and large here, and everyone fell to eating them, the Indian remarking on how big they were. On bare, rocky carries the trail was often so faint that I lost it again and again; yet when I walked behind him I saw that he could follow it almost like a hound, seldom faltering, or if he did stop a moment on a bare rock, his eye at once picked out some sign that I would have missed. Often _we_ could find no path at all in such places, and were held up in a way he could not account for. All he would say was that it was “ver strange.” We had heard that there was a Grand Fall on this stream, and supposed that each fall we came to must be it; but after giving the name to several in a row, we abandoned the hunt. There were more Grand or Petty Falls than I can recall, and I could not say how many times we had to go on foot because of falls or rapids. We kept expecting the river to make one last plunge and reach smooth water, but nothing improved that morning. Still, the carries made a pleasant change. As sure as we got out of the canoe and stretched our legs, we found ourselves in a garden of blueberries and raspberries, our rocky trail around the falls bordered on both sides by one kind or the other or both. On the main East Branch there was not a single carry where we failed to find plenty of both berries, since these were the rockiest spots and partly cleared, just the sort of ground those plants like, and nobody had come before us to pick the best. On our three trips across each carry—for whenever the canoe came out of the water we had to cover the ground three times—we gave the berries their due, and they were exactly what we needed to offset our diet of hard bread and pork. Making a portage might as well have been called going a-berrying. We also came upon a few amelanchier, or service, berries, most of them abortive, though they held on more generally than they do in Concord. The Indian called them _pemoymenuk_, and said that in some places they bore a great deal of fruit. Now and then he also ate the northern wild red cherries, calling them good medicine, though they were hardly fit to eat. We bathed and had dinner at the foot of one of these carries. It was usually the Indian who reminded us that dinner-time had come, sometimes simply by turning the bow toward shore. Once he offered a roundabout but lengthy apology, saying that we might find it odd, but that a man who worked hard all day was very particular about having his dinner on time. At the largest fall on this stream, as I was crossing the carry close on the Indian’s heels, he noticed a track on the rock, which had only a thin covering of soil, and, bending down, muttered “caribou.” On our way back he spotted a much larger track near the same place, where some animal’s foot had sunk into a small hollow in the rock half filled with grass and earth, and he cried out in surprise, “What that?” “Well, what is it?” I asked. Crouching and laying his hand in it, he answered with an air of mystery, almost in a whisper, that it was the Devil (meaning the Indian Devil, or cougar), that there were ledges about here, that it was a very bad animal, one that would pull the rocks all to pieces. When I asked how long ago it had been made, he said today or yesterday. But when I asked him later whether he was sure it was the devil’s track, he admitted he did not know. I had been told that a cougar’s scream had lately been heard near Ktaadn, and we were not far from that mountain.
