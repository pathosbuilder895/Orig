last-named Indian described the _lunxus_, or Indian devil (which I take to be the cougar rather than the _Gulo luscus_), as the one animal in Maine that a man had any reason to fear; it would trail a person and paid no attention to a fire. He added that beavers were becoming fairly plentiful again in the country we had passed through, but that their pelts now fetched so little that hunting them did not pay. Wishing to keep the ears of our moose, which measured ten inches in length, I had hung them to dry over the fire along with the meat; but Sabattis told me I would have to skin and cure them, or else all the hair would fall out. He mentioned that they made tobacco pouches from the skin of the ears, placing the two together with their inner sides facing. When I asked him how he made fire, he brought out a small cylindrical box of friction matches. He had flint and steel as well, and some punk, which was not dry; I believe it came from the yellow birch. “But suppose you capsize, and these and your powder all get soaked?” “In that case,” he replied, “we wait until we reach some place where there is fire.” I took from my pocket a small vial of matches, corked watertight, and told him that even if we were upset we would still have dry matches; at this he stared at me without a word. We lay awake a long time talking in this way, and they explained to us the meanings of many Indian names of the lakes and streams round about—Tahmunt in particular. I asked for the Indian name of Moosehead Lake. Joe said it was _Sebamook_; Tahmunt pronounced it _Sebemook_. When I asked what it signified, they answered: Moosehead Lake. Finally, catching my drift, they took turns repeating the word to themselves, as a philologist might—_Sebamook_, _Sebamook_—now and then comparing notes in Indian, since their dialects differed slightly; and at last Tahmunt exclaimed, “Ugh! I know,” and, half raising himself on the moose-hide and pointing to different parts of it, explained that it was as if there were one place here and another place there, and you took water from there and filled this one, and it stayed here; that was _Sebamook_. I took him to mean a reservoir of water that did not flow away, the river entering on one side and leaving again close to the same spot, so that a permanent bay remained. Another Indian said it meant Large Bay Lake, and that _Sebago_ and _Sebec_, the names of other lakes, were related words signifying large open water. According to Joe, _Seboois_ meant Little River. I noticed in them that inability to convey an abstract idea which has so often been described. Once they had grasped the idea, however dimly, they fumbled about in vain for words to express it. Tahmunt believed the whites called it Moosehead Lake because Mount Kineo, which overlooks it, has the shape of a moose’s head, and that Moose River got its name because the mountain points straight across the lake to the river’s mouth. John Josselyn, writing around 1673, reports that twelve miles from Casco Bay, and reachable by men and horses, lies a lake the Indians call Sebug, and that on its edge, at one end, stands the famous rock in the shape of a moose deer or helk, “diaphanous,” known as the Moose Rock. He seems to have mixed up Sebamook with Sebago, which lies nearer, but has no “diaphanous” rock on its shore. I record more of their definitions for whatever they may be worth—in part _because_ they sometimes depart from the ones generally accepted. They had never before analyzed these words. After lengthy deliberation and repetition of the word—for it caused him a great deal of trouble—Tahmunt said that _Chesuncook_ meant a place into which many streams emptied (?), and he listed them: Penobscot, Umbazookskus, Cusabesex, Red Brook, and so on. When we asked about _Caucomgomoc_, he answered by asking what those big white birds were called. “Gulls,” I told him. “Ugh! Gull Lake.” Joe thought _Pammadumcook_ meant the Lake with a Gravelly Bottom or Bed. As for _Kenduskeag_, Tahmunt, after asking whether birches went up it—for he said he did not know it well—finally decided it meant something like this: you go up the Penobscot until you come to _Kenduskeag_, and you pass it by without turning up there; that is _Kenduskeag_ (?). Another Indian, though, who knew the river better, told us later that it meant Little Eel River. _Mattawamkeag_ was a place where two rivers come together (?). _Penobscot_ was Rocky River. One writer states that the name at first belonged only to one stretch of the main channel, running from the head of tidewater to a little way above Oldtown. A highly intelligent Indian whom we met later, a son-in-law of Neptune, also supplied these further definitions: _Umbazookskus_, Meadow Stream; _Millinoket_, Place of Islands; _Aboljacarmegus_, Smooth-Ledge Falls (and Deadwater); _Aboljacarmeguscook_, the stream emptying in (this last was the word he offered when I asked about _Aboljacknagesic_, which he did not recognize); _Mattahumkeag_, Sand-Creek Pond; _Piscataquis_, Branch of a River. I asked our hosts the meaning of _Musketaquid_, the Indian name of Concord, Massachusetts; but they altered it to _Musketicook_ and repeated that, and Tahmunt said it meant Dead Stream, which is likely correct. _Cook_ seems to mean stream, and _quid_ perhaps denotes the place or ground. When I asked what the names of two of our hills meant, they replied that those belonged to another language. Since Tahmunt said he traded at Quebec, my companion asked him the meaning of the word _Quebec_, which has been so much disputed. He did not know, but began to guess. He asked what the great ships that carried soldiers were called. “Men-of-war,” we told him. Well, he said, when the English ships sailed up the river they could go no farther, because it was so narrow there, and they had to go back—“go-back”—and that was Que-bec. I mention this to show how much his authority is worth in the other instances. Late at night the two other Indians returned from moose-hunting empty-handed, stirred up the fire, lit their pipes and smoked for a while, had something strong to drink, ate some moose meat, and then, finding what space they could, lay down on the moose-hides; and so we spent the night, two white men and four Indians lying side by side. When I woke in the morning it was drizzling. For lack of room, one of the Indians was lying outside, wrapped in his blanket, on the far side of the fire. Joe had failed to wake my companion, so he had done no hunting that night. Tahmunt was shaping a cross-bar for his canoe with a knife of peculiar form, of a kind I have since seen other Indians use. The blade was thin, about three quarters of an inch broad and eight or nine inches long, but bent out of its plane into a hook, which he said made it handier for shaving. Since Indians far to the north and northwest use the same sort of knife, I suspect it followed a native pattern, though some white craftsmen may use one like it. For their breakfast the Indians baked a loaf of flour bread in a spider propped on its edge before the fire; and while my companion made tea, I caught a dozen good-sized fish in the Penobscot, two kinds of sucker and one trout. After we had eaten our breakfast by ourselves, one of our bedfellows, who had also had his, came by and, on being invited, drank a cup of tea, and at last picked up the common platter and licked it clean. But he was nothing compared with a white man, a lumberer, who kept cramming himself with the Indians’ moose meat and was the laughingstock of his companions on that account. He apparently believed it was a feast “to eat all.” It is often said that the white man ends by outdoing the Indian on the Indian’s own ground, and this case bore it out. I cannot vouch for how he occupied himself through the night, but I saw him at it again as soon as day broke, though he walked a quarter of a mile to get to his work. The rain kept us from staying any longer in the woods; so, after giving some of our provisions and utensils to the Indians, we said good-bye to them. Since this was the day the steamer ran, I started for the lake at once. I crossed the carry alone and waited at the head of the lake. As I came near, an eagle, or some other large bird, left its perch by the shore and flew off screaming. For an hour after I reached the shore no other human being was in sight, and I had that whole broad view to myself. I believed I heard the steamer before she appeared on the open lake. When she came in, I noticed at the landing one of our bedfellows who had been out moose-hunting the night before, now very smartly turned out in a clean white shirt and fine black trousers, a genuine Indian dandy, who had plainly come across the carry to display himself to any newcomers on the north shore of Moosehead Lake, just as New York dandies stroll up Broadway and stand on the steps of a hotel. Halfway down the lake we took aboard two manly-looking men of middle age, with their batteau, who had spent six weeks exploring as far as the Canada line and had let their beards grow. They had with them the skin of a beaver they had lately caught, stretched on an oval hoop, though the fur was poor at that time of year. I talked with one of them, telling him that I had come all this way partly to see where the white pine grew, the Eastern stuff our houses are built of, but that on this trip and on an earlier one into another part of Maine I had found it a scarce tree; and I asked him where I ought to look for it. He smiled and said he could hardly tell me. Still, he said he had found enough to keep two teams busy the coming winter in a place where people believed none was left. What passed for a “tip-top” tree now would not have been looked at twenty years ago, when he first entered the business; but they did very well now with timber that was then considered decidedly inferior. The explorer used to cut into a tree higher and higher up to find out whether it was false-hearted, and if it had a rotten heart as thick as his arm he left it standing; but now they felled such a tree and sawed all around the rot, and it yielded the very best boards, since in that case they were never shaky. A man engaged in lumbering at Bangor told me that the largest pine his firm owned, cut the winter before, “scaled” four thousand five hundred feet in the woods and was worth ninety dollars in the log at the Bangor boom in Oldtown. They cut a road three and a half miles long for that one tree. In his view, the main source of the white pine now coming down the Penobscot lay at the head of the East Branch and the Allegash, around Webster Stream and Eagle and Chamberlain lakes. A great deal of timber has been stolen from the public lands. (What sort of forest-warden, I should like to know, is the Public itself?) I heard of one man who found some especially fine trees just inside the boundary of the public lands and, not daring to take on an accomplice, felled them himself, and with block and tackle, and no oxen, rolled them into a stream, and so made off with them without any help at all. Surely stealing pine trees in this fashion is not as mean as robbing hen-roosts. We reached Monson that night, and the following day rode to Bangor, once again in the rain the whole way, varying our route a little. Some of the taverns along this road, which were especially dirty, were clearly in a stage of transition from the camp to the house.

* * * * *

The next morning we went to Oldtown. A thin old Indian on the Oldtown shore, who knew my companion, was all merriment and gestures, like a Frenchman. A Catholic priest crossed over to the island in the same batteau with us. The Indians’ houses are framed, mostly one story high, and stand in rows one behind another at the south end of the island, with a few scattered about. I counted around forty, not counting the church and what my companion called the council-house. This last, which I take to be their town-house, was properly framed and shingled like the others. Several were two stories, quite neat, with fenced front yards, and at least one had green blinds. Moose-hides were stretched to dry here and there about them. There were no cart-roads and no hoofprints of horses, only footpaths; very little land under cultivation, but plenty of weeds, both native and naturalized; more imported weeds than useful vegetables, as the Indian is said to take up the white man’s vices rather than his virtues. Even so, the village was cleaner than I had expected, far cleaner than such Irish villages as I have seen. The children were not especially ragged or dirty. The little boys came to meet us with bows in their hands and arrows on the string, crying, “Put up a cent.” Truly the Indian’s grip on his bow is a weak one now; but the white man’s curiosity has no bounds, and from the beginning he has been eager to watch this woodland skill. That springy piece of wood and its feathered dart, certain to be unstrung by contact with civilization, will do as the emblem, the coat-of-arms, of the savage. Alas for the Hunter Race! The white man has chased away their game and put a cent in its place. I saw an Indian woman doing her washing at the water’s edge. She stood on a rock, dipped the clothes in the stream, then laid them on the rock and beat them with a short club. In the graveyard, which was packed with graves and overgrown with weeds, I noticed an inscription in the Indian language painted on a wooden grave-board. A large wooden cross stood on the island. Because my companion was acquainted with him, we paid a call on Governor Neptune, who lived in a small “ten-footer,” one of the humblest of them all.
