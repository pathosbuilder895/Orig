"What in the devil's name do you mean?" the Professor demanded.

"The first record of the Symes dates from the fourteenth century," said Syme; "though tradition has it that one of them rode at Bruce's back at Bannockburn. From 1350 onward the family tree is perfectly clear."

"He has lost his wits," said the little Doctor, gaping at him.

"Our arms," Syme went on calmly, "are 'argent a chevron gules charged with three cross crosslets of the field.' The motto is not always the same."

The Professor grabbed Syme roughly by the waistcoat. "We are nearly at the shore," he said. "Are you seasick, or are you making jokes at the wrong moment?"

"What I am saying is almost painfully practical," Syme replied, quite unhurried. "The house of St. Eustache is a very old one as well. The Marquis cannot deny that he is a gentleman. Nor can he deny that I am one. And to settle the question of my social standing beyond any doubt, I intend, at the first chance I get, to knock his hat off his head. But here we are in the harbour."

They came ashore beneath the fierce sun in a kind of stupor. Syme, who had now assumed the lead just as Bull had done in London, brought them along a sort of seafront promenade until he reached a few cafés, half buried in masses of greenery and looking out over the sea. As he walked ahead of them there was a slight swagger in his gait, and he swung his walking stick as though it were a sword. He seemed to be heading for the very last of the row of cafés, but he halted suddenly. With a quick gesture he signalled them to keep quiet, and with one gloved finger he pointed to a café table beneath a bank of blossoming leaves. At it sat the Marquis de St. Eustache, his teeth gleaming in his dense black beard, his bold brown face shaded by a light yellow straw hat and set off against the violet sea.

CHAPTER X. THE DUEL

Syme took a seat at a café table with his friends, his blue eyes glittering like the shining sea beneath them, and called for a bottle of Saumur with delighted impatience. For some reason he was in a state of odd merriment. His spirits had already been unnaturally high; they climbed as the level of the Saumur dropped, and within half an hour his talk had become a flood of nonsense. He claimed to be drawing up a scheme of the conversation that was about to take place between himself and the deadly Marquis. He scribbled it down frantically in pencil. It was set out like a printed catechism, in questions and answers, and he read it out at astonishing speed.

"I shall go up to him. Before I take off his hat, I shall take off my own. I shall say, 'The Marquis de Saint Eustache, I believe.' He will say, 'The famous Mr. Syme, I presume.' He will say in the most elegant French, 'How are you?' I shall answer in the most elegant Cockney, 'Oh, just the Syme—'"

"Oh, stop it," said the man in spectacles. "Get a grip on yourself, and throw away that scrap of paper. What do you actually mean to do?"

"But it was a beautiful catechism," Syme said plaintively. "Please let me read it to you. There are only forty-three questions and answers, and a few of the Marquis's replies are marvellously witty. I like to do justice to my enemy."

"But what is the point of it all?" Dr. Bull asked in exasperation.

"It builds up to my challenge, don't you see," Syme said, beaming. "When the Marquis has given his thirty-ninth answer, which goes—"

"Has it by any chance struck you," the Professor asked with heavy simplicity, "that the Marquis may not say all forty-three of the things you have written down for him? In that event, I gather, your own epigrams might seem rather more strained."

Syme slapped the table, his face shining. "Why, how true that is," he said, "and it never once occurred to me. Sir, your intellect is out of the ordinary. You will make your mark."

"Oh, you're drunk as an owl!" said the Doctor.

"All that is left," Syme went on, entirely undisturbed, "is to find some other way of breaking the ice (if I may put it so) between myself and the man I mean to kill. And since the course of a conversation cannot be foreseen by only one of those taking part in it (as you have observed with such profound penetration), the only thing for it, I suppose, is for that one party to carry on the entire conversation by himself, so far as he can. And so I shall, by George!"

And he rose abruptly to his feet, his yellow hair stirring in the light breeze off the sea. A band was playing in a café chantant tucked away somewhere among the trees, and a woman had just finished singing. To Syme's heated brain the blare of the brass band sounded like the grinding and jangling of that barrel-organ in Leicester Square to whose music he had once stood up to die. He looked over at the small table where the Marquis was sitting. The man now had two companions, grave Frenchmen in frock-coats and silk hats, one of whom wore the red rosette of the Legion of Honour; plainly they were men of solid standing in society. Beside these black, cylindrical outfits, the Marquis, in his loose straw hat and pale spring clothes, looked Bohemian, even barbarous; yet he looked every inch the Marquis. One might even say he looked a king, with his animal grace, his contemptuous eyes, and his proud head raised against the purple sea. But he was certainly no Christian king; he was more like some dark-skinned tyrant, half Greek and half Asiatic, who in the age when slavery seemed the natural order gazed down upon the Mediterranean, upon his galley and his groaning slaves. In just this way, Syme thought, the brown and golden face of such a despot would have stood out against the dark green of the olives and the blazing blue.

"Are you planning to address the meeting?" asked the Professor irritably, noticing that Syme was still standing there motionless.

Syme emptied his last glass of sparkling wine.

"I am," he said, pointing over at the Marquis and his friends, "that meeting there. I do not care for that meeting. I intend to pull that meeting's big, ugly, mahogany-coloured nose."

He crossed over quickly, if not altogether steadily. The Marquis, catching sight of him, raised his black Assyrian brows in surprise, but gave a courteous smile.

"You are Mr. Syme, I believe," he said.

Syme bowed.

"And you are the Marquis de Saint Eustache," he said elegantly. "Allow me to pull your nose."

He bent forward to do it, but the Marquis jerked backward, knocking over his chair, and the two men in top hats caught Syme by the shoulders and held him back.

"This man has insulted me!" said Syme, gesturing by way of explanation.

"Insulted you?" exclaimed the gentleman with the red rosette. "When?"

"Oh, only a moment ago," said Syme rashly. "He insulted my mother."

"Insulted your mother!" the gentleman cried in disbelief.

"Well, at any rate," said Syme, giving ground a little, "my aunt."

"But how can the Marquis have insulted your aunt just now?" asked the second gentleman, with some justified bewilderment. "He has been sitting here the whole time."

"Ah, it was what he said!" Syme answered darkly.

"I said nothing whatever," said the Marquis, "apart from a remark about the band. All I said was that I enjoyed Wagner when it was well played."

"That was a reference to my family," Syme said firmly. "My aunt played Wagner badly. It was a sore subject. We are forever being insulted about it."

"This seems most peculiar," said the decorated gentleman, glancing uncertainly at the Marquis.

"Oh, I promise you," Syme said earnestly, "your entire conversation was simply crammed with sinister hints at my aunt's failings."

"This is absurd!" said the second gentleman. "For my own part, I have said nothing for half an hour except that I liked the singing of that black-haired girl."

"Well, there you go again!" cried Syme indignantly. "My aunt's hair was red."

"It seems to me," said the other, "that you are merely hunting for an excuse to insult the Marquis."

"By George!" said Syme, turning round to look at him, "what a clever fellow you are!"

The Marquis sprang up, his eyes blazing like a tiger's.

"Looking for a quarrel with me!" he cried. "Looking for a fight with me! By God! no man ever had to look for long. Perhaps these gentlemen will act for me. There are four hours of daylight left. Let us fight this evening."

Syme bowed with truly lovely courtesy.

"Marquis," he said, "your conduct is worthy of your reputation and your lineage. Allow me a moment to confer with the gentlemen into whose hands I shall put myself."

In three long strides he was back among his companions, and they, having watched his champagne-fuelled assault and heard his imbecile explanations, were quite taken aback by his appearance. For now that he had returned to them he was entirely sober, a little pale, and he spoke in a low voice full of intense practicality.

"I have done it," he said huskily. "I have forced a fight on the brute. But look here, and listen closely. There is no time for talking. You are my seconds, and everything must come from you. Now you must insist, and insist without compromise, that the duel take place after seven tomorrow, so that I have a chance of stopping him from catching the 7.45 to Paris. If he misses that, he misses his crime. He cannot refuse to meet you over so small a matter of time and place. But here is what he will do. He will pick a field somewhere close to a wayside station, where he can board the train. He is an excellent swordsman, and he will count on killing me in time to catch it. But I can fence well too, and I believe I can keep him busy, at least until the train has gone. Then perhaps he will kill me to soothe his feelings. Do you understand? Very well, then, allow me to introduce you to some delightful friends of mine." And, leading them briskly across the promenade, he presented them to the Marquis's seconds under two very aristocratic names that they had never heard before.

Syme was prone to fits of remarkable common sense that did not otherwise belong to his character. They were (as he had said of his impulse about the spectacles) poetic intuitions, and at times they rose to the heights of prophecy. In this instance he had judged his opponent's strategy correctly. When the Marquis learned from his seconds that Syme could fight only in the morning, he must have understood perfectly that a sudden obstacle now stood between him and his bomb-throwing work in the capital. Naturally he could not explain this difficulty to his friends, and so he took the course Syme had foreseen. He persuaded his seconds to fix on a small meadow not far from the railway, and he relied on the first bout proving fatal.

When he came down quite coolly to the field of honour, nobody could have guessed that a journey was weighing on his mind; his hands were in his pockets, his straw hat was pushed to the back of his head, and his handsome face was bold and brazen in the sunshine. Yet a stranger might have found it curious that his retinue included not only his seconds with the sword-case but also two of his servants carrying a portmanteau and a luncheon basket.

Early though it was, the sun steeped everything in warmth, and Syme was dimly surprised to see so many spring flowers glowing gold and silver in the tall grass, which reached nearly to the knees of the whole party. Apart from the Marquis, every man there wore dark and formal morning-dress, with hats like black chimney-pots; the little Doctor in particular, with his black spectacles added, resembled an undertaker in a farce. Syme could not help sensing a comic contrast between this funereal Sunday-best attire and the lush, glistening meadow, with wild flowers springing up everywhere. Yet in truth this comic contrast between the yellow blooms and the black hats was only a symbol of the tragic contrast between the yellow blooms and the black business at hand. To his right was a small wood; far off to his left ran the long curve of the railway line, which he was, in a manner of speaking, guarding against the Marquis, for whom it was both destination and escape. Ahead of him, beyond the dark knot of his opponents, he could see, like a coloured cloud, a little almond bush in blossom against the dim line of the sea.

The member of the Legion of Honour, whose name, it appeared, was Colonel Ducroix, came up to the Professor and Dr. Bull with great courtesy and proposed that the contest should end at the first serious wound. Dr. Bull, however, who had been carefully drilled by Syme on this very point, insisted with great dignity and in very poor French that it should go on until one of the fighters was disabled. Syme had decided that he could avoid disabling the Marquis, and keep the Marquis from disabling him, for at least twenty minutes. In twenty minutes the Paris train would have passed.

"For a man of Monsieur de St. Eustache's renowned skill and courage," the Professor said solemnly, "it can make no difference which method is chosen, and our principal has weighty reasons for requiring the longer engagement, reasons so delicate that I am prevented from stating them plainly, but for whose just and honourable character I can—"

"Peste!" burst from the Marquis behind him, whose face had suddenly clouded over. "Let us stop talking and begin." And he lopped the head off a tall flower with his stick.

Syme understood his rough impatience and instinctively glanced over his shoulder to see whether the train was in sight. But there was no smoke on the horizon. Colonel Ducroix knelt and unlocked the case, drawing out a matched pair of swords, which caught the sunlight and became two streaks of white fire. He held one out to the Marquis, who seized it without ceremony, and another to Syme, who took it, flexed it, and balanced it with as much delay as dignity allowed. Then the Colonel brought out a second pair of blades, kept one for himself and handed the other to Dr. Bull, and set about positioning the men.
