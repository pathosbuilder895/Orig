The cart moved at a fair pace, and before long they felt the thrilling sense of having left far behind whoever it was that was chasing them. The puzzle of where the anarchists had found so many supporters, after all, remained unanswered. A single man had been enough to put them to flight; one glimpse of the Secretary's twisted smile had sent them running. Now and again Syme turned to glance back at the host following in their wake. As the wood thinned out and then shrank into the distance, he could make out the sunny slopes rising past and above it, and over those slopes the dense black crowd was still advancing, like a single enormous beetle. The sun was very bright and Syme's eyes were very keen, nearly as good as a telescope, so he could see this body of men with perfect clearness. He could pick them out as individual people, yet what struck him more and more was the way they moved together as if they were one man. They appeared to wear dark suits and ordinary hats, like any crowd off the streets; but they did not straggle and scatter and stream toward their goal along different paths, as a normal mob would do. They advanced with a kind of horrible, evil stiffness, like a glassy-eyed army of mechanical men. Syme drew Ratcliffe's attention to it.

"Yes," the policeman answered, "that is discipline. That is Sunday. He may be five hundred miles away, but every one of them feels the dread of him, like the finger of God. Yes, they are marching in step; and you may be sure they are speaking in step, too, and thinking in step. But the only thing that matters to us is that they are vanishing in step."

Syme nodded. It was quite true that the dark smudge of their pursuers was dwindling steadily as the peasant thrashed his horse onward. The sunny country, level enough taken all together, dropped away past the far edge of the wood in great heavy swells of hillside running down to the sea, rather like the lower reaches of the Sussex downs. The one difference was that in Sussex the road would have wound and zigzagged like a small stream, whereas here the white French road plunged straight down ahead of them like a waterfall. Down this steep, direct slope the cart rattled at a sharp tilt, and within a few minutes, as the road grew steeper still, they saw beneath them the small harbour of Lancy and a vast blue curve of sea. The moving cloud of their foes had vanished entirely from the skyline.

The horse and cart swung sharply around a group of elm trees, and the horse's nose came within an inch of hitting an old gentleman seated on the benches in front of the small café called "Le Soleil d'Or." The peasant muttered an apology and climbed down from his perch. The rest dismounted after him, one at a time, and addressed the old gentleman with scraps of polite conversation, since it was plain from his generous bearing that the little inn belonged to him. He was a white-haired old fellow with cheeks like apples, drowsy eyes, and a grey moustache; plump, fond of sitting, and thoroughly guileless, of a sort often met with in France but more common still in the Catholic parts of Germany. All his belongings, his pipe, his mug of beer, his flowers, and his beehive, spoke of a peace handed down through generations; only, as his guests glanced upward on entering the parlour of the inn, they noticed the sword hanging on the wall.

The Colonel, who hailed the innkeeper as an old acquaintance, went quickly through into the parlour and took a seat, calling for some customary refreshment. The soldierly briskness of his manner caught Syme's interest, and, sitting beside him, he seized the chance to satisfy his curiosity once the old innkeeper had stepped out.

"Might I ask, Colonel," he said quietly, "what has brought us here?"

Colonel Ducroix smiled under his stiff white moustache.

"There are two reasons, sir," he replied, "and I shall give you first not the weightier but the more practical one. We have come here because there is nowhere else within twenty miles where we can obtain horses."

"Horses!" Syme echoed, glancing up sharply.

"Yes," the other answered; "if you people truly mean to outrun your enemies, it must be horses or nothing, unless, of course, you happen to carry bicycles and motor-cars in your pockets."

"And where would you advise us to head?" Syme asked uncertainly.

"Without a doubt," the Colonel answered, "you should hurry as fast as you can to the police station on the far side of the town. My friend, for whom I acted as second under rather misleading conditions, appears to me to overstate greatly the chances of a general uprising; but even he would scarcely claim, I imagine, that you would not be safe among the gendarmes."

Syme nodded seriously; then he asked suddenly, "And your second reason for coming here?"

"My second reason for coming here," Ducroix said gravely, "is that it does no harm to look upon a good man or two when one may be close to death."

Syme raised his eyes to the wall and saw a roughly painted and touching religious picture. Then he said, "You are right," and almost at once afterward, "Has anyone seen to the horses?"

"Yes," Ducroix answered, "you may be entirely sure I gave the order the instant I arrived. Those enemies of yours did not seem to be in any hurry, but in fact they were covering ground amazingly quickly, like a well-drilled army. I never imagined the anarchists possessed so much discipline. You have not a moment to lose."

Nearly as he said it, the old innkeeper with the white hair and blue eyes came shuffling into the room and reported that six horses stood saddled outside. On Ducroix's advice the five others provided themselves with some food and wine that could be carried, and, holding on to their duelling swords as the only weapons they had, they clattered off down the steep white road. The two servants who had borne the Marquis's baggage back when he was a marquis were, by general agreement, left behind to drink at the café, and this was by no means contrary to their own wishes.

By now the afternoon sun was sinking toward the west, and in its light Syme could see the solid form of the old innkeeper shrinking smaller and smaller, yet still standing there and watching them go in complete silence, the sunlight in his silvery hair. A fixed and superstitious notion had lodged in Syme's mind, planted by the Colonel's chance remark, that this might really be the last honest stranger he would ever lay eyes on in this world. He was still gazing at that diminishing figure, which showed as no more than a grey speck lit with a white flame against the vast green wall of the steep down behind it. And as he gazed over the crest of the down beyond the innkeeper, an army of marching men dressed in black came into view. They seemed to hover over the good man and his house like a dark swarm of locusts. The horses had been saddled not a moment too soon.

CHAPTER XII. THE EARTH IN ANARCHY

Spurring their horses into a gallop, with no regard for how rough the road's descent was, the riders quickly won back their lead over the men on foot, and at length the mass of the first houses of Lancy blocked their pursuers from view. Still, it had been a long ride, and by the time they reached the town proper the western sky was glowing with the hue and character of sunset. The Colonel proposed that, before heading at last for the police station, they should try on the way to enlist one more person who might be of use.

"Four of the five wealthy men in this town," he said, "are ordinary crooks. I imagine the ratio is about the same the world over. The fifth is a friend of mine and a thoroughly splendid fellow; and, what matters even more from our standpoint, he has a motor-car."

"I fear," said the Professor in his merry fashion, glancing back down the white road where the black, creeping smudge might show itself at any instant, "I fear we can scarcely spare the time for afternoon visits."

"Doctor Renard's house is just three minutes away," said the Colonel.

"Our danger," said Dr. Bull, "is less than two minutes away."

"Yes," said Syme, "if we ride hard we are bound to outdistance them, for they are on foot."

"He has a motor-car," said the Colonel.

"But we may not be able to get it," said Bull.

"Yes, he is entirely on your side."

"But he may not be at home."

"Be quiet," Syme said abruptly. "What is that sound?"

For a moment they all sat as motionless as statues on horseback, and for a moment—for two or three or four moments—heaven and earth seemed just as motionless. Then all of them, straining their ears to the utmost, caught along the road that thrill and throbbing beyond description which can mean only one thing—horses!

The Colonel's face altered in an instant, as though lightning had struck it and yet left it unharmed.

"They have outwitted us," he said, with curt soldierly irony. "Stand ready for cavalry!"

"Where can they have found horses?" Syme asked, as he spurred his mount to a canter without thinking.

The Colonel said nothing for a while; then he spoke in a tight voice. "I was being perfectly precise when I told you the 'Soleil d'Or' was the only place within twenty miles where horses can be had."

"No!" Syme cried fiercely. "I don't believe he would do it. Not with hair as white as that."

"He may have been compelled," the Colonel said gently. "They must number at least a hundred, and that is why we are all going to call on my friend Renard, who has a motor-car."

So saying, he wheeled his horse abruptly around a corner and tore down the street at such a thundering speed that the rest, although already riding at a full gallop, struggled to keep up with the streaming tail of his horse.

Dr. Renard lived in a tall, comfortable house at the head of a steep street, so that when the riders dismounted at his door they could once again see the firm green ridge of the hill, with the white road running across it, rising above all the rooftops of the town. They breathed more easily on seeing that the road was still empty, and they rang the bell.

Dr. Renard was a cheerful man with a brown beard, a fine specimen of that quiet but extremely industrious professional class which France has kept intact even more completely than England has. When the situation was laid before him, he dismissed the ex-Marquis's alarm out of hand; he declared, with sturdy French scepticism, that a general rising of anarchists was not remotely likely.

"Anarchy," he said with a shrug, "it is mere childishness!"

"Et ça," the Colonel exclaimed all at once, pointing past the other man's shoulder, "and that is childishness too, is it not?"

They all turned and beheld a crescent of black horsemen come sweeping over the brow of the hill with all the force of Attila. Fast as they rode, though, the entire rank still held closely together, and they could make out the black masks of the leading line as even as a row of uniforms. Yet although the main dark square was unchanged, apart from moving faster, there was now one startling difference which they could see plainly on the hillside, as if on a tilted map. Most of the horsemen rode in a single body; but one rider raced far in front of the column and, with wild motions of hand and heel, drove his horse on faster and faster, so that one might have imagined he was not the hunter but the hunted. Even at that great distance, though, they could see something so fanatical, so unmistakable in his shape, that they knew he was the Secretary himself.

"I regret having to interrupt a cultivated conversation," said the Colonel, "but could you lend me your motor-car now, within two minutes?"

"I rather suspect that you are all out of your minds," said Dr. Renard with a friendly smile; "but heaven forbid that madness should ever get in the way of friendship. Let us go round to the garage."

Dr. Renard was a gentle man of enormous wealth; his rooms resembled the Musée de Cluny, and he owned three motor-cars. He appeared to use them very seldom, however, having the plain tastes of the French middle class, and when his impatient friends set about inspecting them, it took a while to satisfy themselves that even one of them could be made to run. With some trouble they got this one round into the street in front of the Doctor's house. When they emerged from the gloomy garage they were surprised to discover that dusk had already come down as suddenly as night falls in the tropics. Either they had spent longer in there than they supposed, or some strange covering of cloud had collected over the town. They gazed down the steep streets and thought they could see a faint mist rising from the sea.

"It is now or never," said Dr. Bull. "I hear horses."

"No," the Professor corrected him, "a horse."

And as they listened, it became clear that the sound, drawing swiftly nearer over the clattering cobbles, was not that of the whole troop but of the single rider who had left it far behind—the mad Secretary.

Syme's family, like most of those who finish up in the simple life, had once had a motor, and he understood everything about them. He had sprung straight into the driver's seat and, red in the face, was wrenching and heaving at the neglected machinery. He threw all his strength onto one lever, and then said very calmly, "I'm afraid it won't work."

Even as he said it, a man came sweeping round the corner, stiff upon his galloping horse, with the speed and stiffness of an arrow. He wore a smile that pushed his chin forward as though it were out of joint. He came rushing up alongside the motionless car, into which its party had packed themselves, and placed his hand upon the front of it. It was the Secretary, and his mouth set perfectly straight in the gravity of victory.

Syme was bearing down hard on the steering wheel, and there was no sound except the rumble of the other pursuers riding into the town. Then, all at once, came a shriek of grinding iron, and the car sprang forward. It snatched the Secretary clean from his saddle, as a knife is jerked from its sheath, dragged him kicking horribly for twenty yards, and left him thrown flat on the road well ahead of his terrified horse.
