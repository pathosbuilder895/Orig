I The flattening

Every paper now reads like
every other paper.

The use of AI will quietly do what is already happening: shaving off the unique parts of every writer. It gives every student a perfect score in grammar, at the cost of lifeless prose.

The variance that once distinguished a freshman from a finalist (the loose comma, the unjustified but, the sentence that forgets its own beginning) has been quietly ironed out.

AI detectors ask the wrong question: they guess what wrote this, a person or a machine. The more useful question, and the harder one, is whether this piece sounds like this student.

Original builds a baseline from writing the teacher has approved as the student's own. When a new piece reads differently, it shows the teacher what moved, so the next step is a conversation, never a verdict.

Do we walk into ruin?

We walk into the ruin of writing not through copying someone else, but through a lack of true inspiration, a conformance to nothing but the next AI suggestion. Over time it is no longer your writing: not something you could have written, but what everyone has written.

Death is not detected by a grammar check, because grammar is the one area to survive. It is rather their hand, their style, fading into nothingness; not just one, but all future generations giving papers that no one can tell apart.

Only if no one can say whose words are whose. That is the part Original refuses to lose.
II The premise

Every writer leaves a fingerprint.

Style doesn't live as decoration, but as the structural, underlying habits of the writer: the cadence, the semicolon instead of the em dash, the habitual style people reach for, growing inside them since they first composed a sentence.

Because these habits are stable over time and run through every paragraph, they can be measured: Original uses ninety-seven of them.

Together they sketch how one person tends to write. A sketch, not proof: people learn, change genre and revise.

III The method
97features

Seven families of quiet evidence.

Original defines 109 stylometric features across eighteen tiers, from the surface (sentence length, vocabulary range) to the idiosyncratic (how often you reach for a dash). Ninety-seven are used. The other twelve are switched off: six keystroke-timing features, because Original collects no keystroke data, and six uniformity measures that have not passed validation.

III·b The bluebook

A fingerprint is only as honest
as the hand that gave it.

Every comparison Original makes rests on one question: what does this student's writing actually look like? The answer has to come from writing the teacher trusts was the student's own, such as work written in class.

Bluebook is a locked, in-browser writing window. The teacher opens a timed session, students write, and each piece is sealed when they hand it in. Sealing adds nothing to a profile on its own: only work the teacher approves becomes reference writing, and an approval can be undone.

The more approved writing a student has, the steadier the comparison. With only a few pieces it is not reliable, and in testing so far it has raised too many false alarms on small baselines; that is why Original is off by default and still in testing. Zero student setup: they just write, the way they always have.

IV The tension arc

What a real writer
cannot help building.

κ

Beneath the surface features, Original measures one deeper signal borrowed from catastrophe theory: the tension arc, κ = σ(ρ) · (1 − μ(ρ)). It describes the unresolved tension that runs across an argument: the writer's instinct to not yet say it, to build, defer, dwell, and only then resolve. It is one feature among ninety-seven, and not a test for machine writing.

V The baseline

Their own pages.
Their own voice.

Original does not ask whether writing is different from a norm. It asks whether it is different from the writer's own.

The teacher approves pieces of the student's own writing, five or more to start. From these, Original builds the student's baseline: the usual value and the usual spread of each feature.

Every later piece is compared with that baseline, so each student is measured only against themselves, and an unusual style is not by itself a reason for concern. A piece can still read differently for good reasons: the student learned something, changed genre, revised heavily, used permitted help or needed an accommodation. A difference starts a conversation; it never ends one.
